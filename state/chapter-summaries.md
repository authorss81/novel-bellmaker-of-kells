# Chapter Summaries

## Chapter 0001 — The Bell Without a Clapper

Tomas receives a waist-high fixed Crown survey carrier at the Vale Bellhouse before first light and discovers that its survey clapper is missing. The carrier rings without being struck; Tomas measures the warp twice and distrusts the agreement because his hand was steady twelve years ago while something that should have been loud went quiet. He clears his own flood bell out of the casting room to make space, soaks a paid job to keep the carrier documented, and sends the customer a delay notice. He documents a child-sized wax handprint, a near-cut coupling with a thin jumper, and a note attributed to Mara saying not to call it an answer. Surveyor Pever certifies the damage, says the order came out of the survey store rather than from him, and leaves. Tomas locks the carrier in the casting room, preserves the marks, and leaves with a route fragment and a warning that outlasted anything the cut coupling could account for.

## Chapter 0002 — The West Road Warning

Tomas follows the old route to the Low Ward and asks witnesses separately what they heard, recording a low sound, a pulse under the stones, and a child's fragment about children and dawn. Halden orders him to keep the carrier away from the public and shows him the L-4 notice, giving the route number and the dawn but refusing the filing date, so Tomas leaves the landing office without knowing whether the order predates the bell. Tomas gives an ordinary spoken warning, names what he knows and does not know, and receives a courier note about Tamsin Rook's missing handbell.

## Chapter 0003 — The Stolen Handbell

From her own point of view, Tamsin investigates the theft of her palm-sized courier handbell from the north-gate lockbox. She traces a Crown cord and green wool, refuses a house-by-house search, learns that her hearing was damaged by forced resonance, and follows a square-key clue to the cooper's loft. She recovers the altered handbell and keeps it. Oren arranges a watch-supervised comparison that **has not happened yet**; what she does allow on the page is unwrapping the bell once on the top step of the Bellhouse stair, in full view of the lane, so Tomas can lean over it from the doorway and read the widened wear without touching it — a private, unwatched exchange. The record contains a J. Vale key sign-out that may implicate Tomas or be a convenient lie.

## Chapter 0004 — What a Bell Can Carry

Tomas asks Sabin Fell to explain the old craft rules instead of pretending the warning is self-explanatory, and costs Sabin a customer: a woman with a fish-scale clapper walks out over Tomas taking the bench, and warns she will send the miller elsewhere. Sabin defines witness matter, a keeper's specific duty, and an informed answer, while withholding Hearthline history and naming no route failure beyond relay distortion. He then demonstrates the refusal test on himself, refuses to carry the warning to the wool market, and makes Tomas write the refusal down verbatim instead of improving it. He alludes to having once carried something past the gate that reached one house and not the next, then declines to say what it was, so his Hush guilt is hinted but still unnamed. He refuses to bind the carrier or place his name under Tomas's claim. Nell Fenn arrives with a traced child handprint and asks for a bell to bring her missing father home, saying her father went to answer a Crown summons.

## Chapter 0005 — The Child Who Was Not Named

Tomas visits Nell's Low Ward room and matches the traced handprint to her eight-year-old brother Jory, not her father Edan. Jory and Edan answered the previous Low Ward renewal round, six and five days before Nell speaks, not the L-4 posting; the board posts each round a week ahead, and a lodger notes the round is not special to their street. The landlady reports a woman in the room above who went looking for her son and never returned, and Tomas records the address but not the name, since nobody with a right to it had asked. The household fragment is incomplete, its date beneath the seal reads three days before the carrier rang, and the record treats Nell as present while marking Jory as not returned. Tomas refuses to promise a bell can find her father, tells Nell he will ask her mother before questioning her, and earns her qualified trust by asking what she wants to know first.

## Chapter 0006 — The Order Before the Bell

At market court, Tomas makes the clerk write the L-4 notice's filing date, route, and seal on a public board, accepting that his investigation will be visible. The order was filed three days before the carrier rang and permits custody of unregistered minors, but Tomas cannot yet identify who signed, copied, or accepted it. He traces a physical splice near the court, then learns from Oren that the inspection came through a watch route and that the local surveyor's service entry has disappeared.

## Chapter 0007 — A Warning Without an Order

Under a no-public-ringing order nailed to the Bellhouse door by Oren himself, Tomas casts a new trial handbell from ordinary stock. A first blank cracks and is remelted, and a correction splits his old tuning file. Using a sliver of the market-court L-4 posting as witness matter, wedged into the bell's frame so anyone can remove and read it, Tomas accepts only a narrow duty to warn about the possible inspection, and Nell gives one informed answer limited to carrying the words to one neighboring household. After two private notes, Tomas senses only that Nell's answer is strained and suffers ringing ears; the north-gate bell repeats the two-note pattern that evening.

## Chapter 0008 — The Watchman’s Account

Oren records Tomas's account of the Hush rope cut, including the fact that the cut saved one child but ended the warning before it reached Ewan. He seals the fixed carrier in its existing frame without moving it, includes the route fragment in the receipt, and orders a no-public-ringing condition for the trial bell. Oren's grief remains active, but he treats Tomas as a person who must publish evidence and accept custody rather than as an innocent or a permanent enemy.

## Chapter 0009 — The Missing Surveyor

Tomas finds the survey store partly cleared, follows the physical L-4 line, and discovers a service ticket saying the carrier's clapper was removed and the carrier disconnected after a warning was filed; no keeper answer is recorded. He feels the two-note initiating vibration travel through a narrow bypass before the north-gate bell repeats it, links a J. V. tag to the earlier J. Vale register entry without naming a suspect or choosing a conclusion, and records the route. Tamsin retains her recovered handbell, carries the route number as a record rather than a signal, and Halden sets a midnight deadline and says the commission's paper is coming.

## Chapter 0010 — The Answer Is Not Consent

Halden's temporary writ, delivered inside the deadline he set at the gate, names the fixed carrier, Tomas's trial handbell, and the loose relay bells. Tomas refuses to ring the private bell as a town command or order families to move; Nell, her mother, Tamsin, and Oren each state only what they personally choose to do. Oren invokes the writ's narrow pending-hearing clause, and Halden signs a seven-day joint receipt placing only the fixed carrier in neutral watch custody while it remains in its mounting frame. The trial and working bells stay at the Bellhouse under a no-public-ringing order; Tomas loses paid work, stands in the empty dust-outline where the frame stood, and accepts a three-answer public test before the rescheduled dawn.

## Chapter 0011 — The First Refusal

Day 3, the first full day of the stay, Tomas loses a customer's bell to his own bench: a wheelwright's counter bell hung a quarter inch long, whose note beat against itself, cannot be dressed while the bound trial handbell stands four feet away under its cloth and answers every strike, and he will not move a bell named in a Crown order in front of a guard with a book, so it goes back into the cart untuned and is owed back when the stay is over. Then he finds he has three blank witness slips and no lawful bell. He rules out the bound trial handbell, the loose relay bells, and the north-gate watch bell, and leaves a fourth candidate he does not want to write: the market court's unbound iron call bell, which the court clerk will not lend for words nobody in the room has accepted. Sabin teaches the rule that governs the test — write the claim once, read the same words to each person, write what they actually said, never treat two matching answers as a stronger one than a third that disagrees. Oren sets out the four-part frame: physical witness, keeper's duty, three independent answers, and a carrier that may not add anything. Tamsin warns that a public tone is larger than the claim under it and points out that a version of Tomas's warning ending in *before dawn* is already circulating at the landing. Five witnesses come to the market court. **Bevin Sarr refuses**, because a slip with his name on it would be filed in the same office as the ward list that holds his unpaid entry; Oren records the refusal in his own words, unsigned at his request, and Tomas does not call it sabotage. The witnesses who remain insist the test must ask a real question about the water, not only about the Crown order, and the clerk will answer for the bell only when a keeper of standing accepts a duty for it.

## Chapter 0012 — A Promise Before the Rain

Day 4, morning, in rising rain, Tamsin meets **Hollis Renn** at his low flood gate under his written duty, which he reads off an oilskin board: the keeper is the man who has read the mark with his own hand in the hour before, the words said at the gate are the words the keeper himself can support, and nothing is added in the ward or above the landing. She rings her handbell once at the gate with a boy listening on the landing stage, a stone's throw above the low drain gate that is the last thing the route touches, and the boy hears it — which fixes the authorized boundary and proves nothing about the reach, a fact she states she has known since March. The wax laid over the older groove is what makes a distance nobody signed for. She pays for the ring in ringing ears and a nosebleed, and sets the rule that a duty governs words while metal governs distance. Adults, including Foreman Crane, want the bell used to close the low lane; she refuses and offers to knock door to door instead. Tomas arrives with a rod and no finished claim. **Wyman Dace**, the bridge keeper, accepts a promise written on slate — to walk the west road and report before evening what he finds, and never to say the bridge is safe — because a man who says a bridge is safe is asking the river to prove something it has not agreed to. Renn accepts only his own duty, refuses to carry a road claim, and will not ring until the gate has been walked and the windlass has turned twice under load. The water stands a finger's width above the second mark and climbing, and when Tamsin leaves, the gate is still stuck: the pawl slips and a barrow-load of gravel is packed behind the sill. A fish seller says the gate has never come up in a rising water in forty years. A boy reports that the low lane is under and a Crown road gang's cart is across the culvert with nobody coming.

## Chapter 0013 — The Low Ward Does Not Answer

Day 4, midday into afternoon, after Tamsin's morning, Nell asks her mother first and is refused permission to answer for the household, then writes her own question, pins it to the ward board, and reads the identical words to three adults. **Foreman Crane** accepts a duty to carry the words to the landing and stop there. **Wenna Cray** asks for time until she can find the round paper for the woman who went looking for her sister. **Garn Fisk** refuses the route and refuses to be named. A Crown officer asks who called the meeting; Jerm records the answers in the watch book instead. Nell keeps the household fragment, decides to have it copied in daylight at a board rather than hidden, and publishes only that three different choices were made. Tomas wants to write the refusal as fear; Nell refuses, and he accepts her record. He tells her he will not hand her a question to ask. The Bellwrights' Measure's notice arrives: the private binding and route work are held to be a hazard to public order, and the trial handbell must be submitted for guild inspection or the workshop's licence is challenged.

## Chapter 0014 — The Bellwright's Condition

Day 5, late afternoon, Tomas goes to Sabin, then to the Measure's temporary room above the fish market, where **Iven Sore** — the living master, and the name on the withdrawn docket — inspects the trial handbell without sounding it, because the no-ringing order is in force, and files that limitation himself. He lifts the L-4 posting sliver out of the bell's frame with two fingers and calls the wedging correct, because a paper any person can remove can be removed by anyone. He unrolls the Measure's road map and shows a dated, keyed mark for each of the fifty-three wards that have taken their answers back since before the Crown laid a mile of copper — a number with nothing to do with the forty-one households that will appear on the Crown's L-4 answer schedule in Chapter 20 — then says plainly that a map shows where a line runs and a hand does the altering. Asked whether he withdrew the L-4 service entry, he answers that he can support never holding a Crown commission, cannot support the rest, and will not say he did not sign it, because a denial made in a recorded room is a sentence that can be used in either direction. He offers a certified record, a copy of the map, and a seat in the room where a route is built, in exchange for a master key and a licence making the Bellhouse answerable for every route answer; Tomas refuses both. The Measure issues a **conditional challenge** — the material inspection plus a published withdrawal procedure lodged at the market court — and not a ban. Sabin refuses to give the guild an opinion, which is what keeps his name off their record. Oren's Crown officer blocks the bell's trip at the court arch; Jerm reads the order and lets it pass, and the officer logs a complaint.

## Chapter 0015 — The Sealed Workshop

Day 6, morning, Halden brings an inventory and sealing order. Oren separates four object classes aloud in the yard, seals only the eight connected working bells, puts his own watch mark on the same wax so it takes two hands to remove, records the rest, and enters that nothing in the inventory moves ownership or makes a private answer into a public one. Tomas sets the bound trial handbell out in public himself and shows that the posting sliver lifts out with two fingers. The **watch-supervised reading of the courier bell's wear is performed here**: Tamsin unwraps her handbell on the cloth, Tomas reads the wax over the older groove from a distance without touching it, and the record states that the widening is a fact about metal and not evidence the ward agreed to anything, with no custody changed and the route neither disclosed nor required. Oren also states in the same breath that the comparison he arranged in Chapter 3 — the courier bell against the fixed carrier — **has never been made and cannot be made in that yard while the carrier sits in the watch store under the joint receipt**, and the record carries it as outstanding. The miller takes his daughter's feast bell to a Miremarket maker; the chandler's work goes when Nell declines. Tomas nearly hides the trial bell in the casting-room cellar stair and does not, because Tamsin tells him a manufactured route cannot be repaired by anybody including its maker. Oren requires a public account of the Hush rope cut before the town will answer him.

## Chapter 0016 — The Rope in the Market Court

Day 6, late day, in the rain, Oren holds a public account rather than a trial and says so. Halden objects in the record. Tomas says he cut the frame's lifting line to reach a child trapped under the counterweight, that the line was already carrying a warning, and that the cut is the reason the warning ended before it reached the ward. He states that he cannot prove what the tone would have done had the line held. **Wen Anner**, the child he saved, refuses to be used as absolution or as condemnation and has it entered that he is angry at both. A Low Ward witness enters that a thin sound followed the frame's fall and that people moved because of what they saw, which corrects Oren's private belief without clearing Tomas. Oren enters three conditions on any public signal — name known, unknown and next keeper inside the words; any household or keeper may withdraw with no reason, fee or consequence, recorded as a withdrawal and never as silence; and such a signal is not a municipal act. The crowd is not satisfied and the record survives. **Gib Hask** demands the Crown's buried line be pulled out of the ward, and Oren records both positions as unreconcilable. A message from the low gate says the west road has gone soft and the sill is climbing.

## Chapter 0017 — What the Water Owes

Day 7, all day, the water crests. Tomas measures an undercut at the north end of the west road's low section, finds the Crown road gang's cart across the culvert with the foreman's signature on it, and issues one bounded claim naming the second mark, the cart, the undercut and every sentence about what he cannot tell anybody. **Wyman Dace** accepts and reports with two additions of his own; **Wenna Cray** accepts only with the Crown's cart stated in her sentence, only as far as the ward board, and with the cost to herself named in advance; **Hollis Renn** refuses to carry anything of anybody's. The gravel behind the sill was cleared on Day 6, but the ring still comes late: Renn reads the third mark above by a finger at the sixth hour, brings the gate up, and four houses at the foot of the ward take water to table height; the chandler loses his flour. This is the batch's only crest; the water falls from here. Tomas's cost is a loss of balance in the crowded lane and renewed ringing ears. Bevin Sarr makes a Crown officer say in front of eleven witnesses that a works order held the cart. Four households moved goods, two waited and lost a day, six stalls lost the market's trade, and a household's pig drowned. The chapter ends with three answers that agree with nothing and the question of how a bell carries three positions without merging them.

## Chapter 0018 — Three Answers, One Danger

Day 8, the water falling, Tomas lodges the withdrawal procedure at the market court, satisfying the Measure's second condition. The market court usher brings the unbound iron call bell and states she is its keeper and will count every answer including the ones she dislikes. Halden objects in the record and then gives his best argument: he is not there to stop the assembly, he is there because if a child drowns the Crown will send a clerk to ask whether the ward was warned, and he wants that answer on paper. **Sena Dunn withdraws in open court** because two Crown officers are present and her name is already on two rolls; Tomas begins to build her leaving into an answer he could use and Nell stops him — *she is not a silence you get to fill*. Before the court sits Tomas puts the four agreements on the page, one name per sheet — **Cray, Dunn, Dace and Renn** — having asked each of them alone over three days, and watches two of them change in person while a third sends word: **Wenna Cray withdraws to the ward board**, **Hollis Renn sends four words — *I keep a gate, not a court* — from his own gate and withdraws**, and **Foreman Crane and Bevin Sarr substitute**, with Crane using Tomas's own procedure, lodged that same morning, to withdraw from two doors on his row with no reason given. The claim is read twice in identical words, and three answers are given: Crane accepts with that boundary, Dace asks for time until he has walked the road again, and Sarr refuses the route and agrees to the refusal being read aloud unsigned. Dace returns before the court closes and his answer is entered as an **amendment in his own column**, not a fourth answer. The usher counts one, two, three on the iron, including the refusal, and refuses to count the amendment again. When a man shouts that the Crown is calling the ward, the usher refuses Tomas a fourth stroke and Oren answers in words. **Surveyor Rhea Collis** arrives off the north road, states she is not the officer who certified the carrier and never held the L-4 entry, and her line on the roll bears no withdrawal. By evening the ward's version has collapsed to two answers both agreeing, and a Crown clerk arrives with an order treating the three-answer record as a **single local response** on L-4 and demanding the slips.

## Chapter 0019 — The Cost of a Public Tone

Day 9, morning, Tamsin is worn down by the counted strokes and cannot follow speech in a crowded room. A woman tells her a bell said everybody must leave; the version did the damage, and Tamsin is more useful in thirty seconds of truth than in a whole afternoon of assembly. At the watch house, the Crown's filing clerk wants the three answers as one packet; Jerm reports that Oren refused, and that the refusal came out of a conversation in which the clerk admitted he had no authority to say no. Tamsin logs the handbell's real reach with Jerm — seven of eleven between the north gate and the landing heard it, three of five on the ferry road beyond it heard it, and the second cottage past the toll shed heard nothing at all — and writes a rule: the handbell is a repeater, not an author; it repeats only; it rings only with a named person beside it who can say what it means; it rings for the whole of a record or none of it; and it is never rung to fill a withdrawn place or to carry only the convenient parts. Tomas arrives with a plan and an instruction. She stops him, makes him ask, and says yes on her own terms: on foot, once at the gate, no Crown wages, the route ending where the route ends unless a person asks, and a village that will hear it late and a house that will not hear it at all. Tomas admits he has been handing her work and calling it a favour, and writes the admission into the record. At noon Halden posts an **emergency custody hearing for the first hour of light on Day 10**, before the rescheduled dawn, over the whole of the workshop's signal apparatus, on the ground that a workshop which can sound an answer without the ward's agreement is more dangerous than before.

## Chapter 0020 — The Hearing Before the Hearing

Day 9, evening, at the watch house, Oren lays the whole documentary record out in the order it was made and says he did it so that nobody can object to an order that flatters Tomas. The order predates the sound, the line was cut and bypassed afterwards, the service ticket names nobody, and the market court bell is iron on a rope with no line, so the Crown's claim that it is a connected route is a claim rather than a finding. The Crown's filing man asks for a single packet, is refused, and privately admits to Oren that he has merged divided answers into single ones for eleven years of filing because a ward that cannot make up its mind is read as a ward that will not answer. Tomas's beautiful, impossible promise — that Kells will be warned before dawn and no child will be taken by surprise — is read by Nell and Tamsin, torn up, and replaced with the duller sentence Tomas had abandoned on Day 8. Nell puts the original household fragment on the table against her own earlier refusal, the clerk pushes the pen across to her instead of taking her words down himself, and she writes the copy in her own hand and spelling; **Oren** signs beneath it as the copyist, not as the man who made the thing. Oren opens the L-4 file in front of a Crown copyist. Its service ticket now reads *answered in office, L-4 ward roll, per local practice*. Its sixth sheet is a schedule headed *ROUTE L-4 — RESPONSES ENTERED BEFORE SERVICE*: forty-one households, one mark each, an office number and no signature, dated the day each round was **posted**. Line twenty-two is the **Fenn household, six persons**, with two marked *accompanying* and no names, where the ward fragment says three with a fourth line cut away. Tomas tries to name the two accompanying and the clerk asks which two; he answers that the page does not say and that supplying them would hand the Crown two people to go and find, and the clerk enters *two persons marked accompanying, unidentified on the page*. The clerk enters the count, the office number and the date and refuses the conclusion; Tomas enters the same and refuses to draw the line for the town. Oren tells Tomas he will not stop the seizure of the trial bell. The batch ends with the dawn still ahead, the custody notice on the board, and the Vale Bellhouse open under three stacked marks on its door.

## Chapter 0021 — The First Hour of Light

Day 10, **sitting from half past two in the morning** and over by twenty-five minutes to four, the round of light being the Crown's phrase on the posted notice rather than an hour on the page, at the market court with about a hundred and forty people under the tiles. Deputy Crake has the emergency custody order flat on the clerk's table and waits twenty minutes before speaking. Oren enters two objections first: the market court call bell is unbound iron on a rope with no wire and no crown groove (Jerm's Day 8 inspection record), and the custody order's phrase *thereafter as the commission shall direct* covers the millrace bridge bell, which Oren objects to on the record with the Day 7 flooding as his reason. The Crown's filing clerk produces a **merged single-response page**: all three Day 8 answers in one column under the heading *RESPONSE RECEIVED ON ROUTE L-4, SINGLE, FROM THE LOCAL WARD*, with the withdrawal column, Foreman Crane's two withdrawn doors, and the word *amended* against Wyman Dace's late entry all gone. Oren has Ferrow read the ward record's heading and the merged page's heading back to back in open court. Oren then concedes the Crown's claim to the trial handbell and the eighteen objects on the shelves, on the explicit ground that he does not have the votes and will not put two of his men in a lane in the dark; the seizure is pulled forward to the hearing so that it is done in the open, in the record, and Oren enters that he chose the hour. Tomas does not contest and does not accept the merged page to buy the bell back. Ardle and Cask fetch the handcart and the oiled linen out of the Bellhouse yard at ten past three and take the trial handbell on its own ash frame and the eighteen objects on the shelves down the lane before twenty-five to four. Oren reads the inventory aloud: unworn lip, no tuning marks, the posting sliver wedged in the frame and touching no metal, liftable with two fingers, one wax line in the casting carrying his father's stamp, nothing else in the crown. The sliver is removed and packed separately so any person may read it. The Bellhouse door keeps the no-ringing notice, the Crown wax, and Oren's mark, and nothing of Tomas's but the crookedness. As the bundle goes down the hill a Crown clerk reads out **forty-one households entered and two hundred and six persons entered as answering on route L-4**; Ferrow finds the market court's ward roll for the same round — **thirty-eight households, one hundred and forty-one persons**. Tomas writes both figures and the difference of sixty-five on the back of his hand. Rhea Collis comes off the north road at four to a one-page commission: witness, certify, record.

## Chapter 0022 — What the Dawn Inspection Found

Day 10, dawn into morning, Oren's POV, at the north gate and through the Low Ward. **The Crown produces no per-household authority** and proceeds on the response schedule alone; Collis states at the gate that she will not put her seal to a visit conducted against a roll she has not been shown, and works off the schedule on the record. Jerm writes house numbers, hours, and officer names on the ward slates and refuses to write the Crown's marks; Oren puts his own mark under Jerm's, and the Crown officers write the marks themselves. Line 4 passes; at line 7 a woman disputes the entry and the officer writes *household disputes entry*. **Line 12, house 114, the Teage household** — mark *four entered, three returned, one not returned, entered in office the day the round was posted*; Absalom Teage, carter, produces the slate his household holds; his wife says four words at the door, *He is not on your paper*; a boy of nine, in his shoes, is taken at the fourth hour and forty minutes. Collis requires that line logged as a child removed from a household whose entered response records a return, and countersigns **that line only**, refusing to seal the two others because she cannot certify a child's age. **Line 19, house 121, the Dunn household** — mark *four returned, two accompanying, unnamed on the page*; Sena Dunn asks *which child* four times; a child of eleven is taken at the fifth hour and ten minutes. At line 22, the Fenn house, Mrs Fenn says *there are two of us* and then offers to find the two accompanying persons, and Oren refuses her in front of Nell and enters both the offer and the refusal; a slate is nailed with the house number and hour only, and Oren writes *six entered against a household of two*. **Line 28** is Gib Hask, who asks in the open whether the copper under his house was put in with the ward's consent, and a twenty-six-year-old Crown officer answers in the open that the line is Crown-maintained, was laid before the commission, and exists to carry warnings — so Hask's argument becomes public and useless, and Oren chooses not to write that. **Line 34, house 133** — mark *two returned, one accompanying*; no answer and no name; a child of thirteen taken at the fifth hour and fifty minutes. **Three children, twenty-two marked doors.** Oren enters the removals separately by household with the time and the household's own answer, and adds in his own hand that he had two men, a warrant and seventy-odd people in the lane behind him and did not put them between children and a lawful order, and why. A woman of about forty who is on the tailboard and is on no warrant is entered by Watchman Cask. Collis asks, on the watch house step, to see the schedule her inspection was conducted under; the joint receipt on the Crown file expired at the fourth hour that morning, and Oren has to decide whether to hand a Crown page to a surveyor of his own commission.

## Chapter 0023 — The Receipt

Day 10, afternoon into evening, Tomas. The empty yard, the swept-around rectangle, the clean square on the bench. He enters the day's losses in the day-book, including a line for three children taken under a schedule nobody in the ward had seen. Oren re-makes the no-ringing order **by his own hand**, so it continues until the Crown lifts it in writing or the watch lifts it, and warns that the commission will come back for the millrace bell. The custody receipt describes the object as *signal apparatus, workshop-bound, capable of public sounding*; Tomas cannot change the custody and stops trying, and instead corrects the **description** in the margin in front of a Crown copyist: not capable of public sounding, did not sound, one claim, one keeper's duty of his own, one informed answer given privately and never publicly, not a response of the ward, court, watch or Crown, witness matter packed separately, the article bound and unspent. He then adds the one thing the Crown did not have — **that the answer was given by Nell Fenn** — without asking her, and the copyist initials the margin as not being agreement. He goes down the hill and tells her, and she tells him he put it in writing before he asked, and makes him state the cost in the order *three children, then my name*, and tells him he has never once asked her for anything that was not already written. **Sabin Fell** tells him the trade will call the taking a judgment on the Bellwrights' name within a fortnight, that four houses in the Measure will say a man who loses a bell and a child in a morning should not hold a licence, and that one of them is Iven Sore and he will be right. A Crown clerk empties four shelves of the survey store of L-4 working papers in twenty minutes. The chandler's note takes his work to Miremarket; the miller's note says the mark on the rim has not come off. Tomas enters the two figures of answering persons and the sixty-five that differ as **a question he cannot yet answer: whose office is counting**. At the ninth hour the ward board has a copy of the schedule pinned up overnight without asking anybody, with *206* written under it in charcoal and *Nobody in this ward was asked* underneath.

## Chapter 0024 — Two Men on the Gate

Day 11, morning, Oren's POV, at the watch house and the north gate. Ardle and Cask want to be told they were right. Cask's account: he barred the gate for the wagon without authority, the wagon went through the ford to the axle, the far horse took fright, and a woman of about forty rode on the tailboard who was not on the warrant. Oren enters it in Cask's words without the word *failed* and tells both men they are neither to be blamed nor praised, and that he can do neither. Jerm's grievance: a record that names only the men who obey is a list of examples, so the book must name the refusers as well as the obedient. Oren agrees and writes the order each man was given and the thing each man did about it. The Crown's **return cites the Kells watch gate book, entries fourteen to nineteen, as evidence that the ward was notified and had responded** — and Oren's own hand wrote those entries as gate traffic. The black-case man asks to interview the two men who did the seizure in a private room; Oren requires an open door, the book on the table, a watch clerk, and attendance by any man who wants it. Oren then writes a **memorandum on his own motion**: the gate book is a record of traffic and observation and has no standing as a record of consent, any paper citing it is citing a document for a thing it does not contain, he has not read the gate book for the night of the Hush and will not let any man read it aloud because a man who has read it will be tempted to use it, and if the commission believes the watch has given an account of the ward's will then that account is false and it is false in his hand. He reads it out in open court at noon and leaves a copy on the clerk's table. Crake offers a district appointment at a stone bridge four days' ride; Oren refuses and insists on saying it in the open, which Crake accepts and warns will cost him. Tomas asks for the **second copy of the L-4 file** and is refused: the watch does not make copies of Crown files for private parties, though he may read all six sheets at the ninth hour with Jerm present and the reading itself noted.

## Chapter 0025 — The Order Was Entered

Day 11, midday into evening, Tamsin's POV — the volume's reserved midpoint. She cannot follow the argument at the board, so she reads the paper. Ferrow stopped his reading of the schedule at the ruled line below the forty-one households, as readers do, and has looked under the ruling twice without touching it. In the watch house she works out the office mark **OFF. 411** and finds the same four hundred and eleven on the response schedule, on the Crown culvert works order dated before the warning, on the maintenance service ticket's *answered in office* line, and on the inspection order itself. Then she reads under the ruling. There is one more row: the first field says **L-4** where every other line says a house number; the field where all forty-one hold a count of persons is **empty**; the response column holds the office mark; the date is **nine days before the carrier rang**, and the order it answers is dated three days before. Six days. The reversal: a bell carries a claim that a living person has taken responsibility for, so it does not need to know the future — somebody in that office accepted a duty about an inspection six days before the inspection was written, entered it in an ordinary form in the ordinary week, and the bell carried it. **It was not prophecy. It was filing.** Tamsin refuses to name a person, refuses to let Tomas guess one, and hands him only the arithmetic, and keeps her route. Her professional horror: the wax laid over the older groove in her own crown, which she has called an accident for eight months, is a technique, and the same technique lives in a district office in ink, on a form, with a column and a return cycle. Jerm reads the four findings out twice at the board to about sixty people and Tomas will not supply a bell, of which he has none. Oren calls OFF. 411 a district renewal desk, not a man.

## Chapter 0026 — The Name That Went

Day 12, morning, Nell's POV. Two families have left the ward in the night — 122 and 136 — and she does not write their number down. Her mother will go to the court and agree to anything that sounds like work, and Nell sends her to the washhouse instead. At the board she counts the schedule's response marks: **nine lines read *not returned* and six read *accompanying***, and every household the Crown took a child from is on one of those fifteen lines, while every other marked door is on a plain line. The Crown's own notes about people who were not in the house were read by the Crown as the answer. **Mercy Slee** of 119, whose boy is nine and who is on no roll, refuses to be on any list and says that the correct use of a true record might be to leave a house off it; Nell agrees to keep her slate free of names and numbers and to burn it where Mercy Slee can watch. A Crown clerk demands the ward board's covered box by the fourth hour; eleven people refuse it one at a time. At the half past one, in a filing room behind the market court, Nell asks **one question of Clerk Ansel Stroud**: is there a column on the Crown form for the person who was in the room when the answer was given. The answer is yes — the column is headed **Witnessed by**, it is empty on every Kells return Stroud has filed in eleven years (four hundred and six), it takes a name in a household's own hand, and a reason field exists for an office-entered answer that the summary form drops. Nell writes it down, reads it back, lets him correct one word, and then **burns the slate in the washhouse fire** after reading it to Mercy Slee, because the one paper that could have been used is about her own family. The Crown's notice invites evidence at the district renewal office at Fourmile.

## Chapter 0027 — The Surveyor Who Will Not Sign

Day 12, late day, Tomas. He asks Collis to countersign a paragraph saying the schedule's answers were not the households' answers. She refuses and explains the mechanic: a written statement that a certification means nothing will make the next round uncertifiable. She gives the narrow thing instead, and the market court hears it in the open at the seventh hour: a certification of a visit is a statement that a visit was made and not a statement that the answers under it are true; she was not shown a schedule, asked at the gate, and refused; and she will not countersign any record asserting that a household gave, assented to, or received an answer. The refusals are entered in full — she will not say the schedule is false, will not say who entered it, will not say whether the district office keeps a practice, will not name the twenty-six-year-old officer who held the schedule, and will not say the commission has done anything wrong — along with her statement that she is **not** the person named on the withdrawn service entry and will not be made into him. Crake objects that three true sentences read to a filing clerk as a Crown officer declining to warrant her own certification, and files that he would rather have nine houses and a clean return than forty-one houses and a clerk guessing. Tomas accepts the limit and writes it up rather than resenting it. In the doorway, **unofficially and off the record**, Collis says that a page like the schedule does not appear by itself and that an office does not enter pages, and declines to write it down, because a written sentence by a Crown surveyor about filing practice would end careers and would be quoted back to her. Tomas does not write it down.

## Chapter 0028 — Publishing a Practice

Day 13, full day. Three hours of argument produce the condition that **no bell is rung**, the paper goes on foot, and nobody goes past the toll shed. Tomas does not speak; Nell Fenn reads the finding, because a practice voiced by its maker is the thing the town has learned not to trust, and because a Crown office finds it easier to refuse a girl of sixteen with a slate. **Crown Officer Ambrose Rudd** objects, in terms, that a ward publishing a procedure for disputing its returns becomes a ward with a grievance, that the only way to notice a question is to notice the house, and that a ward whose houses are noted for asking is a ward asked fewer questions — Oren has the objection read out twice, in the morning as well, and it is true. Three households withdraw from the board's arrangement, including a woman whose grandmother is on line twenty-eight. **Oren compels a public Crown counter under the arcade** with one sentence to Rudd, and **Absalom Teage** puts the three questions to Clerk Stroud: the line is read, the day is given, the hand is refused, and Stroud writes two sentences in the margin in his own name and initials them — *No person in this house was asked. The entry was made in office. I cannot say whose hand* — and *Disputed at this counter, thirteenth day… The entry stands*. Teage's wife asks whether it is a win and is told it is a witness. **Gib Hask** states the Unrung case properly: a town that has learned to argue with paper will not be looking at the ground when a Crown gang renews the line, and he has six men and a bar and will not stop being told so. The page is made **co-held** by the watch archive and the ward board, with Mercy Slee named at the foot of the ward's copy, and read out twice. The Crown's answer arrives at the seventh hour: the round is **revised and posted for the twenty-first day**, with the form to be exhibited in full and any person admitted as a witness; the market court call bell and every bell joined to any line are heard at the next sitting; and the practice is declared to have originated in the Vale Bellhouse, whose standing the commission will consider. Tamsin: it is not about bells.

## Chapter 0029 — The Route She Will Not Give

Day 13, evening, Tamsin's POV, on the towpath. Her hearing has taken the **note**, not the strike, and she noticed it properly in a washhouse on the twelfth, so she can no longer carry at all. **The chapter states that no one in Kells is going to be told, because a courier who says her note is gone is a courier whose bell is free. She keeps it from Jerm, who walks the route beside her as her witness and is on the slate, and is never told what is wrong; Tomas, who comes down the towpath at the fourth hour with a slate and a day-book, is the one person she tells.** She walks her own route with Jerm and a slate and rings once at seven places. The reach answers at the gate, the washhouse, the market lane above the ward, the landing stage, forty yards above the stage, at the low drain gate itself — felt through four feet of stone — and a mile out at the first cottage past the toll shed, where the same woman came down to ask who was calling the market, as in June. It does not stop anywhere; it thins out. **She cannot find where her rule stops being true, so the bell cannot be trusted to carry even a keeper's boundary.** She therefore reduces what she offers to four things: paper on foot at doors with a person who can say what it means; the handbell rung once at a door only when somebody at that door asks and names what they want repeated; the handbell as nothing more than a *call to come to a door*, saying nothing about what; and nobody repeats it, for anybody, including the man who paid for the wax in her crown, and nobody may ask her for a reason. Tomas asks properly and is refused. She tells him the note is gone and she cannot carry again, that she has turned down two paid runs and the ferry company will not carry the ferry notice on a bell described in a Crown notice, so the towpath's nine coppers a month is gone, and that she will answer a request and refuse a request with no way to tell in advance. Tomas writes that he has **no standing to know**. At the end a rider comes up the towpath: a Crown man at the board asking whether the ward has appointed a keeper of any route, and two houses on the ferry road who have been to Fourmile to ask the Crown for a bell.

## Chapter 0030 — What the Town Does With It

Day 14, morning into evening. Three Crown notices before the eighth hour: the round is **revised for the twenty-first day with the form exhibited in full**; the market court call bell and every bell joined to any line are heard at the next sitting and the court is requested to produce the bell; and the ward-board practice is declared to have **originated in the Vale Bellhouse**, whose standing the commission will consider. Oren is ordered by the district to produce the Bellwrights' Measure's road map — the fifty-three recorded withdrawals — by the eleventh hour tomorrow, and enters that he produces it under compulsion and not as a witness to its contents. A petition from nine adults in four households asks to be placed on a Crown line, for the cart at the culvert. The court sits at the tenth hour with about two hundred people. Tomas says nothing for the first hour and a half, is asked four times what the ward should do, and refuses each time, including once on purpose so the refusal is in the public record. **Three positions are stated and none is merged**: Mercy Slee's — keep the record and the three questions and take no route from anybody, prepared to be poorer; **Ivo Prentice's** — ask the Crown for a line, because only a Crown order moves a cart, and he refuses to be ashamed of it in front of eleven witnesses; and Gib Hask's — take the copper out, with six men and a bar, asking the court to say in public whether it objects, and nobody tells him to his face. The question of the children is started at the third hour, gets nine words into the air, and is lost to a Crown clerk reading the market court bell notice; Tomas writes that it passed and underlines it. **Ansel Stroud files the ward's divided answer as a single response** and asks to read it both ways: the form's version, and the three columns, so that the whole room hears the single sentence the ward's three positions become in one box. He says it is a weapon for about a day. Tomas takes back the **wheelwright's counter bell** — the job the bound trial bell blocked on Day 3 — and dresses it for an hour and ten minutes, the only bell in town he can sound. At the last hour the Crown road gang is marking out in the low lane for a renewal tomorrow, in the lane where the copper runs and the children walk to the landing, and Hask's two men are at the low end with a bar and a spade.

## Chapter 0031 — The Lane at the Bottom of the Ward

Day 15, morning, Tomas, in the low lane below the Low Ward, the washhouse step and the landing approach. A Crown road gang pegs a renewal line across the lane at twenty past the seventh hour, eleven feet above the washhouse step, in the lane **eleven children walk to the landing twice a day** — eleven feet wide, eleven children, and the chalk crossing it. **Gib Hask** has been at the low end of the turn since before Tomas arrived, with six men, a crowbar, a spade and a coil of rope, having asked a court to tell him to its face whether it objects and been told by the clerk that nobody had. Tomas can see, by looking at it, that the mark stands over a **filled service cut** — the Bellwrights' washhouse run, sixty years old or more, its paler settled line crossing the lane at an angle — and he can corroborate it in one line of his father's bench-book, which puts the washhouse corner a yard and a half from the mark. He says so out loud, in the lane, to the gang, to Hask and to about thirty people, **at an ordinary volume, because that is the only one available**; he does not touch the Crown line, does not ask them to stop and does not invite it. The foreman, **Brann**, who signed for the cart at the low-turn culvert, presses his spade down, gets a stone at a foot, moves the mark **two yards toward the ward side on his own hand** at nine minutes past the ninth hour, and initialled the change with **Perry**, a Crown road hand of nineteen who keeps the gang's book and who asked Tomas to say the reason again so that it would be in a mouth that was not hers. Brann says the mark came off a plan sent up with the works order on **Day 8**, that he has not read the ground part of it, and that he will find out who drew it and write it in the gang's book. Hask's argument is aimed at Tomas and is true: the ward has a man who can read the ground and the Crown has had him on an inventory since the second day. **A Crown clerk is on the lane about two hours later to complete a form field headed *cause stated by*, and the foreman's report — naming Tomas Vale as the man who advised him — has already been copied out fair into a district office.** Tomas tells the clerk to enter his name, the hour, and his reasons in his own words and not to be summarised, and then refuses, in front of twenty people, to ask anybody to close the lane. Hask calls it a coward's answer. Tomas enters that Hask is right and he has no answer. The gang dug from the ninth hour to the seventh; at three feet the first shovelful was old lime and a bell offcut with the casting seam still on it.

## Chapter 0032 — What Was Under the Ground

Day 15, midday into evening, Tomas with Crake, the low lane, the watch house and the survey store. The copper comes up at the fourth hour in three lengths, hand's breadth, sheathed in black cloth gone to the consistency of tar. **Under the lime bed beneath it there is not lime: there is a stone channel, hand wide, three courses, the ward's own drain**, shown on the Crown sheet that came with the works order in a **different ink, dashed, labelled *existing culvert***, and **bridged at four points** by the Crown line above it. A conduit from the millrace to the washhouse trough runs within a yard for about forty feet and is not lifted. Crake comes at the fifth hour, verifies items one to three himself with a spade, **asks Tomas for a name for the file**, and is given the page and not the name, on the ground that a correction sourced to a Crown officer's name sends a Crown man to the ward instead of correcting a sheet. Crake signs the page **in his own name against his own works order of the eighth day**, and says the thing he is right about: a rain puts a foot of water in that lane, Renn's gate will not come, and there are eleven children in it. Tomas's page ends: **I have no instrument.** At the eighth hour, at the watch house, Oren reports the eleventh hour's business — **the Bellwrights' Measure's road map has gone to the district in a sealed case with a receipt, fifty-three dated withdrawals, and the officer read the *refused* column, asked the year of the first, was told the Measure keeps dates, and said he must file it as a schedule of non-response. Nobody asked Sabin Fell.** Oren says aloud that the reason he did not send word is that he did not want to be in a room with him while it happened, and has it entered. At the ninth hour in the Bellhouse yard, Tomas finds **a second line under his own floor**: a damp strip from the ash-pit wall to the back door that does not dry, a rod finding it easy two feet, hard a foot, easy again, running out under the yard wall toward the millrace, and **under the loose threshold stone two flat iron bars a foot apart, wrapped in tarred rope and pitch**. Oren enters it in the gate book in three sentences, tells the ward board nothing, and gives the reason: an unexplained line under a workshop the Crown has already sealed comes back as an order with a spade in it, and the answer to *who put it there* in this town is one word long. **It is known to three people: Tomas, Oren, Jerm.** Tomas writes the reason in his own day-book in a book nobody will look in.

## Chapter 0033 — The Road That Is Not Closed

Day 16, full day, Tomas with Wyman Dace and Foreman Crane; the west road, the toll shed, the mill narrows and the market lane. Dace keeps three slates and **writes the wind and never the word safe**, because a bridge is not a road and a keeper who says his bridge is safe has said he does not expect to be asked why. Tomas walks the three narrows twice, dry both times: one above the ford where a wagon gathering way cannot pull itself back up, and **the mill narrows**, where the bank is cut to a foot and a half for thirty feet above cut steps belonging to the town and a hundred yards short of Dace's bridge. **A bar and a chain stops a loaded wagon and does not stop a cart**; a stopped wagon has three ways out and one of them is the bridge. Crane can close a lane in an hour and **will not do it for a warning**, and says why: he carried a boy of nine onto a tailboard on Day 10, and a foreman told what to do is the thing he cannot be trusted for. **Crane's question is the one nobody had asked — who pays for a road a keeper has closed? — and Tomas's answer is the market court's small fund, which has never been spent, and a figure the clerk read out twice and nobody wrote down.** Tomas does not ask anybody to close anything. He hands over four hundred words that contain not one opinion and says he has lost the ability to be useful on the question the ward most wants answered, and the ward turns to him anyway and he sends them to the three people who said the sentences. **At ten minutes past the seventh hour three sentences are said in three columns in the market lane, in front of about sixty people, and nobody merges them**: **Alis Marrow** of the toll shed, whose seventy-nine-year-old mother goes down four cut steps four times a day; **Crane**, who puts *road* in his sentence four times and *warned* once; and **Dace**, from a slate, read out and handed to Ferrow to hold. The bar and chain go up with a man and a lamp. **A Crown officer goes round the ferry road asking whether the ward has shut its own road, and Ivo Prentice tells him it has, on the ward's own account, and then volunteers that the Crown cart is still across the culvert** — handing the district the sentence Tomas predicted on page four of his own sheet. The same day, **house 91 is found packing for Fourmile**, and the reason is that on Day 14 a young man behind a counter in Fourmile told Marda Hodler that *the round comes at first light, and they will want the children out of the ward, and the ones that are out of the ward on the day are the ones that are not moved.* She told four households, including Siss Larke at 96, who has done more of it because she has a boy of fourteen. Tomas says the ward has not been told to move a child, writes down that he has not seen a word about it, and declines to ask her to stop telling people, because that would be asking her to lie to her neighbours. She will wait until Friday. **Not because she believes him. Because it costs her four days to wait and everything to be wrong.**

## Chapter 0034 — A Watch Captain's Arithmetic

Day 17, morning into afternoon, **Oren's POV**, the watch house and the market court. The district's return asks for a list of the ward's signal keepers **and of all persons in the ward having public standing in matters of signalling, with names**, by the seventh hour. Oren works out in a few hours that the twelve words *all persons in this ward having public standing in matters of signalling* are the mouth of the fish, and that the reason he can answer in a morning is that he spent nine days teaching this town to speak in public with dates on it. He writes **eleven names with dates** — including **Mercy Slee of 119, who is on no roll, and Tamsin Rook, who is on it for the thirteenth day, when she walked her own route after dark with a named man beside her, rang once at seven places and wrote what was expected and what was heard at each, and then offered four terms out loud, once each** — in a flat hand, **with no column saying what they said, because the Crown paper has none and inventing one would be a forgery dressed as a courtesy. He reads the first five paragraphs of the covering page to Ardle and Cask before he signs it and the sixth after, and reads all six in open court.** **Watchman Cask** reports that a Crown officer asked him at a tavern on the night of Day 14, loudly and in front of a table of men, whether the watch would do it again. Ardle reads the list, says that publishing the ward's practice has put Mercy Slee on a Crown return under *public standing*, and asks whether it is not the opposite of what she asked. Oren says he cannot remove or protect one name, and says so to both men in plain words. He adds a **six-paragraph covering page**, the sixth of which is entered later in the chapter and says that no keeper in this ward is entered on the return as a keeper — a list of keepers identifies duties and duties can be answered, a list of persons who have spoken identifies citizens, the practice of public record is the watch's own doing and every entry is a lawful proceeding at which the watch was present, no keeper list is produced on a return framed in these terms, and the watch has protected nobody and removed nobody — and **reads it aloud to Cask and Ardle before signing it**, which he has never done in eleven years. He sends it and reads it and the whole list aloud in open court with the ward in the room and Nell Fenn at the clerk's table, and tells the district clerk that the ward asked questions in public on purpose because a thing said in a room arrives in a file alone, and that legibility is not being helped. **He puts the two men who said *do not send it* on the gate for the days that matter, in writing.** Crake arrives with an order he will not show, on the ground that showing a man a paper you must act on and cannot refuse is making him choose; he gives the district the answer it wants — no disorder, and a ward with a public practice that has declined every request made of it — and **declines to state a figure of signal duty holders in a public room**, on purpose, and lets it be entered. **At the eighth hour that evening the return comes back with one line in a foreign margin: index retained, no further return as to keepers required, and the return amended to read *persons having public standing in matters of signalling, with dates*.** The Crown did not lose the list. It renamed it. Oren enters the amendment, will read it into the market court at its next sitting, has not appealed, and has not asked one of the eleven.

## Chapter 0035 — The Order He Could Not Defend

Day 17, evening, Oren with Tomas; the low gate, the watch house and the yard. **Serjeant Teale** reads out a form at the low gate at dusk requiring, for seven days, a record of **every bell sounded within the ward, with the hour, the place, and the name of the person who sounded it**, including any bell joined to any line, wire or ground, and including a bell rung in the open with people standing in front of it. Ardle says at once that it is a keeper list by the back door. **Oren neither obeys nor refuses.** He complies with the hours, the places and every bell, joined or unjoined, held by anyone, and refuses the name, on the ground that a register of the names of persons who sound bells, made by a Crown office in a ward in the week of a round, is a list of persons who can be removed from a route — a distinction he enters, with the Crown's own public position, in four plain sentences that Jerm reads back. Teale is told the office will have to send a form that says *must* and names the field *keeper*, and Oren's four sentences are about a *keeper register* the district was refused at the front door. Then, in the yard with the door shut, **Oren tells Tomas in advance that the watch will be a record and will not be a route**, that if his bell goes the watch writes the hour and the place and not the keeper's name, **and that this is a gift and it is also a leash** — because he has now told every woman in a doorway in the ward in advance that the watch will come and write and will not stand there, and because he has been two men and a gate for twelve years and did not put two men between a lawful order and seventy-odd people on Day 10. Tomas accepts and writes it down in about forty words with no request in it, and says that if he had written four sentences Oren would have taken one as a request. Then Tomas asks the question he walked down the hill to ask — *if a bell rang tonight and nobody answered, what would the watch do?* — and Oren gives him the shape of it: **a bell with no answer is a noise; the watch would write the hour and the place and nothing else; and the harm would not be the four hours of waiting, it would be the page afterwards that says the ward waited four hours, because a crowd that has waited four hours has been shown that waiting works.** And he says he is not Tomas's protector and has stopped pretending to be, and that he is telling him where the teeth are because he has signed his name off a page of his own.

## Chapter 0036 — The Column Nobody Has Seen

Day 18, full day, **Nell's POV**, the washhouse, house 119, the ward board and the arcade. The problem of publishing a thing you have destroyed is that you have to be able to swear to it and cannot show anybody. Nell works out — after eight days of refusing to let Tomas speak first — that a column has to become a question before it can go into anybody's mouth, and that the only shape that works is a question any household may put to a Crown clerk **about a paper the Crown handed them**, because the Crown puts the question in its own mouth. She gives the slate to Mercy Slee before she says anything further. **Mercy Slee takes two thirds of the argument**: *any person* becomes *a person who wishes to*, with a line that nobody has to and nobody has to say why; *dispute* becomes *asked*, because words are the whole of what is being taken off them; and *clerk* is struck and replaced with *officer of the Crown*, because the word has begun to mean one particular young man with ink on his fingers, and if that is the word the ward publishes, the first person to be frightened of is the only person it has. The price is that the first household to ask in a Crown room becomes a household the Crown has a file on, and Nell works out that it must be Mercy Slee because her house is on nothing, and Mercy Slee says out loud that a girl who asks people first is still a girl who made the decision happen and has not noticed, and that if nobody had been there Nell would have done it herself and been quite right to, and that this is a worse thing and not a better one. **Mercy Slee then gives the reason nobody expected**: a Crown officer has been at her door twice since Day 10 and the only thing she has is a question that makes them write down what they did, and if asking it puts a file on her house then the file is on a house that has nothing else on it, and she would rather have a file than be invisible on the twenty-first. **The column question is read aloud in daylight at the ward board at the fourth hour, in about forty people's hearing, by Mercy Slee, who says her own address out loud first; Nell Fenn's name, Mercy Slee's and Oren Pike's are on it, and Oren's is the first thing he has put his name under in this town that is not a matter of order.** At the sixth hour **the first household to put the question to a Crown clerk is Sena Dunn of house 121, whom nobody chose**, with a copy of the slate and Mercy Slee beside her to say what the words mean. The clerk is Ansel Stroud, and she has the slate and a copy of it that Mercy Slee insisted on, and Mercy Slee beside her to say what the words mean; and his answer takes four minutes and ten seconds: the column is for a person present at the answering, the hand is the house's, it is empty on every Kells return, and it is **not** empty in every district and he is prohibited from saying which. And then, unentered, in front of about thirty people, **he says the rounds in this ward are conducted at four in the morning and there is no room at four in the morning** — and refuses to write it down. Sena Dunn asks for her number to be left in the book, having seen him write it, and says she would like a page saying she came to a table on her own feet. Nell writes five lines and stops at the fourth, and the fifth is that she did not choose the first household and the slate was not hers.

## Chapter 0037 — A Plain Bell

Day 19, morning into afternoon, Tomas with Sabin Fell; the Bellhouse yard, the watch house, the Measure's temporary room, the millrace and the ward board. Sabin will not supply a design — if he does, what gets copied is him — and gives four rules. **The fourth is the one he has never given anybody in this county in twenty years: take the person most likely to refuse, and ask them first.** The first answer is a claim and every later answer is a claim about the first, and a carrier carried by three people who are all certain is a carrier that has had its teeth taken out on the way to the market. Also: the witness matter must be **findable**, or it is a relic and not a witness; and a carrier may not add anything, which means a bell is a mouth, a thing that comes out of it a long way off arrives thinner and rounder and more certain than it went in, and a man who tunes a bell to stop at a distance is tuning it to a lie. Tomas asks Oren **in writing** to cast and bench-test; Oren cuts his own request of one strike in the hour down to one, on the bench, with a leather, not in the lane, with Jerm holding the book, signs it, and enters that he has permitted a man under a Crown declaration to cast a bell and that this is the page he will have to defend. **The bell is cast out of the last of a half-barrel of the shop's own bellmetal out of his father's hearth, which was never on a Crown inventory because an inventory counts objects.** Six and a half inches at the mouth, ash stock, iron clapper, no wax, no groove, only VALE KELLS and the date. Two faults are recorded: **it is too bright and he cannot reduce it**, and **it repeats within eight feet of the ash-pit wall**, because a bell near hard stone comes back a fraction later and a thing that comes out twice is a claim and a copy of a claim. The Bellwrights' cut to the market lane was cut by the Crown's own trench on Day 15 — a route that is not connected. **He asks the ward board for one bar of the ward's own drain grating at the ninth hour, in daylight, in front of about thirty people, having read the whole of the claim and the whole of the cost out first, including the fact that the Crown register will carry it to Fourmile on the twenty-fourth — and the board gives it before the metal is melted.** Mercy Slee writes the gift, the date, the words and the cost in the ward's book with two more signatures, and Hask sends word that the grate is his to keep. The condition is written up in the ordinary way, with the weight at the mouth and the shoulder and the difference being his and known to be his. He asks **four** people, one at a time, with the same words, having meant to ask three. **Tamsin Rook is asked first**, because Sabin's rule, and because Tomas had her at the bottom of his own list for two days out of pure wanting and told her so in writing with the hour on it before he asked her anything. **She refuses at once**, says *no* before the reason because a no with the reason first is a different kind of no, and gives one her fourth term does not require: a bell in a claim cannot be a bell that is only a call to come to a door, and if she takes a public duty the day somebody wants her bell they will not have to argue with her about the route, because the route will already be gone. She writes it and signs it, and then tells him, before she goes, what the bell is going to do. Wenna Cray accepts with four clauses and makes him read them twice; Hollis Renn accepts in eleven words and writes his own sentence on his oilskin board; **Foreman Crane refuses in a minute and signs the refusal at Tomas's request.** Four asked, two accepted, two refused, and the count is a count of two, and he does not go looking for a fifth. He rings it once at the fifth hour of the afternoon in the middle of the yard, and **he does not know where it was heard**. Two people heard it more than a quarter of a mile off and neither of them knew who had sent it.

## Chapter 0038 — Further Than the Rule

Day 19, evening, Tomas; the low lane, the Bellhouse yard, the market lane and the ward board. The water comes at the sixth hour, **a day and some hours early, on a date written on a page on the ward board.** Renn's gate will not come and he says the cause out loud: the culvert and the ward's drain are one water and there is a Crown cart across the low-turn culvert under a works order a fortnight old. **Tomas had known, and had left the cart out because a Crown work in a warning brings a Crown office to the ward, and had put in a drain he could stand in and prove. The claim was true and the reason in it was wrong.** He rings once, correctly, and Renn and Cray answer once each inside the two hundred yards they agreed to, and it buys four things — eleven children stopped on the lane, Hask's four men on their dry step, a carter turned back at the low turn — and it reaches neither the market, nor the ferry road, nor the mill end of the west road. **At the eighth hour Siss Larke stops him with a rope round a box and a boy of fourteen and asks whether to send the box or the boy**, and he has nothing, and a paper takes a day and a bell takes a moment. He takes the bell off its frame and carries it into the market lane and **rings it four times and asks nobody.** He sends the true easy part only, having read Sabin's sentence about what a long thin answer turns into, and the market lane clears: about one hundred and eighty persons in nine minutes, about seventy through the north gate in the next hour, a carrier named Rutledge loses a handcart and load at **thirty-six pounds**, and **Perry — nineteen, a Crown road hand, standing in the market lane with a book and a lamp because Tomas had written to her on Day 15 and asked her to be there and write down where his words reached — has her right wrist broken by the shoe of a handcart.** He stops it himself on the fish market step at the fifth hour of the morning of the twentieth, in daylight, in front of about sixty people, and does not call it a false alarm, because the alarm was true and the panic was his fault. He records the cost in figures and words, **names Crane and Tamsin Rook as the two whose refusal would have prevented a keeper at the mill end of the west road**, tells the keepers before the ward, and gives no explanation for four days. Oren enters the whole nineteenth in the bell register by place and by hour and by nothing else — **six in the maker's own yard** (three bench strikes with a leather an hour apart, one in the open, the fifth hour of the afternoon, and the seventh hour, which is the evening), one at the low drain gate, one at the ward board, four in the market lane — with *none* in the name column **twelve** times, and does not ask him, having thought about it for a day, because the answer would be in a book. **And at half past the eighth hour on the ferry road, six hundred yards off, Ivo Prentice repeated it in his own gate and added four words Tomas never said: *at first light*** — the Crown's own phrase off the Crown's own notice for the twenty-first, repeated four more times that night by people believing they were telling their neighbours.

## Chapter 0039 — The Form, Exhibited

Day 20, full day, **Nell's POV**, the board, the market court and the arcade. Nell gets a paper in front of a Crown clerk before the eighth hour, citing the Crown's own notice of the seventh evening, and is answered in forty minutes with the right answer: the form will be exhibited **today**, in the same room the round will be held in, entire, readable from the floor, untouchable, admission by the door. About a hundred and thirty people come — the ward is smaller than on Day 14. The Crown exhibits the **summary form**, in full, and **there is no *Witnessed by* column on it, and that is true.** Nell stands up and asks whether the ward board's pinned page came off this form, and it did not — it is six lines deep where this is one, and it has a persons field and a row under the last rule that is not a house. **Ansel Stroud stands up, unasked, says he brought the full return in his satchel and was not going to put it on that table, and has it, carries it to the table himself, squares the top edge with both hands, and has its ninth column read aloud: *Witnessed by*, printed, and empty.** The Crown then does the thing nobody had prepared for: **it offers to fill the column**, today, for any household that names a person. **Mrs Corris of house 133** — the household that gave no answer and no name on Day 10, whose thirteen-year-old was taken, and to which nobody had been back in eleven days — asks what the column is for, is told, is told by Mercy Slee what a name in it would cost, and says she will put **her own** name in, because she was in the room, because it is her house, and because she is sixty-five and not frightened of a book. Stroud writes it in the margin in his own name and initials it: the second time a Crown clerk has written in a margin in a public room, and the ward counted, and the first of them was a carter's. The Crown then produces a **supplementary return** carrying the objection, the witness name, **and the reason field the summary form drops** — an answer to everything Nell wanted, and a trap, because nothing on it says which hand wrote the reason. Mercy Slee puts it in one sentence: *they have put the box and the label on the same shelf.* The ward refuses in **three columns** — Mercy Slee on a name in a Crown hand; **Absalom Teage**, who turns the argument round and asks for the reason field on the form a house is handed, today, or none of it; and **the market court usher**, who declines to say a fourth thing about her bell — and the refusal is entered as a dated public act. **Crown Officer Rudd then offers Surveyor Collis's certification to any household on the route**, not the form but a person, and says plainly that he is not being kind: a commission that refuses to offer is a commission that has lied, and the price is her and not him. **Mrs Corris says the sentence that makes the offer survivable for an afternoon: *nine of our doors, out of forty-one.*** At the ninth hour of the evening, **two Crown officers go round about thirty doors with a slate and a lamp, politely, asking whether the household intends to attend in person, and write down every answer**, and about forty households said yes, and the slate goes to Fourmile in a satchel. Nell hears about it from a boy of eleven and writes two lines, the second of which is that the whole of her argument to those doors was that a question is a notice, and the Crown has now found a cheaper notice than hers, and that she will read that line out at the board tomorrow before anything else, because the day she stops is the day the ward stops being able to check anything.

## Chapter 0040 — The Twenty-First Day

Day 21, first light into the last hour, Tomas with the ward; the ward board, the market lane, the low lane, the market court and the Bellhouse yard. **Three hundred and forty people are in the lane at first light by Oren's count at the north gate** — forty of them had said yes to a Crown officer at their door the night before, and about three hundred came unasked, including about sixty who had no business being out of bed. Nell reads her own line of the previous night out first, in the market court, before anything else, as she swore she would. The round opens at first light and **Surveyor Collis's return says it was conducted at thirty-seven doors with the form exhibited entire and any person admitted as a witness**, with a Crown table under the arcade that the watch compelled on Day 9 and nobody withdrew. **At twenty-two of thirty-seven doors a household put the three questions and had the answers written in the margin in a clerk's own name and initialled; at nine of those the answer was *I am not permitted to say*, and that was written down too.** And **one child is removed: Hattie Wilde, of house 108, aged ten, at the fifth hour and thirty minutes.** The household had never been marked. The line reads five entered, five returned, and the *Witnessed by* column **is not cleared between rounds**, is filled in **two lines** that day, and on the Wilde line holds the name of the household's mother, in the mother's own hand, given at that door **two autumns ago and carried forward through every round since**; the mother has been dead two years, and the other filled line is Mrs Corris's own name, put in a public margin the previous afternoon. Mrs Wilde says *that name has been dead two years* in a lane with sixty people. The name is not struck out, the objection is entered in a margin in a clerk's own name and initialled, **and the objection does not suspend the renewal.** There is no scene. **Tomas stands four feet away with a bell half a mile off and does not move.** Renn tells him at the sixth hour that there is not a bell in this town that says anything about a wagon, and Tomas says out loud, in front of the ward, that the reason is not law and not courage: a bell cannot be made to say a thing it was not bound to say, anything else would be a version, and there is already a version on the ferry road. Absalom Teage gives his reason out loud unasked. **Crake comes down the lane without a black case and says the truest sentence anybody in the commission has said all month: a ward that publishes a procedure can make an office legible and cannot make an office stop; the Crown's paper became good enough to be worth reading, the reading is done by a clerk in a building, the clerk is right, and the child goes; and careful is not the same as effective, and they are all about to find out how different those are on the twenty-eighth.** He says he is going to write the word *clean* on a page about a morning on which he had thirty-seven houses and a child. **Tomas spends the whole round writing down what the ward actually did, house by house, in three columns headed — not *position*, not *answer* — *first thing, second thing, third thing*, fifty-one lines, checkable at any door** — including 108, including 133, and including the fact that Mrs Corris's own name in the column is half of the single worst fact of the day and that nobody noticed. Perry returns the paper he wrote to her on Day 15, and he does not take it back, and Mercy Slee enters that the reason he did not is *that he has learned the one thing this month, which is that a man who takes a thing back off a person who is frightened has had it twice.* Oren records three answers in three columns and states, in the watch's answer, that the word *standing* in that office means, in this ward, that a person stood in a lane and was heard. **Tamsin's route is not used and she states four reasons in public, the fourth of which is *I cannot hear it*, and about nine people hear the difference — **and then she publishes the fact of it herself, on purpose, in the same lane**, which breaks the Chapter 29 rule that nobody in Kells was to be told. **This is deliberate and is recorded as a canon change.**** The Crown's answer is nailed at half past the eleventh hour and is the shortest notice the commission has ever put on that board: the ward's practice is **a matter for the district and no longer for the local court**; the renewal is **posted again for the twenty-eighth day**; and, in a second paragraph in the small hand, **the district will consider the renewal of the ward's own line, of the possession of the ground in which it is laid, and of the persons under seventeen in the Low Ward.** The Crown has spent nine days building a procedure and has found a cheaper one: the ward's own unasked question, asked out loud, in the Crown's hand, in five words. And at the last hour Serjeant Teale reads a fourth paragraph, which is a question and not a notice: **the district will accept any answer** to whether the ward intends to remove the persons under seventeen from the ward before the next renewal and **to whom**, will certify it, and **a certified answer will be a return.** Nobody answers. Tomas's four things are on a fresh slate on the bench: the route fragment, the three column-headings, the copy of the schedule co-held with Mercy Slee, and the custody receipt with his correction in the margin. **The trial handbell is in Crown custody in a district building, bound and unspent, never rung. The fixed carrier is in the watch store in its frame in a padded cradle, and the joint receipt on its file expired at the fourth hour of Day 10 and there has still never been a custody review. The half-barrel is empty. The door has three seals on it and all three are still on.**

## Chapter 0041 — What a Page Is For

Day 22, full day, Tomas, with Nell and Mercy. Hask's six men are back at the low end of the turn **with the crowbar in the ground four inches**, and **Renn's gate has still not come up** and the Crown cart is still across the culvert under a works order the Crown's own papers carry as current. A **printed extract of Collis's return** comes up on the morning post and is read at the ward board: **thirty-seven doors, the form exhibited entire, twenty-two objections in margins in a clerk's own name and initialled, nine of them recording the answer *I am not permitted to say*, one person removed at the fifth hour and thirty minutes, and the *Witnessed by* column filled in two lines and not cleared between rounds** — with Collis's four sentences about a form, a round, and a surveyor who no longer knows what a form is for. **Mrs Corris says the second number, and Nell reads both and refuses to let the lane have either without the other.** Tomas hands his own **fifty-one-line page to Mercy Slee** and keeps nothing. He is asked about the market lane four times and refuses four times; at the fifth ask, by **Absalom Teage** in a lane with about sixty people, he gives the same reason he gave on the twentieth and will not improve it, and says he is not going to give anybody a version. Then **Teage begins asking one question at each door — what did you do on the twenty-first, and who saw you do it — gets to nineteen doors by the second hour of the afternoon, and writes his own answer at 114: *Nothing. Stood. Two hours. I have a boy of nine.*** Tomas watches from his own gate and does not help. **The Crown's answer is not a notice but a second-series supplementary return** to every household before the close of the twenty-sixth, with the reason field the ward demanded, a witness field, no signature, and a certification. Mercy Slee names it: it is the three questions in a Crown hand with the argument taken out. **The chapter's sentence, entered by Oren in the gate book: a procedure can make an office legible and cannot make an office stop — and the page is not the town's instrument, it is what the town has instead of a reason.**

## Chapter 0042 — The Name in the Column

Day 23, full day, **Nell** with Mercy Slee and **Mrs Wilde of 108**. Nell wants the Wilde household at the board; Mercy refuses her the argument and then gives it back — that it is a good argument and it is about *her*, and she has been letting it be about her for nine days. The alignment is named and **not spent**: *I have got something and I am not going to use it.* At 108, Mrs Wilde is on the floor of a front room with neighbours and is described before she speaks: she is not grief, she is a person waiting to find out whether she is going to be told. She refuses for nine seconds, then sets the terms: **she will read the question at the side of the clerk's table with her back to the lane, she asks to be named before she stands, and she names her own price in advance** — a file on house 108, forever, and being the one who is looked at. Her grounds: **eleven letters to Fourmile in two years, four answers, three saying the return stands, one saying a return is not amended except at a renewal, and a daughter of ten in a wagon because of a name her mother-in-law wrote before the daughter was born.** She quotes Tamsin's Saturday reasoning back as the reason she has chosen which version of herself gets written down. Nell publishes the **second column question** in a household's own words — *is there a name in the Witnessed by column on my line; is that person living; whose hand is it in* — and knocks it at **eleven doors** with no slate, because a slate is a notice. The finding is that **the never-cleared column is a practice and not one household's bad luck, and that it exists on lines the Crown has never acted on** — including a house at 44 that has never been marked and never visited, carrying a dead elder sister's name. A Crown officer comes to the ward board to ask whether a **list of households intending to remove children** exists, and **Nell has the word *none* entered in three hands**; he says he did not want to be the one asking it and will not write that down either.

## Chapter 0043 — The District

Day 24, full day, **Oren**, with Tomas in the evening. A district form at the second hour requires keeper **names for seven days commencing with the twenty-first, a day already elapsed.** Oren neither obeys nor refuses: he accepts the hours, the places and every bell, declines the names, and adds a **second reason in his own hand — that he knows who sounded the four bells in the market lane on the nineteenth, has not said it in any book, and enters it so that he will not hold a private file. He names nobody.** He also enters his own Day 10 conduct, his Day 17 signature and the eleven names, so that if an office ever says the watch withheld, it will find it on a page and not in an assembly. At the seventh hour he walks the register down to the north gate himself and **permits the district's rider to read it on the parapet in a lane in front of about nine people, at the watch captain's own risk and with his reason entered first**; the rider reads all twelve entries of the nineteenth and the word *none* twelve times and says a Crown man in Fourmile is going to want a name. In the evening Oren does the arithmetic he has avoided: **the four papers of the fortnight all end in the word *names*, and he supplied every one of them** — the eleven, the twelve *none*s, the refusal, and the *none* entered under the girl of sixteen. He says he has built a town out of names without entering a ward, and that he has done it with his own hand. The district's return comes back on its own horse and **names, before the register has arrived, the officer who will read it: District Officer Marten Cope, of the district renewal office at Fourmile**, and Oren reads the name out loud, has Jerm enter it, and says the sentence of the day: *it has been a desk for eleven years and there is a name in it, and I have only just worked out that never was a comfort.*

## Chapter 0044 — The Name in the Margin

Day 24, evening, **Tomas** with **Tamsin Rook** at the low drain gate and afterwards in his own yard. **His four days of silence end and he says the market lane out loud to her first**, including that she could not tell whether he rang it once or four times, because she felt it in four feet of stone and cannot hear a note and will not hear one again. She tells him the whole of the same fact from her end and neither of them can fix the other's: **a signal that goes further than its keeper can govern is not a fault in the signal, and a keeper who cannot hear where it went is the same problem from the other side, and two people who are extremely good at this cannot do the work for each other and have to go on.** **He does not read the trial handbell's custody receipt to her and the chapter does not have him do it.** He gives her the leaf and goes up the hill; afterwards, alone in his own yard with a lamp, he takes out the **custody receipt — his margin correction, *Object received bound. No answer recorded at taking. Maker has not certified the binding*, and a single initial under it that is not a name and is not agreement** — and says out loud, to nobody, that an initial in a margin is a man saying *I have seen this* and going back to his desk, and that the only difference between that and agreement is that one of them has to come back to the door. **He writes the request** — a leaf off the day-book with a heading, three numbered lines (the answer may be no; no reason required or asked for; he may not know in advance), a finding on the other side of the fold, and a witness line — and **gives it to Jerm** and does not press. She reads it in about a second and a half, does not read the other side, tells him she is not going to answer tonight, and says she will not be dignified about it. **The chapter does not say what she has chosen.** She goes up the towpath and does not come back down it. In the yard he sets the plain carrier out in the open, looks at it, and realises the claim lives in four pieces of paper and that on the twenty-seventh it will have to stop carrying it. **He hears, or does not hear, and could not afterwards have said which, a sound once off the river, out in the dark, where there is no route and no door; and there is nothing there when he listens.**

## Chapter 0045 — The Ward's Answer

Day 25, morning into evening, **Nell** with Oren and Mercy Slee. Nell finally asks her two people: **Mercy Slee, who agrees on conditions, and Mrs Wilde of 108**, who refuses for nine seconds and then takes the whole of the arrangement back from Nell in reverse — *sit me at the side, and put my name first, and I want it entered that I asked what it cost before I said yes.* The market court sits at the ninth hour with about **two hundred and thirty people**. **Oren Pike certifies the room and refuses to certify the contents**, for the first time in eleven years, and gives the reason in the open. Nell reads the ward's answer: three columns, each separately certifiable, the three not certifiable together, and **the third column empty, because a certification is a witness and a witness is a person and a third column is not a person** — and when the room counts seven, she answers that an empty column can be counted two ways and both counts are true, and a sentence is the one thing a Crown office has never had to check. **Mrs Wilde reads her own question at the side of the table with her back to the lane, once, and sits down, and the room goes absolutely silent.** **Three supplementary returns are on the board and Nell has three house numbers copied onto the back of the pinned schedule: 114, 133 and 121**, of which **121 was written on the afternoon of the twenty-fourth** and the chapter dates no other; Chapter 41 has two households answering by the morning of the twenty-third, which is what makes three. The first column has one household and the second twenty-five and the third is empty. Between about thirty and forty households then come one at a time over five hours and say one of three things themselves. **Mrs Corris of 133 puts her hand up in a full court and takes the Crown surveyor's certification, says why, and says out loud that she will not be asked about it; the ward does not treat it as betrayal, and she names that herself.** **Tomas is in the court for five hours, says nothing, rings nothing, and is not asked once**, and Mercy Slee tells him so and asks him to write *nobody asked, nobody turned round* as a fact and not a lesson. **The district's reply is an instruction to Crown Officer Ambrose Rudd to attend at the ninth hour on the twenty-eight and produce four things** — the full return entire, both series of supplementary returns with the house numbers, and the authority to ascertain what works of metal are in the ground of the Vale Bellhouse yard — plus a fourth line under the fold requiring the watch to say whether any bell is bound to a claim and by whom, **not accepting *none* as an answer and not accepting it as a refusal.**

## Chapter 0046 — The Measure

Day 26, full day, **Tomas** with **Iven Sore** and **Sabin Fell**. The Measure sits on its own notice. Sabin is not in the room and nobody mentions it. Sore takes the bell item first and states his limitation before opening: the Measure has not seen the bell since the nineteenth, has not heard it and does not propose to, and everything it says is said from a record and not from an ear. **He is right, and about something that is not the trial handbell.** He reads four clauses of craft law: a hearth stock is not a traceable source; a bell with no certificate of stock cannot be made certifiable afterwards; a Crown declaration does not touch the making; **but a bell made on declared ground out of an untraceable stock is a bell that exists, is in use, is carried on a public route, and whose maker cannot show where the metal came from, and that is a hazard to the public created by the declaration and not by the maker** — and he says he did not see clause four on the nineteenth and that it was in front of him. The four houses want the licence; **Sore is one of the four and says he is right and is right about a different thing.** Tomas does not defend himself, refuses to use Sabin's absent kindness as a lever, and says in an open room that **he does not know what a bell is for.** The Measure enters the narrow finding: **a professional bar on the plain carrier**, not on the licence, with the four houses' request refused; the Measure will not certify, inventory, attend with a spade, or supply the commission with anything. And **in the room Sabin asked not to be in, Tomas decides to put the Measure's road map on the record in writing** — that the map is a craft map of refusals and not a schedule of non-response, and that the district is asked to enter that. It goes on the minute book and a copy goes to the market court. **Sabin finds out within three hours, comes up the lane at dusk, and is entitled to be angry and is** — the argument is that Tomas answered for the craft's record without asking the man whose life's work it is, and the sentence he leaves across forty feet of cobbles is: *I was in a hurry, and being in a hurry felt like duty, and it was not duty, it was the other thing.* He asks for the copy **not to read, to put in his own drawer and to know it exists**, which is the only thing he is entitled to. **At the door, alone, Sore repeats his Chapter 14 position word for word — he still will not say he did not sign it — and puts the one question he can support, which is a question and which Tomas cannot use: did the Crown withdraw a commission the Bellwrights' Measure granted, or did it withdraw a name?** The chapter records that Sore also warned him on the morning of the nineteenth and that the Measure will not put a man's survival and a professional finding in the same place in his head.

## Chapter 0047 — The Line Under the Yard

Day 27, full day, **Tomas** with **Oren** and **Jerm**. The authority to open the yard exists because **Oren enclosed a fair copy of the watch gate book's entries for the fifteenth with his return of the seventeenth**, and his own three sentences about a damp line under a bellmaker's floor are why a district sent a schedule. The three decide separately — Oren that the watch will attend, admit nobody, not obstruct, and not take part in the finding; Jerm that he will write out in advance what he expects to see at each hour; **Tomas that the floor is opened in daylight in front of the ward, because a record of an event made in an empty yard is the Crown's account of his yard** — and he prices the cost before the act, naming the bench-books, the forty people, and the **Crown lock register that echoes a name in a book of his.** **Oren refuses, in the lane in front of Collis, the district's requirement that the watch say whether any bell is bound to a claim and by whom**, and Collis refuses to certify the refusal, in her own return. The **Crown wax is broken at the Crown's own request**; the floor comes up at the fourth hour; the books come out into daylight; at about the seventh hour the line is lying open in a trench four feet deep and nine feet long **and does not go toward the millrace — it turns under the front of the shop and goes out under the wall facing the north road.** A bearing is taken and **read out in a lane in front of about forty people.** **Nobody says what it means. Oren does not write it. Jerm enters that he did not write it and why.** The floor is laid again and a bar of the Bellwrights' own ash is laid across the damp strip. **Crake applies in writing for the millrace bridge bell** — a properly made paper with a keeper, a limit and a return date — and **Oren grants it in the lane in six lines before about thirty people, naming Jerm, limiting it to the north gate for one morning, and entering his own reason: that a watch which tells the Crown it has no instrument is handing the Crown a reason to invent one.** Collis's objection is entered before the grant, not after. **The district copyist rides out at about the eleventh hour having never in nineteen years put a bearing in a finding, and tells Tomas at the gate, and Tomas writes four words: *He said it to me. He wanted it said.***

## Chapter 0048 — First Light

Day 28, dawn into morning, **Tomas** with the ward. **Before light, Gib Hask's six men take ninety feet of Crown backfill out of the low-turn culvert with a bar and a spade**, put it on the ward side and take none of it away; he comes to Tomas's gate and says he has done it for Renn's gate and not for the round, and Tomas tells him that sixty men at a flood gate is going to help somebody fill in a Crown form, and Hask says write it down. **Renn's gate comes up at about twenty past five, hard, on the first pull, and about sixty people watch.** In the yard Tomas **withdraws the water claim** — the ward board's copy and Renn's oilskin-board line — and **burns both in the ash-pit in the dark**, because a paper you keep is a paper somebody finds in nine years; Renn holds his sentence and agrees in advance to be contradicted by his own bell. **Wenna Cray withdraws in writing at about a quarter past five, in her own words, before the bell is sounded and without knowing whether it will be**, and her ground is that her four clauses were the ward board and two doors, and that a Crown officer with a spade asked her yesterday which bell was bound and she would not say and he did not need to, and that a duty she cannot stand behind is a signature. **The count is one.** Tomas rings once at about twenty minutes to five and the words are on the page entire: the known facts, the unknown facts, his own duty, **that the bell is barred by the Measure on the twenty-sixth and he is ringing it anyway and nobody made him**, the keeper, the count of one, his own name, and that it is too bright and it repeats within eight feet of a wall and he cannot make it stop. **It tells nobody what to do.** Jerm writes the bar beside the hour because the maker asked him to. **Jerm sounds the millrace bridge bell twice at the north gate at the sixth hour under the lawful grant**, and the two sounds are ninety seconds apart. The Crown comes up the west road and the half-closure is a bar, a chain and a man with a lamp; **a wagon goes over the bank at the mill narrows and takes the top four of Alis Marrow's mother's steps into the water.** The ward does four things in four columns: **nine moved, twenty-one stayed, eleven children in eight other people's doors, four doors refused.** **Foreman Crane asks Tamsin Rook at a quarter past five, in his own words, and she walks her whole route and rings once at the landing door and stands there while he says his own words in his own voice, and she does not ring again, and does not ring past the low drain gate, and cannot hear it and asks nobody.** Nell gets **thirty-one children off the landing in forty minutes** from a slate she has had since the fourteenth day. Oren holds the north gate from the sixth hour to the ninth and does not put two men in a lane. **House 114: Absalom Teage produces his own Crown return of the twenty-second in his own hand, the district's file says the boy was delivered on the twenty-sixth, the list says he is here, the cart did not come, and a Crown officer of twenty-six will not contradict his own district in a lane.** Tomas is four streets away and hears about it afterwards and does not improve it. **Rudd attends at the ninth hour with the four things, finds that the third was discharged the day before by a Crown surveyor who was not asked to, and reads out a district finding that the ward's bells are unaccounted for. Tomas stands up and certifies his own words as the Crown's evidence against himself, asks for the bar to stand beside the hour, and has the district's sentence read three times by a copyist with a cough.** The chapter ends on Crake's own answer to the two-line form, filed that afternoon and not seen in Kells until the twenty-ninth: the ward co-operated, no violence was committed on any Crown officer, and the bells are unaccounted for, which is a different question and is not disorder, *and I have put the two on the same piece of paper because the office has given me one piece of paper.*

## Chapter 0049 — What Burns

Day 28, morning into evening, **Tomas** with Oren and Nell. At the tenth hour **Rudd produces a fifth schedule**: the workshop's records are Crown material within the L-4 matter, are to be taken and copied, and **the originals are to be destroyed in the yard in daylight in the presence of the ward with the hour entered by a copyist.** Rudd says four words about it in a lane and they are on the page: *It is a form. It is a schedule. It is right.* Four Crown officers take **six people** between the tenth hour and the second hour of the afternoon, and **the watch's only part is that Oren writes the record of each arrest in his own hand — the ground, the hour, the place, the officer, the name, and whether the person was told and whether a reason was given — and gives each of them a copy, because Rudd asks him for it and says he could produce six arrests without asking a question of the watch.** The six and their grounds: **Gib Hask** under the Crown works order, for ninety feet of backfill taken out at night; **Mrs Wilde of 108** as the household of record on a line in the *Witnessed by* column the district cannot produce a living witness to; **Wenna Cray**, entered by the watch captain on the seventeenth for public standing and on the twenty-eighth for withdrawing a duty in public; **Ivo Prentice**, entered on the same list for a petition and for stating in public that the Crown's cart was still across the culvert; **Louisa Tace of 44**, a house never marked and never visited, entered because she put the column question at her own door; and **Absalom Teage of 114**, whose own return saved his son three hours earlier. **House 108 is marked again and no child is taken, and Collis asks the household to countersign the objection and not the answer, and the household countersigns the objection** — a surveyor's name in the column beside a dead woman's, for the first time. The six names are read out in the afternoon and **all six came off the district's amended return of the seventeenth in Oren's own hand**, and Oren writes the two-paragraph statement he has never taken out. At the half past two the Crown party goes up the lane with about a hundred and forty people behind them; Tomas gives up the L-4 papers himself, naming each one aloud; **the copyist comes to the bench-books, and one of them is for Reckoning 619, and the district copyist says out loud that he has never had a schedule with a year in it in it before.** **The bench-book for Reckoning 619 is burned in the ash-pit in three goes at the third hour, in daylight, in front of about a hundred and forty people, with the hour and the page numbers entered.** **Tomas rings nothing and asks nobody**, and gives Mercy Slee the reason in a lane — *a bell cannot stop a fire, and a bellmaker who thinks it can has stopped being a maker* — and writes the day's three-column record in thirty-one lines and gives it away. **He tears a leaf out of the running bench-book and gives it to Bevin Sarr, who has refused three times to be a witness and is on no list, and Sarr's answer is: *not one word to anybody, and if anybody comes and asks me, I have never had it.*** A Crown officer's armful of L-4 papers goes down on the bench on top of the watch's open duplicate register and **Sarr lifts the page clear and hands it to Nell Fenn**, and it is the surviving register fragment. The yard is sealed at the fifth hour with a district wax and **Oren's watch mark under it** — nineteen words, and no opinion. The millrace bridge bell comes off the gate bracket and goes back up the hill and is sounded once on the way by Jerm, unasked. The trial handbell is produced unwrapped in the market court and identified by Tomas, who produces his own receipt in a lane, and Collis puts it in her return unasked. **Tomas is taken at the sixth hour and asks for one thing, which is to see his custody receipt, and reads the margin aloud in front of about sixty people, and the copyist's initial is still not agreement.** The last thing out of the yard was a page.

## Chapter 0050 — Before Dawn

Day 33, before dawn, **Tomas**. Days 29 to 32 are compressed in the opening, in the order written, with the hours: taken to a district building at Fourmile on the night of the twenty-eighth; **Mrs Wilde released on the thirtieth** because the district cannot hold a household of record on a witness column for a second day longer without a witness and Collis refuses to certify her line again, **and the other five before the district on the same day and released together on the thirty-first**; five men and one woman released without charge on the thirty-first on a single sheet in **Crake's own hand**, Tomas released the same day and in Kells by the second; **Crake relieved of the commission on the thirtieth for recording disorder and co-operation on one paper** and moved to a works office, and nobody in Kells will ever know; **the Crown cart off the culvert on the thirty-first at about the eleventh hour** and the backfill back in on the **thirty-second** under a works order signed by a foreman who had been signing them for nineteen years, uncompacted and unchecked; **Foreman Brann's gang's book entry on the thirty-second that the ground part of the Day 8 plan came up with the works order from Fourmile on a folded third sheet in a hand that is not a road hand**, with the nineteenth's entries still illegible; and **Nell Fenn spending the nine *not returned* and six *accompanying* lines on the thirtieth as a checkable question by the north post, the question going up on the thirty-first, and the district's two-paragraph reply on the thirty-second** — it will answer any line in an officer's own name, it regrets calling a schedule non-response, and it notes that the respondent is a person of sixteen of the household of line twenty-two and does not propose to ask why — **pinned on the ward board beside the pinned schedule, and no household on any of the fifteen lines has yet come to ask.** At about the third hour of the morning of the thirty-third a **custody review** is held at the watch house, on Oren's four-word note, because **District Officer Marten Cope wrote on the second that the district cannot answer whether any bell in this ward is joined to any line, wire or ground and needs the only instrument in the ward whose manner of working is recorded** — which is the reason Oren arranged in Chapter 3 and the last chance to make it. **Tamsin Rook unwraps her own bell and sets it on the cloth; Tomas reads the wear from a distance and does not touch it; nothing is rung.** The comparison shows **a technique and not a person**: the courier bell's crown re-cut inside by a chisel about a sixteenth of an inch with a **riffle file** mark at four points, and the fixed carrier's coupling cut nearly through with a copper jumper across it, both at two removes from a Crown pattern, both ordinary work, both nameless — and the service ticket's ***answered in office, L-4 ward roll, per local practice*** with the mark **OFF. 411** is read out again with **no person behind it and none this month.** Collis's return **withdraws the district's finding that the ward's bells are unaccounted for and makes no finding against the watch or any person.** **Oren releases the same fixed stripped carrier to Tomas in its mounting frame, bolts released only for transport, the frame in its reinforced padded cradle, no clapper and none to be furnished, four clauses and a fifth that Tomas asks for and Ferrow writes: that the receipt does not make the holder a keeper of any route and that no answer given to any signal on or by the object may be entered as the answer of a household of this ward.** **Tamsin gives back the Day 24 request with one line on the outside of the fold — no, no reason, and you did not press me once — and travels with him on a slate Mercy Slee wrote, which neither of them wrote first, which puts a leash on both of them, and which she read to each of them separately**, and she says out loud in a lane that **a warning does not require her to obey him.** The Vale Bellhouse sign hangs crooked over a locked door with the district's wax on it and the watch captain's mark under it. On the river road she rings the handbell **once** at about the seventh hour, waits, and gets an answer **from Ocklebridge, a village of about two hundred that has not yet heard the Crown's order**, and does not ring again. **Nobody in this volume asks her for a reason.** His day-book ends on the two questions: who taught a Crown office to turn one clerk's answer into a whole settlement's consent, and where are the names being sent when they are registered.
