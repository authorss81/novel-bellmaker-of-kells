# Character State After Batch 0003

## Tomas Vale

Tomas is 32, still the practical third-generation bellmaker and keeper of the Vale Bellhouse. He is at the **first edge of Stage 1 (Near Toll)** and gained **no power at all** in Chapters 21–30. **No chapter in the batch contains a perception of any kind**: he does not reach for one, and he is not described as reaching for one, and in Chapter 25 the only thing on the page is that he writes four words in his own day-book about the pen a summary page is written in. The claim is not near him but in a filing office in the district town. He identifies no source, emotion, copied answer, official, or future event. **The midpoint reversal is a documentary and civic discovery, and the only craft he does in the batch is ordinary: taking a counter bell up a quarter inch and dressing it.** Costs: a lawful open seizure of everything in his yard, a name he wrote into a Crown document without asking, the L-4 working papers taken out of the survey store, the chandler's work gone to Miremarket, the miller's bell still away, eleven days of delay on a wheelwright's bell, and three children out of a ward whose only warning he was the keeper of.

His movement is real and it is measured in things he did not do. He did not contest the seizure of his own bell. He did not accept the Crown's merged page to buy it back. He stopped trying to change the custody and changed the **description** instead, which is the correct use of a smaller power. He then made the batch's central mistake in the correct register: he wrote **Nell Fenn's name** into a Crown custody receipt because a record that will not say a name is a record that can be used against anybody, and that argument was true and it said nothing at all about a girl who had not been asked. He told her the same afternoon because he had promised himself in writing that he would. He published a finding and **declined to speak first at his own publication**, and refused four times in one afternoon to tell a market court what the ward should do. He accepted that he has no standing to know in advance whether Tamsin will answer him, and wrote it down rather than asking for a way round it. He is at the end of the batch with no instrument, no income, one customer's bell on his bench, and a name the Crown has attached to a public accusation.

## Tamsin Rook

Tamsin's age is not on the page anywhere and her career in bells is ending inside the batch. She could not follow the argument at the board on Day 11, so she read the paper, and reading the paper is what found the reversal. She handed Tomas the arithmetic and refused him a name, twice — once for the office, once for herself, because a name would turn a practice into a grievance two men can argue about. She retained her handbell throughout and **never rang it for any public purpose in this batch.** She logged the reach at seven points on Day 13 and found it does not stop anywhere it thins out, so **she cannot find where her own rule ends and the alteration begins**, and she therefore reduced her offer to four terms: paper on foot at doors, the bell only at a door that asks and names what it wants repeated, the bell as nothing more than a *call to come to a door*, and no repeating for anybody, with no reason required from her and no right to ask. Her hearing has taken the **note** out of her left ear, so she can ring and feel and see but cannot carry, and **Chapter 29 states that no one in Kells is going to be told, because a courier who says her note is gone is a courier whose bell is free. She keeps it from Jerm through the seven-point walk — he is her witness and is on the slate, and he is never told what is wrong — and then tells Tomas alone at the low drain gate at the fourth hour, as the first of three things given for free: *my hearing is worse than I have told anybody in this town except you.* Nobody else in Kells is told.** The ferry company has withdrawn the towpath notice; the route's nine coppers a month are gone; she has turned down two paid runs.

Her relationship with Tomas is now a boundary he can be held to in writing, plus one sentence she will not give him: *I will answer a request and refuse a request, and you will not be able to tell in advance which.* He wrote that he has **no standing to know**, in his own hand, and showed it to her, and she said it would do. She has also told him, to his face, that doing the right thing once has not made him humble, it has made him out of practice, and that it wears off.

## Oren Pike

Oren's age is not on the page anywhere and this batch cost him the thing he has spent eleven days building. He conceded the seizure on the record because he did not have the votes and would not put two of his men in a lane in the dark — and he entered that **he chose the hour**, so that the act belonged to him rather than to a night. He recorded three removals separately by household with the hour and the household's own answer, had them countersigned by a surveyor where a surveyor would countersign them, and then wrote that he had two men, a warrant and **seventy-odd people in the lane behind him** and did not put them between children and a lawful order, and why. He wrote the memorandum denying his own gate book's standing as consent, in his own hand, and read it in open court twice. He named the men who obeyed and the man who refused and exonerated neither by reflex. He refused a district appointment and insisted on refusing it in the open. He has named in writing that he has not read the gate book for the night of the Hush and will not let any man of his watch read it aloud.

He has also become less safe. Halden has told him the district office will hear about him by the end of the week, and Oren has answered that he would rather the district hear about him from a bridge post than from a file. He is not Tomas's protector and has not pretended to be. He is a captain who chose an hour, wrote his own name into the worst line in the book, and knows exactly what the arithmetic cost.

## Nell Fenn

Nell is 16 and is now the person the ward actually acts through. She counted the schedule's marks on the ward board and found that every household the Crown took a child from sits on a line reading *not returned* or *accompanying*, and every other marked door does not. She got one precise question into a filing room and one answer out of it — the *Witnessed by* column, empty on all four hundred and six Kells returns — and then **burned the slate in the washhouse fire** after reading it to Mercy Slee, because the one document that would have made it evidence is about her own brother and father. She refused the Crown's demand for the ward board's box and watched eleven people refuse it one at a time. She published the ward's three questions to a hundred and twenty people and, when Tomas would not, read them herself. Three households then withdrew from the board's arrangements because being visible is a cost, and one of them left a written reason on the slate to be left on the record.

She has also been put on a Crown page by Tomas, and has told him he has never once asked her for anything that was not already written. **Nobody has been taken from the Fenn household; their door was marked and the count on the page is six against a household of two.** She still holds the original fragment and the copy, and she is still the only person in Kells who can say no and know exactly what it costs. **On Day 14 she told Tomas in advance that he will not be told which two people she is going to ask whether they will stand beside her before she says the word *children* out loud, because if he is told first he will turn up — and she said it in the open rather than asking him not to be told, which is a thing she had already learned she could not do. Her slate carries thirty-one households with somebody under seventeen in them, of which three lost a child on Day 10.**

## Sabin Fell

Sabin is 61 and has stopped being able to protect Tomas and has said so twice, in the yard, in his own words. On Day 10 he told him the trade will call the taking a judgment on the Bellwrights’ name within a fortnight, that four houses in the Measure will say a man who loses a bell and loses a child in a morning should not hold a licence, that two of them will say it in the open, and that one of them is Iven Sore and will be right. On the evening of Day 14, with the district’s order for the Measure’s road map falling due at the eleventh hour of Day 15, he came down the lane, looked at a wheelwright’s bell on a bench and said nothing about it, and then said the thing he had come to say: that **the fifty-three recorded ward withdrawals on the Measure’s road map — every one of them a refusal and not an agreement — is going into a district file with a Crown seal on it, and that in six months a clerk in Fourmile will read it as a list of wards that do not answer, and the next man who comes up this lane with a form will have read it, and he will be right about his facts.** (The fifty-three are withdrawals. The fifty-three **years** are Sabin's own craft, and the two numbers must never be conflated.) **He asked Tomas to write it down in his own words, and asked not to be in the room when Tomas decides what to do with it.** He has still not named what he carried past the gate in the Hush.

## Halden Crake

Halden is the Kells deputy reeve under the Crown inspection commission and **has not become a villain**. He is on the page in six chapters of this batch — 21, 22, 24, 25, 27 and 30 — and each time he is colder and more precise than the last: on Day 11 he read his own answer to Oren’s refusal of the district appointment out in full in open court and said plainly that the district office would hear about Oren by the end of the week; on Day 12 he said he would file Collis’s three sentences with his report *because* they are the four words a commission needs in order to say that the round was not properly constituted, and Oren told him to file that beside it; and on Day 14 he said one sentence into a gap in the court proceedings and then went and stood in the lane where he could be seen standing there. **The escalation itself is the commission’s, not his personally: three notices were nailed to the ward board at eight o’clock on the evening of Day 13, and a fourth, shorter summons was posted before the eighth hour on the morning of Day 14, and his name is on none of them. Do not count the escalations in hours or days; the chapters give a posting time, not a span.** He waited twenty minutes before speaking in the custody hearing because waiting was a choice; he read the operative words himself rather than have them read for him; he made the seizure happen in the open and then said the reason plainly; he took children off three households on the strength of marks his own office had made; and when Gib Hask put the buried line to the ward in a public street it was a twenty-six-year-old Crown officer who answered him, and Oren chose not to write that down; and he filed Collis's three sentences — the first three of the four statements she entered — alongside his own line that he would rather have **nine houses and a clean return** than **forty-one houses and a clerk in an office guessing**. He has said the word *bridge* four times in one sentence about the millrace bell and has not yet applied. He offered Oren a district appointment behind his back and has told him the district office will hear about it. **He is not a fool and has never been one, and Oren's own judgement at the foot of Chapter 24 is that the man will be more dangerous in four days than he has been in eleven.**

## Rhea Collis

Surveyor Rhea Collis is of the northern district, her age is not stated on the page, and by the end of the batch she is a person rather than an appointment. She arrived on Day 8 eleven days off the north road. She certified the Day 10 round **on the schedule**, having said at the gate in the open that she would not seal a visit conducted against a roll she had not been shown. She was conducted to nine of the twenty-two marked doors and **did not see the other thirteen marked at all**, which she entered as the first of her four statements. She watched three children taken. She countersigned **one** line of the removal record — the one about a household whose entered response records a return — and refused the other two because she cannot certify a child's age. She put four statements and five refusals into the market court record in full, including that she **is not the person named on the withdrawn service entry and will not be made into him.** She refused Tomas a paragraph and gave him three sentences, and explained exactly what her words cost the next surveyor. In a doorway, unofficially, she said that a page like the schedule does not appear by itself and that an office does not enter pages, and she declined to write it down, and Tomas did not write it down. She is going to Fourmile at the end of the week with a return that says nine houses.

## Iven Sore

Iven Sore is the living master of the Bellwrights' Measure and the name on the withdrawn docket, and he has not appeared in person in this batch. He is present in the volume as a man who will act: Sabin has said on the page that Sore may discharge his challenge, and that he will want a room, a witness and a day, and that Tomas should give it to him without asking why. His challenge is live, and only partly discharged: the material inspection and the published withdrawal procedure are satisfied, and the question he refused to answer — whether he signed the withdrawn service entry — is still open. **The genuine-or-borrowed question stays open and must not be settled by assumption.**

## Ansel Stroud

**Clerk Ansel Stroud** is new to the prose in this batch and is the most important new face in it. He is a Crown filing clerk, younger than Nell expected, with ink to the second knuckle. On the night of Day 9 he told Oren, unwitnessed, that he has merged divided answers into single ones for eleven years because a ward that cannot make up its mind is read as a ward that will not answer. On Day 12 he told Nell, in a filing room with the door shut, that the Crown form has a **Witnessed by** column, empty on every Kells return, that he has filed four hundred and six in eleven years, and that a summary form drops the reason. On Day 13 he sat behind a public Crown counter that Oren compelled into existence with one sentence, and wrote two sentences in the margin of a household's own line in his own name and initialled them. On Day 14 he filed the ward's three positions as a single response and asked to read it both ways so the room could hear what it became. **He is frightened, competent, useful, and the mechanism's ordinary face, and he is the man who will be asked who let him do it.**

## The Low Ward and town figures after this batch

- **Absalom Teage**, carter, of house 114, lost a son of nine on Day 10. He produced the slate his household held from the posting day, heard his wife say *He is not on your paper*, and on Day 13 used the three questions at a public Crown counter and got a Crown clerk to write *No person in this house was asked* in a margin in his own name. His wife asked whether it was a win and was told it was a witness.
- **Sena Dunn** lost a child of eleven on Day 10, after agreeing to a public route and withdrawing in open court. Her name is in no book in the town; the schedule line names nobody; she asked *which child* four times.
- **Mercy Slee**, of house 119, has a boy of nine and is on no roll. She refused to be on any list, told Nell the correct use of a true record might be to leave a house off it, watched Nell burn the slate, and is the **named co-holder of the ward's copy** of the published page.
- **Gib Hask**, gate-porter and rope-maker, is now the standing Unrung position: the copper under the ward was laid without consent, a town that learns to argue with paper will not watch the ground, and he has six men and a bar and has asked a court to tell him to his face whether it objects. Nobody has. He is treated seriously and he is not safe.
- **Ivo Prentice**, of the ferry road, petitioned with eight others in four households to be put on a Crown line, because only a Crown order moves the cart at the culvert, and said in open court that he would not be ashamed of it in front of eleven witnesses.
- **The Cafferys and the Oakes** left the ward overnight on Day 12, houses 122 and 136, with a churn, a box of papers, two beds and a chest. They are gone and neither returns in this batch.
- **Crown Officer Ambrose Rudd** objects, in terms, that a procedure for disputing returns makes a ward's houses notable and therefore reduces the number of questions a ward is asked. He is right, and Oren had him read out twice.
- **The market court clerk, Ferrow**, stopped reading the schedule at the ruling because readers stop at rulings, has looked under the ruling twice, has been spoken to for four minutes about nothing by a Crown man, and is frightened. He also wrote down, on Day 14, that the market court had produced its keeper and not its bell and had surrendered nothing.
- **The market court usher**, keeper of the unbound iron call bell, stood up on Day 14, which was not a thing she did, and said she had three things to say and would then sit down because she had three and would rather use them well. She gave the three facts of her bell in open court — iron, leather handle, striker on a thong, no wire, no groove in the crown, no wax — with Jerm's Day 8 inspection behind her, and refused to have the bell described, exhibited and carried by an office that has not put a hand on it. She is the batch’s most economical witness and she carries no Crown paper at all.
- **The market court usher** is still the keeper of the unbound iron call bell that the commission has now asked the court to produce, and the count the bell made on Day 8 was **three** answers, one of them a refusal.
- **Watchmen Ardle and Cask** did the Day 10 seizure on Oren's instruction. Cask barred the gate without authority, the wagon went through the ford, and he reported the woman on the tailboard.
- **The unnamed wheelwright** brought his counter bell back after eleven days, refused Miremarket, and paid the same price.

## Relationship and civic movement

Tomas and Tamsin have a working boundary in writing plus one sentence she will not give him, and no romance and no binding bell. Oren and Tomas are a practical alliance that is not trust and not forgiveness and now has two signed sentences in it that neither of them wanted. Nell and Tomas are a relationship in which she corrects him, he takes it, and he has finally given her something to correct him about. Sabin has given instruction and refused protection and has forecast the trade's verdict. The commission has escalated over the ward's head, with the originating-workshop declaration aimed at Tomas's licence rather than at the town. The town has published a checkable procedure, made its page co-held, and filed a divided answer that a clerk has read both ways in a full room. And it has a question about its own children that it could not say out loud on the fourteenth day.
