## Tomas Vale

**Tomas is 32.** His own number is in Sabin Fell's mouth in Volume 01 and is not restated in this batch, and no other number for him exists on any page.

**He is at the first edge of Stage 1 (Near Toll) and gained nothing in this batch, and this is a hard boundary rather than an accident.** He attempts no perception in any of the ten chapters and is not described as reaching for one. **He rings no bell in any of Chapters 51–60.** He has no way of knowing where anything landed and does not claim to have one. On the fortieth, in **Chapter 56**, he wrote in his day-book that he came four days looking for a man in a room with a plan in it and found **a column and a stamp and a habit**, and that **nobody taught it — it only had to be convenient, and then it had to be printed, and then it had to be stamped.** And on the forty-second, in **Chapter 58**, at the Fourmile office, he wrote that **he learned nothing about how an office gets into a bell.** **The two sentences are from two chapters on two days and the state layer once merged them; the second is the batch's boundary.**

**What he did in this batch.** He left the barrow outside every village and every lane mouth and made the bolts fast at each place and entered the hour and the place. He went into a lane and asked a household to be a witness to itself and was refused, and recorded that he had known it would not work and did it anyway. He got one piece of paper out of a drawer by asking for it instead of asking for a story, and accepted the condition attached to it. He was told he was not thanked and did not expect to be. He was refused at a water bell before he had finished his first sentence and went back up the lane. He was told the exact thing Sabin Fell had told him in a room over a fish market, by a stranger who had never heard of Sabin Fell, and the sentence is the same one in both mouths. He was refused a page of a book and promised to come back with a reason in writing and did. He was woken at the fifth hour in an inn yard by a page a woman had written without him and did not say he had told them. He asked a district officer four questions and did not argue with a single one of the answers, and he wrote the four in four columns with the hour against each and **did not merge them, including himself.** He asked a road for permission in three questions in the same words and was refused by one person and answered by another and **the refusal is what chose the road.** He did not ask Tamsin Rook whether she would come, and wrote down that he did not ask, and gave the reason, and the reason was fear.

**What he owns at the end of this batch:** a frame in a cradle on a boat; a receipt in five clauses; a dated copy of a Crown form and a signature; a road sheet with a panel missing; four sheets from a district office; a day-book; a route fragment; a copy of the Measure's road-map correction; **and a loose leaf in a smith's book that he has put back and told nobody about.**

**What he has lost:** the idea that his own fifth clause governs him — *a receipt is not a permission*; the belief that being right is the beginning of an exchange; a night's sleep, paid for by somebody else; the comfort of a man who has a room with a person in it to ask; and, he wrote it himself, **the movement toward being useful and the movement away from the right to speak for anybody are the same movement and are not a good one.**

## Tamsin Rook

**Her age is not on the page and has not been supplied in this batch, in the narrator's voice or in anybody's mouth.**

**She rang nothing in Chapters 51–60.** Her handbell was in her bag the whole batch, and the chapter says it was the heaviest thing she carried.

**Her hearing loss is permanent and it is never a subject.** No person asks her for a reason at any hour in this batch. She reads shoulders and hands rather than mouths, and no chapter mentions that she cannot follow a conversation across a room as a fault; it is used as a professional fact — she is the one who can see a gap in a sheet of paper at a counter while a man is looking at a tide table.

**What she did in this batch.** She walked the towpath and asked three carriers about paper and got the working distinction of the volume out of them: **a carrier's book carries who carried a thing and how far, and may not carry the owner, a debt, or a price, and people are not parcels and always were not.** She said her own reason for the road out loud on a rise and it is not about Tomas: a boy of eight called Jory Fenn, taken in the round before the Crown notice, whose only papers are a schedule line and a household fragment with a fourth line cut away, and **a name that is on a roll in a building four counties from here is on a road somewhere, and the road is the only place anybody looks, and looking is what she is for.** She read the fourth panel of a road sheet and then the fifth and found the part that says where an answer goes gone from every copy, and said so at a counter before anybody else in the batch noticed, and got a better answer out of a map-seller than Tomas would have got because she was a woman plainly asking about paper and he was a man with a bell in a cradle. She gave him the method: **ask what a document is, not who is on it, and then make the clerk put the answer in a document with his own name at the bottom of it.** She sat up all night in a wagon yard with her back against a wheel, produced a Crown object for a lawful request, read four clauses of a receipt out loud to eleven people, entered a refusal of a name in an inn's yard book, and **did not wake the man she was travelling with and has never given a reason for that and nobody has asked her for one.** She put her slate in the water at a sluice so that nothing she had written would be in her bag. She put the same three questions to nine doors in the same words and entered **eight refusals as eight refusals and not as eight silences**, which is Nell Fenn's practice and which she learned in a market court in a different month. She refused the leash twice, correctly, and he asked once each time and did not ask again; **on the third use, in Chapter 54 on the thirty-eighth, she asked him to stay out of a shop while she asked about paper, in the same terms, and he did not come in. The leash is used three times in this batch — two refusals of him and one of her — and neither of them has been refused twice.**

**Her own line about it, in Chapter 57, said to a wagon wheel and not to him:** *I am very good at this. That is the part I have been sitting here for four hours with. I have been good at this for eleven years and I have never once enjoyed it and I have never told anybody that. Being good at it is not the same as liking it, and a person who cannot tell the difference will do it at two in the morning for four years and be proud and will not know what it has cost, and that person is me, and that is why I have got a bag on my belt and not a slate.*

**Her five words in her own column in Chapter 60: *As far as it goes.*** *As far as it goes* is five words. That is the first line of Mercy Slee's slate. It is not a promise and it is not a refusal and she did not improve it.

## Marta Vell

**Marta Vell keeps the water bell at Ocklebridge. She is about sixty on Tamsin Rook's word and has no other age on any page, and she has no surname on any page anybody has ever seen. She has rung that bell every morning for thirty years.** On the thirty-fifth she rang it at about the seventh hour, four strokes, unhurried, and at about the fourth hour of the afternoon she was told by a stranger that her bell is entered on a schedule in a building in Fourmile as a bell of her ward with nothing at all in the field for a keeper.

**What she did.** She told him to go up the lane before he had finished his first sentence, and she was right, and he went. She let him read the receipt's fifth clause out loud in a yard and then said he had come down there to read her a piece of paper, and he said yes. He told her afterwards that he had read that clause for two days as a reason not to tell her, and she said: *You put them together because you did not want to come down here. And now you have come down here.* **She would not look at anything and would not read anything.** He asked her instead to write down, in her own hand, with the hour, what she does every morning and who has ever asked her to ring it. **She wrote it on a slate on her own step in about eleven minutes, turned it round and held it against her own body, and he did not read it, and she will not show it to him until she decides to.** It took her a week to decide and **the week is hers to ask for.** She gave him one sentence off it and no more: *A form that has no field in it for the only person in the entry is a form that is not about the person.* **The first name written on that page is not his, and she will not give it for nine days.** Nine days from the thirty-fifth is the forty-fourth, **which is Chapter 60's day, and no chapter in this batch collects it. It is owed, and because the batch has moved on the term has lapsed uncollected: the lateness is his and not her breach, and she is not obliged to give the name because a man arrived late.**

## Marten Cope

**Marten Cope is District Officer of the Crown district renewal office at Fourmile.** His age is not on the page and is not supplied in this batch. He is **permitted, useful, and not a door, and he did not open one.**

**On the forty-second he answered three of Tomas Vale's four questions in writing, in his own name, within the hour, and refused the fourth in writing, in his own name, with a reason.** The three answers: **the Ocklebridge water bell is entered on the district's sheet of water bells for the north river with the keeper's field empty, and the reason the field is empty is that the district's form has four fields and none of them fits a water bell, and he has entered a marginal note on the line asking for an instrument and has entered that in his own name;** **the office holds four returns in one file, from four districts, over eleven years, all four bearing at the signature line the same hand, described by him as leaning forward with a hesitating descender that comes back and a d and a g that stop and start in the same place, and he refuses to describe the households;** and **this office transmits nothing, he has put the question to his district twice in writing and had no answer to either, and he will not speculate in a document because a document is a thing people believe.**

**The refusal, and the whole of its reason:** that this office's instruments require a return, that **a roll is not a return**, that to answer properly he would have to write down in a document that leaves his building that the largest instrument the office holds upon one route is not one of the things the office's own forms recognise; that **the office has no instrument for a roll, none, and he asked for one in the ninth year and was refused and did not ask again**; and that **he is not yet willing to say it in a document that goes to a district office, and he is not going to pretend to a braver reason than that.**

**He then did the one thing nobody has ever done at that office.** Tomas asked for the refusal to be **filed where a person can ask for it**, and Cope filed it in the office's **book of refusals**, which has about two hundred and forty entries in eleven years and in which no person has ever before asked to see one. He recorded in the same sheet that he is the officer who stopped the practice of leaving the requester's name out of that book, four years ago, for a reason he cannot account for and has never asked about.

**What is not changed about him.** His letter of the thirty-second, the custody review of the thirty-third, the filing of Collis's withdrawal, and the release of the object to Tomas are his acts and **he repudiates none of them in this chapter.** He says the withdrawal of the district's finding of the twenty-eighth was **not his** and was a surveyor's, done on her own paper, and that he will not stand in another man's clean water. And he says the withdrawal **is not a win: it is a document that says his district was wrong about a set of bells, there are about nine hundred bells in this district, and nobody has asked for it.** **Nobody has asked for it and no state file may report it asked for.**

## Hesk Rann

**A form-writer.** Lives above a sluice, on the towpath four miles on from the stage at about eleven miles. **No age is on the page and none may be supplied.** He has been filling in Crown forms for people who cannot write for about **nine years**, about **nine hundred** of them, and **he does not charge.** He has **never once been asked** to, by anybody, in nine years. He is not Crown, not a clerk, and not cruel. He came to the lane of the household of six four times a year for six years and knew that husband's step. He also carries a household's answer between the district and the brother-in-law two counties over **twice a year**, and the brother-in-law does not know he is signing, and **asked in the second year only whether the paper wanted him to say something, and was told no, it wants the answer you gave last year, and there is a line to confirm it on.** The brother-in-law **cannot remember agreeing to any of it** and last described the paper as *a thing about a gate*.

**He is not a villain and is not converted.** He is competent at a thing nobody defined, and he is owed nothing by law, and he told Tamsin Rook all of it at a gate in about four minutes **because she asked him nine questions and never told him a story, and because a man with a Crown bell is coming up the road in about four days and he would like a record of the conversation with a date on it in case anybody comes looking for a reason.** He refused to sign her refusal, and asked her to write down that he asked and she did not, and then declined to be asked to write down that he would rather have been one of her eight.

**His hand:** forward slope, hesitating descender, the downstroke of the d and of the g stopping and starting in the same place, the same in every one of them. It is on the current year's form at the door of the household of six, on Amos Trewin's bill of the spring for about nine and six, and on four returns in a file in a building in Fourmile. **No law anywhere says a man may not be the hand on both kinds of paper.**

## Amos Trewin

**A smith past the county line, at about the sixth mile.** No age on the page. **He is not a master of the Bellwrights' Measure, has never been in that house, and cannot certify anything at all — not a bell, not a staple, not a hinge.** He will not cast anything and has not cast a bell in about nineteen years and says so before he is asked. He sells metal and **keeps a book**, and the two are the same thing, and the ninth column is why.

**His book:** nine columns, running back forty years under a different name over the door, nine years of it readable, and a ninth column called the where-from holding the name of the place, the date and the man. He has kept a book every day for about nineteen years because a woman told him in a room what his metal did and did not raise her voice. He **refused to sell a page of it** — the households column — and gave his reason whole, and the reason is that the eighth and ninth columns together make a list of houses, and the book is the only copy, and he told Tomas Vale that the district does not need his book, **because the district made its own.** He quoted the craft law before Tomas knew he had heard Sabin Fell say it, and then asked him to say it again with a word changed. He keeps a bundle of bills because a bill is a legal paper, and one of them is Hesk Rann's.

**He is a colleague and not a rescuer, and he was not converted, and he still thinks a Crown store is *available* rather than merely expensive, and that is a professional opinion and he is entitled to it.**

Maud Prine does the writing. A brother-in-law left six years ago and has not been in the house since. No age is on the page for anybody in this household and none may be supplied.**

**What Maud Prine did.** She wrote the unsigned letter — the one that reached the Kells ward board inside a district rider's bundle of Crown papers on the second, was read by Mercy Slee on the third, and went up the road in a ward bag on the thirty-fifth with a bellmaker's name still on the bag list. She said no to Tomas Vale at the door for about four seconds and then let him read four paragraphs standing in the lane.
