# Character State After Batch 0004

## Tomas Vale

Tomas is 32, the practical third-generation bellmaker and keeper of the Vale Bellhouse, and he is **still at the first edge of Stage 1 (Near Toll) with no power gained in this batch.** **No chapter in Chapters 31–40 contains a perception of any kind.** He does not reach for one and is not described as reaching for one. He states the limit in a document a Crown deputy has signed: *I have no instrument. I can see a filled trench and I know where the Bellwrights' cut ran from my father's hand, and if there is a live line under this ward I cannot find it and if I find one I cannot tell whether it is live.*

**What he made.** One bell. Six and a half inches, cast on Day 19 out of the last of a half-barrel of his father's hearth metal, no wax, no groove, only his mark and the date. He records its condition the way he would record his father's: weight at the mouth and at the shoulder, the difference being his and known to be his, and two faults entered plainly — **it is too bright and he cannot reduce it, and it repeats within eight feet of a wall.** The witness matter is a bar of the ward's own drain grating, cut twice with a file in three places so a stranger can find it in ten years with a lamp. **The half-barrel is now empty and he can cast no second bell in that yard this year.**

**What he did with it, and this is the batch's whole shape.** He struck it on his own bench with a leather when the skin came off, twice more an hour apart, and once in the open in the middle of the yard after the ash-pit wall gave the note back, and rang it at the fifth hour of the afternoon to nobody's notice, and then rang it once, correctly, at the seventh hour, and it bought four things inside two hundred yards. **Six soundings in the yard and four in the market lane is what the ward's bell register says, and that register goes to the district on the twenty-fourth.** Then, on the same evening, because a woman with a box stood in a lane and asked him whether to send the box or the boy and he had nothing to offer, he carried it into a market lane and **rang it four times and asked nobody**, and about one hundred and eighty people emptied a lane in the dark, a carrier lost a handcart and load at thirty-six pounds, four stalls lost a market day, and **a nineteen-year-old Crown road hand he had written to on Day 15 and asked to stand where his words reached had her right wrist broken by the shoe of a handcart.** He stopped it himself, on the fish market step, at the fifth hour of the morning of Day 20, in front of about sixty people, without permission and without making anybody decide for him. He did not call it a false alarm. He recorded the cost in figures and words, **named Crane and Tamsin Rook in the record as the two whose refusal would have prevented a keeper at the mill end of the west road**, told the two keepers before the ward, and gave no explanation for four days.

**What he did right.** He asked Oren in writing for a bench-test exception and Oren narrowed it. He asked the ward board, in daylight, in front of about thirty people, for a piece of its own iron, having read the whole of the claim and the whole of the cost out first, and the board gave it and Mercy Slee wrote the gift and the date and the words and the cost into the ward's book. He said out loud in a lane with about twenty people in it that he would not ask anybody to close the lane and that Hask was right and he had no answer. He wrote the Bellwrights' name in a Crown return in his own words and refused to have a summary of himself. He found a line under his own floor and did not put it on the board. **On Day 21 he stood four feet from a wagon with a bell half a mile off and did not ring it, and said out loud, at the low gate, that the reason was not law and not courage: a bell cannot be made to say a thing it was not bound to say, and anything else would have been a version, and this ward already has a version with a Crown phrase in it that he never said.**

**His flaw, paid for.** He spent nine days learning to ask before the act and then did the one thing he was learning not to do, and the cost was a girl of nineteen's wrist and thirty-six pounds and a road he does not get back. He also put the answer he wanted at the bottom of a list for two days, and had to move her to the top and tell her so in writing with the hour on it before he asked her anything. **He is at the end of this batch with a bell of his own that is the wrong bell, a day-book with fifty-one checkable lines in it, four days of silence owed to a town, and a line of iron under his floor that nobody in Kells is allowed to say out loud.**

## Tamsin Rook

Tamsin's age is not on the page. **She rang nothing in Chapters 31–40.** Her four terms of Day 13 stand and she is the batch's central relationship object because she is the one thing Tomas cannot have.

**She was asked first.** Tomas had her fourth on his own list for two days, out of wanting, told her so in writing with the hour on it before he asked her anything, and asked. **She refused at once** and gave a reason her own fourth term does not require: a bell in a claim cannot be a bell that is only a call to come to a door, and her four terms are the only thing she has left that is hers, and if she took a public duty then the day somebody wanted her bell they would not have to argue with her about the route at all — they would have her name in his book next to a claim, and the route would be a thing she gave away for nothing in a bad afternoon. She said *no* before the reason, deliberately, because a no with the reason first is a different kind of no and he would use it. She wrote it, signed it, and let him copy it out for the record.

**On Day 19 she predicted the batch's disaster out loud**, before it happened: two careful keepers will draw down to two hundred yards, the bell is too bright and he has written that down, and on that lane there is a man with a Crown cart waiting to be proved right about Crown lines, and everything he is about to do will arrive at that man before he knows it has. She told him to write the limit inside the claim so the difficulty travelled. He wrote it. He rang it four times anyway. **Then a man on the ferry road did exactly what she described and added a Crown phrase to it, and she says on Day 21 that it is still being repeated and she cannot take it out.**

**On Day 21 she gave four reasons in public for not ringing**, in the same voice, and the fourth was *I cannot hear it*, and about nine people heard the difference and Tomas was one of them and wrote it down in four words. **Then she broke her own rule on purpose and said the rest of it out loud in the lane, telling about a hundred people to write it down**, on the ground that a thing said once by the person it belongs to is a record and a thing found out in a lane is a rumour. **This is a deliberate canon change to the Chapter 29 secrecy and is recorded in `state/continuity.md`.** She has rung nothing and given nothing but the fact of it away. Her hearing is still bad enough that she can ring and feel and see and cannot carry, and **no one in Kells is to be told except Tomas and the reader.** She is not to be rescued out of it and her hearing is not to be restored as a convenience. She has not asked for anything and has not been thanked.

## Oren Pike

Oren is a Crown-appointed watch captain in a Crown town whose age is not on the page, and this batch cost him the two things he had left.

**He handed the district an index of his own town.** A return asked for keepers and for *all persons having public standing in matters of signalling, with names* arrived on Day 17, and Oren realised that nine days of teaching a ward to speak in public with dates on it had made him the man who could supply it in a morning. He sent **eleven names with dates** and a **six-paragraph covering page** stating the objection in terms, and he read **the first five of it** aloud to Cask and Ardle before he signed it and **the sixth** after, which is the paragraph about the two of them, and then read all six in open court. **He refused to remove or protect a single one of them**, and said so to his two men in plain words, because the alternative was to hand an office a list and then tell the town which names he had saved. He read the page and the whole list aloud in open court with the ward in the room and Nell Fenn at the clerk's table, and said in court that a ward which publishes a procedure has made itself legible, and that legibility was what he wanted and that it is not the same thing as being helped.

**The Crown renamed the document rather than losing it.** At the eighth hour that evening the return came back with one line in a foreign margin: the list retained, no further return as to keepers required, and the return amended to read *persons having public standing in matters of signalling, with dates*. **Oren has entered the amendment, will read it into the market court at its next sitting, has not appealed, has not written to the district, and has not asked one of the eleven.** He put the two men who said *do not send it* on the gate for the days that matter, in writing, and entered that he did it on purpose.

**He narrowed his own authority again.** A Crown form at the low gate on Day 17 wanted every bell in the ward for seven days with the hour, the place and **the keeper's name.** He complied with the hours, the places and every bell, joined or unjoined, held by anyone, and refused the name, and entered exactly what he did and did not do, and had Ferrow read it back and signed it. **He told Tomas in advance, in the yard, with the door shut, that the watch will be a record and will not be a route, and that if Tomas's bell goes the watch writes the hour and the place and not the name, and that this is a protection and it is also a leash** — because he has now told every woman in a doorway in the ward in advance that the watch will come and write and will not stand there, and because he has been two men and a gate for eleven years and did not put two men between a lawful order and seventy-odd people on Day 10 and does not sleep properly. He gave Tomas the shape of the thing he was not going to let him find out the hard way: a bell with no answer is a noise, and the harm is not the four hours of waiting but **the page afterwards that says the ward waited four hours**, because a crowd that has waited four hours has been shown that waiting works.

**He is not Tomas's protector and did not pretend to be.** He is a man with a district file, a list of eleven names, a register going to Fourmile on the twenty-fourth, a cover-page dispute in his own hand, and two men at his gate who are angry with him on purpose.

## Nell Fenn

Nell is 16 and she is the person the ward acts through, and this batch she published a practice and was corrected by the person she published it to.

**The *Witnessed by* column is now public** and it is a practice, not a scandal, because she refused to say a Crown clerk's name, refused to say which form, and worked out — after eight days of refusing to let Tomas speak first — that the only shape that works is a question any household may put to a Crown clerk about a paper the Crown handed them. Mercy Slee took two thirds of the argument away from her: *any person* became *a person who wishes to*, *dispute* became *asked*, and *clerk* became *officer of the Crown* because the word had started to mean one particular young man with ink on his fingers. **The public reading of it was given to Mercy Slee**, because her house is on nothing and the first household the Crown has a file on ought to be a household it has never had anything about. Mercy Slee said the true thing back at her, that a girl who asks people first is still a girl who made the decision happen and has not noticed.

**She did not choose the first household.** Sena Dunn of house 121 walked up to the Crown table on the evening of Day 18, unchosen, and put the question, and gave her name to be written down because it was already written down. **Sena Dunn said the sentence of the whole volume by accident in Chapter 40**, when Mrs Corris said *nine of our doors, out of forty-one*, and changed the shape of Rudd's offer in a minute.

**Nell's private count — nine *not returned* lines, six *accompanying* lines, every household a child was taken from on one of those fifteen — is still unspent and is still in no public room in any chapter.** She has now told Mercy Slee that she is holding it and is not spending it today. Her fragment copy is still unused. **She has still not got the two people to stand beside her about the children**, and on Day 21 the Crown asked the question in its own hand before she got there.

**And she wrote the hardest line of the batch on the night of Day 20**, when she found out that the Crown had got forty households onto a slate by being kind at their doors: *the whole of my argument to those doors was that a question is a notice, and the Crown has now found a cheaper notice than mine.* She says in the ward's book that she will read that line out at the board before anything else, because the day she stops is the day the ward stops being able to check anything.

## Sabin Fell

Sabin is 61 and he has refused to protect Tomas for the third time in a month and has given him the most valuable thing he owns.

**He would not supply a design**, and said why: if he gives Tomas a shape, then what gets copied is him, and he will be standing in a room in Sennet with a guild on one side of the argument and a bell on the other. He gave four rules instead. **The fourth is the one he has never given anybody in this county in twenty years: take the person most likely to refuse, and ask them first** — because the first answer is a claim and every answer after it is a claim about the first, and a carrier carried by three people who are all certain has had its teeth taken out on the way to the market, and the carrier is not the bell, it is the disagreement, and the disagreement has to be somebody's and it should be the person most likely to give it. **He also said the witness matter must be findable, or it is a relic and not a witness, and that a carrier may not add anything, which means putting the difficulty in deliberately and letting it travel.**

**He found out that his life's work is in a district file.** Nobody asked him. He was not in the room, was not sent for, and learned on Day 16 that a district officer had read the *refused* column of the Measure's road map, asked the year of the first withdrawal, been told the Measure kept dates, and said he must file it as a schedule of non-response. **He has still not named what he carried past the gate in the Hush. He has never been told about the *Witnessed by* column.**

## Halden Crake

Halden is the Kells deputy reeve under the Crown inspection commission and **he has been given two things he is genuinely right about in this batch, and he has paid for both out loud.**

**He verified the ground himself with a spade, signed Tomas's page in his own name against his own works order of the eighth day, and said the thing he was right about**: if the ward's drain is what Tomas says it is, then a rain puts a foot of water in that lane and Renn's gate will not come and there are eleven children in it, and a commission that renews a line is not a flood authority, and if he files it the work will be suspended and the ward will have no warning in the ground at all. **He asked Tomas for a name for the file and did not get one, and Tomas gave him the page instead and said in the lane why.**

**On Day 17 he had orders he would not show and said why**: showing a man a paper you must act on and cannot refuse is making him choose, and he has watched people choose in front of him this month and one of them is the only reason there are not three children on a Crown wagon. He declined to give the district a figure of signal duty holders in a public room, on purpose, and let it be entered that he did it on purpose.

**He has asked the district whether there is civic disorder in the ward and filed the answer in the words that would hurt him**: the ward has established a public practice, kept it in three places, and declined every request made of it, and that is a fact and not a fault.

**On Day 21 he came down the lane without a black case and said the truest sentence anybody in the commission has said all month**: a procedure can make an office legible and cannot make an office stop; a ward that published a procedure made the Crown's paper good enough to be worth reading, and the reading is done by a clerk in a building, and the clerk is right, and the child goes. He said careful is not the same as effective and that they are all about to find out how different they are on the twenty-eighth. He said he is going to write the word *clean* on a page about a morning on which he had thirty-seven houses and a child. He is not a villain and he is not a martyr and he is a frightened man with a good method and a real grievance about disorder who has just been handed a district.

## Rhea Collis

Collis is a surveyor of the northern district whose age is not on the page, and by Day 21 she is a person rather than an appointment and she has paid her professional price twice in public.

She certified the Day 21 round **as a round**: thirty-seven doors, the form exhibited entire, an objection written in a margin in front of her at house 133. **She did not certify any household's answer, and declined again to countersign any record asserting that any household assented to or received one, for the reasons she entered on Day 12 and has not been permitted to withdraw.** And then she put into her own return, unasked, that **a form exhibited entire, a published procedure and a clerk with a conscience together make a round that is conducted properly and that this is not the same thing as a round that is lawful**, and that she was at a gate at four in the morning on the tenth and at a court at four in the morning on the twenty-first, and that one child went into a wagon both times, and that the difference between them is a column with a name in it and a margin with a name on it, and that she is a surveyor of eleven years and no longer knows what a form is for. **She is not the missing bellwright. She is not going to be made into the missing anything. She is going to Fourmile with a return that says nine houses.**

## Ansel Stroud

Stroud is a Crown filing clerk, frightened, competent, useful, and **he has now said one true thing in a public room that he refused to write down.**

He stood up unasked in a market court on Day 20, brought the **full return** out of his own satchel, put it on the table himself, squared the top edge with both hands, and had its ninth column read aloud, because he had been asked to be shown a column and had brought the wrong form and said that was his fault. He confirmed the column is **printed**, that the hand is the house's, that it is empty on every Kells return, that it is not empty in every district and that he is prohibited from saying which, and he told the room that the prohibition was not evasion. He offered to enter a name in the column for a household that wanted one. **And then he said, unentered, in a public room with about thirty people under the tiles, that the reason the column is empty in this ward is that the rounds here are conducted at four in the morning and there is no room at four in the morning** — and said he would not write it down, because if he writes it down it is a Crown officer's opinion about the conduct of a Crown round and there is a form for that and it is not this form, and he has a family. On Day 21 the clerk who wrote Mercy Slee's answer said in the open that he had been asked by his office what he said in a filing room on Day 18 and had answered truthfully and would not say what he said. **He is the man who will be asked who let him do it, and he is now a man who has said a thing in a room and let thirty people hear it.**

## Iven Sore

Iven Sore is the living master of the Bellwrights' Measure and the name on the withdrawn docket. **He does not appear in Chapters 31–40.** His conditional challenge is live and only partly discharged, and **he has still not said he did not sign the withdrawn service entry.** The genuine-or-borrowed question is untouched by this batch and must not be settled by assumption. What has changed around him: **his trade's fifty-three recorded withdrawals are in a district file being read as a schedule of non-response, and he was not asked and did not know.**

- **Absalom Teage** of house 114, who lost a son of nine on Day 10, refused the Crown's witness offer in open court in three columns on Day 20 and then turned the argument round: put the reason field on the form a house is handed and he will fill in every field with his own hand. On Day 21 he stood in a lane for two hours while a girl of ten went onto a wagon and gave his reason out loud — *I have got a boy of nine and I have written things in a margin* — and said he will write it down and read it out.
- **Sena Dunn** of house 121, whose name is in no book in this town, is the first household in the ward to put the *Witnessed by* question to a Crown clerk, on the evening of Day 18, unchosen, and gave her name to be written down. She did it because she wanted a page that says she came to a table on her own feet.
- **Mercy Slee** of house 119, with a boy of nine, on no roll until Day 18, is now the public reader of the ward's most important question, the first household named in it, a co-holder of the ward's copy, **and on the district's amended return of Day 17 as a person having public standing, entered by a watch captain under an order whom she did not ask.** She said the cost out loud before she agreed, and she said the true thing back at Nell about who decided.
- **Gib Hask** has been at the low end of the turn for the whole of Day 15 with six men, a crowbar, a spade and a coil of rope, and **has not put the bar in the ground in any chapter of this batch.** His grievance is permanent and the Crown line is now permanently bridged over the ward's own drain. Tomas told him in a lane that he would not ask anybody to close the lane, and Hask called it a coward's answer, and Tomas entered that Hask is right and he cannot answer it.
- **Ivo Prentice** of the ferry road petitioned with nine adults in four households for a Crown line, told a Crown officer in front of nine people that the ward had shut its own road, volunteered that the Crown cart was still across the culvert, and **at half past the eighth hour on Day 19 repeated a bell he had heard at six hundred yards and added four words to it that Tomas never said: *at first light*, which is the Crown's own phrase off the Crown's own notice.** He is the Crown's petitioner and the ward's repeater and has not been asked about either.
- **Siss Larke** of house 96, with a boy of fourteen, was packing for Fourmile, and stopped Tomas in the lane on the night of Day 19 with a rope round a box and asked him whether to send the box or the boy. **He is the reason the extension happened.**
- **Marda Hodler** of house 91, with a boy of four, was told on Day 14 by a young man behind a counter in Fourmile that the round comes at first light and that the children who are out of the ward on the day are the ones that are not moved. - **Wenna Cray** accepted a keeper's duty on the plain bell with four clauses and made him read them twice. **Hollis Renn** accepted in eleven words, wrote his own sentence on his oilskin board, and would not give a reason because his own term does not require one. **Foreman Crane** refused in a minute and signed the refusal at Tomas's request, because a foreman cannot hold two duties on one morning and be trusted for either.

## Relationship and civic movement

Tomas and Tamsin have a boundary in writing, a refused keeper's duty with her reason on the page, a four-day silence owed to the ward, and a slow approach through work and through the correction she gave him before he built the thing. **No romance and no binding bell.** Oren and Tomas are a practical alliance in which a captain has handed over his own authority twice and a maker has accepted a leash he cannot argue with. Nell and Tomas are a relationship in which she corrects him in public and he takes it, and in which he has learned to ask her for the price of something before he does it. Sabin has taught the last rule and refused protection and watched his life's work become a schedule of non-response. Halden is right, twice, and frightened, and legible. The Crown has answered every document with a document and won five times in a row. **And the ward has a page it can check, a bell it cannot, and a question it has not answered in nine days of trying, which the Crown has now asked out loud and offered to certify.**
