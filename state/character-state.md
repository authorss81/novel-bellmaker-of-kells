# Character State After Batch 0002

## Tomas Vale

Tomas is 32, still the practical third-generation bellmaker and keeper of the Vale Bellhouse. He is at the **first edge of Stage 1 (Near Toll)** and gained **no power at all** in Chapters 11–20: he diagnosed an undercut with a rod, a chalk mark and a claim sheet, and could not identify a source, an emotion, a copied answer, or a future event. Costs in this batch were civic and physical rather than metallurgical: four days of bench time, a lost feast-bell contract, an untuned wheelwright's counter bell he still owes, the market's lost trade, a drowned pig, four flooded houses, a loss of balance in a crowded lane, and renewed ringing ears. **No damp store, no bloomed lead, and no bellmetal stock damage are on the page.**

His movement is real but partial. He learned to write the claim once and read the same words to everybody; he recorded a refusal instead of arguing it away; he refused to hide the trial bell when hiding it would have looked like care; he let Nell stop him from turning a withdrawn witness into an answer he could use; he asked Tamsin for help instead of assigning it, and wrote his own habit of doing otherwise into the record; and he tore up a promise that would have covered the whole town, because Nell and Tamsin showed him it had four claims in it and he could stand behind only one. He still wants a name to be angry at, and in Chapter 20 he heard himself want it, said so in front of witnesses, and did not act on it.

## Tamsin Rook

Tamsin is 29, a former Crown road-ringer and signal courier whose hearing was damaged by forced resonance and is now visibly worse: she cannot follow speech in a crowded room, she watches the gate bell instead of hearing it, and the counted strokes of Day 8 cost her the whole of Day 9. She recovered and retained her stolen courier handbell throughout. Her statement that the alteration exists and that the reach is longer than her route is hers and was not measured by anyone on Day 4: what she rang once at the low gate, with a boy on the landing stage a stone's throw above the low drain gate, established only the **authorized** boundary. The alteration was then read publicly under watch supervision in the Bellhouse yard on Day 6, with Tomas reading the wear without touching it and no custody changing; her route was never disclosed and never required. She logged the bell's real reach on Day 9 — it goes too far past the landing and misses people inside its own route — and now governs it with a written rule: a repeater and not an author, rung only with a named person beside her who can say what it means, for the whole of a record or for none of it, never to fill a withdrawn place and never to carry only the convenient parts.

Her relationship with Tomas has moved from wary parallelism to a boundary he can be held to. She told him what yes costs, made him ask rather than assign, refused to be reported as the reason a route is trusted, and walked the towpath herself as far as the toll shed. **She has not granted Tomas or the watch control of her handbell or her route, and nothing in this batch does so.**

## Oren Pike

Oren is 37, Kells watch captain and brother of Ewan Pike. He has moved from suspecting Tomas to working with him on evidence, and he has become more dangerous to Tomas rather than less: he refused the Crown's single packet, put his own watch mark on the same wax as the Crown's over eight sealed bells, imposed three written conditions on any public signal, required the public account of the rope, and told Tomas plainly that he will not stop the seizure of the trial bell. His grief is active and unsoftened — he ordered the public account partly so that Ewan's version would be on paper in his own hand — but he entered the thin sound that contradicts twelve years of his own belief, and he let Wen Anner refuse both absolution and condemnation. His watch is divided: two of his men will carry out a lawful seizure at four in the morning if the Crown orders it, and he will not put them in a position where one of them has to choose in front of a crowd.

## Nell Fenn

Nell is 16 and is no longer only a moral witness; she is a record-keeper. She wrote her own question rather than using a bellmaker's, pinned it to the ward board so nobody could say the ward was asked something else, gathered three independent Low Ward answers in the correct order on Day 4, refused to let Tomas write a refusal as fear, stopped him from turning Sena Dunn's withdrawal into an answer he could use, and had the household fragment copied in daylight at a public board while keeping the original against her ribs. She has told Tomas that nobody may answer for the Fenn household, including her mother and including herself. She no longer sends the chandler's work to the Bellhouse. She remains a sixteen-year-old managing her mother's habit of agreeing to whatever sounds like work.

## Sabin Fell

Sabin is 61 and Tomas's former master. In this batch he taught only rules — write the claim once, read the same words to each person, write what they actually said, never improve an answer — and he explicitly refused to give the Bellwrights' Measure a professional opinion on the trial handbell, which was recorded as *no opinion offered* and which is what kept his shop's name off the guild's record. That refusal is a cost he chose. He has still not named what he carried past the gate in the Hush, and he still cannot protect Tomas from consequences; he can only say, in a yard, in front of Crown guards, that the question was not being asked properly.

## Halden Crake

Halden is the Kells deputy reeve acting under a Crown inspection commission, and he has escalated without becoming a villain. He has signed the narrow seven-day hold, brought an inventory and sealing order, made an emergency custody hearing the first legal object of the dawn, and told the ward in the open that he wants a record saying it was warned and does not care who rings the bell. He is frightened of civic disorder in a specific, legible way: he believes a Crown clerk will arrive after any death on that road and ask one question, and he is building the answer to it in advance. **A woman at the market said some of them never wanted the bells, and Halden called it the first sensible thing anyone had said all week, which is how the Unrung's argument first appears in his mouth.**

## Rhea Collis

Surveyor **Rhea Collis** is new to the prose, in her mid-thirties, of the northern district, appointed to certify the Low Ward inspection on route L-4 at the rescheduled dawn. She arrived eleven days late on the north road because the ferry at Fourmile was not running and the Crown would not pay for a boat, and she says so once. She is not Pever, she states she is not the officer who certified the carrier at the Bellhouse, and she states she has never held the L-4 entry. Her line on the roll carries a date and **no withdrawal**. She is due at the north gate at four on Day 10 and has said she will be there to watch whatever it is they are all doing. **She is not the missing docket surveyor and must not be identified as one.**

## Iven Sore

Iven Sore is 61-ish, master of the Bellwrights' Measure, narrow, ink to the second knuckle, with hands that reach for a tool the way a man reaches for a doorway in the dark. He is on the page now, and he is competent and frightened rather than rivalrous. He inspected the trial handbell without sounding it and filed that limitation himself, entered *competent and expensive* beside the split tuning file, called Tomas's wedging of the posting sliver correct, and showed him the Measure's road map with its fifty-three recorded ward withdrawals, a number that has nothing to do with the forty-one households on the Crown's L-4 answer schedule. He offered a certified record, a copy of the map and a seat in the room where a route is built, in exchange for a master key and a licence making the Bellhouse answerable for every route answer, and both were refused. Asked about the withdrawn docket, he **can support** that he never held the L-4 commission and **cannot support** the rest, and refused to say he did not sign it. He will answer in a Measure room with a witness in eleven days. **The genuine-or-borrowed question stays open.** His conditional challenge is live but satisfied for now.

## New Low Ward and town figures

- **Bevin Sarr**, net-maker. Refused twice on the record, asked for both refusals to stand unsigned, and is the man who made a Crown officer say *Crown* twice in front of eleven witnesses.
- **Hollis Renn**, low flood-gate keeper, with a written duty on an oilskin board and a gate whose pawl slips.
- **Wyman Dace**, west road bridge keeper, who writes his duties on slates and will not say a bridge is safe.
- **Foreman Crane**, market landing foreman, who can close a lane or not.
- **Wenna Cray**, who keeps a slate of the missing and named the cost of speaking before she accepted.
- **Garn Fisk**, who refused the route and refused to be named. **Sena Dunn**, who agreed and then withdrew in open court.
- **Gib Hask**, gate-porter and rope-maker, who wants the Crown's buried line out of the ward and said he would pull it himself if the bell is taken.
- **Wen Anner**, twenty-four, the child saved from the Hush frame, who is angry at both Tomas's act and his inaction and said so on the record.
- **A wheelwright with a counter bell**, unnamed, who brought his bell to the Bellhouse on Day 3 and took it away untuned, promised back when the stay is over. He is a customer the reader met and does not need to meet again.
- **The market court usher**, keeper of the unbound iron call bell, who is a recurring civic figure from Chapter 18.

## Relationship and civic movement

Tomas and Tamsin have a working boundary rather than romance, and the boundary is now something he can be held to in writing. Oren and Tomas are a practical alliance that is not trust and not forgiveness, built out of a watch mark on the same wax and a condition in the book. Nell and Tomas have a relationship in which she corrects him and he takes it. Sabin has given instruction and refused protection. Halden is escalating for reasons the town can partly hear, and one of those reasons belongs to the Unrung. The town has heard a real warning, has made three answers that agree with nothing, has recorded three withdrawals and two withdrawn doors, and is now facing a Crown document that says all of it was one.
