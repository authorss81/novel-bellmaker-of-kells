# Batch 0004 Summary — Chapters 31–40

**Volume:** 01 — *The Bell Before the Crime*
**Batch:** 0004
**Status:** Complete
**Day span:** Day 15 to Day 21 (seven days). Day 0 is the morning the carrier arrived. Chapters 21–30 occupied Day 10 to Day 14.

## Batch result

The road opened, and the ward learned that a document and a bell are both ways of making an office legible, and neither of them is a way of stopping it.

**Day 15 is two chapters and it is the batch's set piece.** A Crown road gang pegged a renewal line across the low lane at twenty past the seventh hour, eleven feet above the washhouse step, in the lane where eleven children walk to the landing twice a day. The peg stood over a filled service cut — the Bellwrights' washhouse run — which Tomas Vale can see by looking at it and can corroborate with one line in his father's bench-book. He said so out loud, in the lane, to the gang, to Gib Hask's six men with the crowbar, and to about thirty people, and he did not touch the line and did not ask them to stop. The foreman, **Brann**, who signed for the cart at the low-turn culvert, moved the mark two yards toward the ward side on his own hand at nine minutes past the ninth hour and initialled the change with **Perry**, a Crown road hand of nineteen who keeps the gang's book and who asked Tomas to say the reason again so that it would be in a mouth that was not hers. Within two hours a Crown clerk was in the lane with a return whose field is headed *cause stated by*, and the foreman's report was already in Fourmile, copied out fair, and it named Tomas Vale as the man who advised the foreman. **Being right in a lane is now a thing that gets written down.** Tomas refused to have a summary of himself: he had his name, the hour, the ground, and his reasons entered in the Crown's own words, and then told Hask, in front of twenty people, that he would not ask anybody to close the lane, and Hask called it a coward's answer, and Tomas recorded that Hask is right and he cannot answer him.

By the fourth hour of the afternoon the copper came up in three lengths, hand's breadth, sheathed in black cloth gone to the consistency of tar — and under the lime bed beneath it there was not lime. There was a **stone channel, hand wide, three courses, the ward's own drain**, shown on the Crown's own sheet, in a different ink, dashed, labelled *existing culvert*, and bridged at four points by the Crown line above it. Deputy Reeve **Halden Crake** came down at the fifth hour, verified it himself with a spade, and asked Tomas for **a name** for the file. Tomas gave him the page and not the name, on the explicit ground that a correction with a Crown officer's name behind it sends a Crown man to the ward instead of correcting a sheet. Crake signed the page in his own name **against his own works order of the eighth day**, and said the thing he was right about, which is that if the drain is what Tomas says it is then a rain puts a foot of water in that lane and Renn's gate will not come and there are eleven children in it. Tomas's page ends: *I have no instrument.*

**On the same day, at the eleventh hour, the Bellwrights' Measure's road map went to the district.** Fifty-three dated and keyed withdrawals, in a sealed leather case, into a district officer's hands, with a receipt. A Crown copyist wrote Oren's caveat down. The officer read the *refused* column, asked the year of the first and whether the Measure kept reasons or dates, was told dates, wrote that down, and said he must file it as a schedule of non-response. **Nobody asked Sabin Fell.** He was not in the room, was not sent for, and did not know until the following day. Oren said aloud that the reason he did not send word is that he did not want to be in a room with him while it happened, and had it entered.

**Then Tomas found a second line under his own yard.** A damp strip the length of the shop that did not dry; a rod going in easy two feet, hard a foot, easy again; the line running under the yard wall toward the millrace; and under the loose door threshold stone, two flat iron bars a foot apart wrapped in tarred rope and pitch, going out under the wall. It is not the Bellwrights' cut, it is not in his father's book, and it is on no Crown sheet the watch holds. Oren put it in the gate book in three sentences, told the ward board nothing, and told Tomas why: an unexplained line under a workshop the Crown has already sealed comes back as an order with a spade in it, and the answer to *who put it there* in this town is one word long. **It is known to three people — Tomas, Oren, Jerm.** The word *line* is not to be said in a lane that week.

**Day 16 is the road that is not closed.** Wyman Dace writes the wind and never the word *safe*. Foreman Crane can shut a lane in an hour and will not shut it for a warning, because a road is a road and the last warning in this ward was carried by a clapper nobody in the ward put in. Tomas walked the three narrows twice, wrote four hundred words about a road and not one opinion, handed it over and shut up. Crane's question — *who pays for a road a keeper has closed?* — has the answer **the market court's small fund, which has never once been spent**, and the clerk read the figure out twice and nobody wrote the number down. At ten minutes past the seventh hour of the sixteenth, three people said three sentences in three columns in the market lane, and the bar and chain went up at the head of the mill narrows: **Alis Marrow** of the toll shed, whose seventy-nine-year-old mother goes down four cut steps four times a day; **Foreman Crane**; and **Wyman Dace**, from a slate, which he handed to Ferrow to hold. A Crown officer went round the ferry road asking whether the ward had shut its own road, and Ivo Prentice told him, in front of nine people, that it had, on the ward's own account — and then volunteered that the Crown cart was still across the culvert. **The same day**, a household in the upper ward was found packing for Fourmile for no reason anybody could give, and the reason was that on the fourteenth a young man behind a counter in Fourmile told Marda Hodler that *the round comes at first light, and they will want the children out of the ward, and the ones that are out of the ward on the day are the ones that are not moved.* **The Crown's own phrase came out of a Crown's mouth and into a woman's box on the fourteenth of the month, and the ward heard it as rumour five days before the round.**

**Day 17 is Oren's chapter and it is the batch's institutional centre.** The district asked for a list of the ward's signal keepers *and of all persons in the ward having public standing in matters of signalling, with names*. Oren realised he had spent nine days teaching this town to speak in public with dates on it and had just handed a district office an index of it. He sent **eleven names**, in a flat hand, with dates, **and a six-paragraph covering page** stating the objection in terms — that a list of keepers identifies duties and duties can be answered, and a list of persons who have spoken identifies citizens — and that the watch produced it because an order is an order. **He read the first five of it to Cask and Ardle before he signed it and the sixth after, and the sixth is the paragraph about the two men.** Cask had been asked, loudly, at a table of men in the Plough and Harrow on the fourteenth night, whether the watch would do it again, and answered *that is not what I asked you*, and wanted it written that he answered. Oren read all six paragraphs of the covering page and the whole list aloud in open court with the ward in the room and Nell Fenn at the clerk's table, **refused to remove or protect a single name, and put the two men who had said *do not send it* on the gate for the days that matter.** Crake, there with a Crown order he would not show, declined to give the district a figure of signal duty holders in a public room, on the stated ground that showing a man an order you must act on and cannot refuse is making him choose. **At the eighth hour that evening the return came back with one line in the margin, in another hand, and the line was not an objection to anything Oren said: *Received, seventeenth day. Index retained. No further return as to keepers is required. The return is amended, for filing purposes, to read: persons of this ward having public standing in matters of signalling, with dates.*** The Crown did not lose the list. It renamed it. Every name on it, including Mercy Slee, who is on no roll, and Tamsin Rook, who is on it for the thirteenth day, when she walked her own route after dark with a named man beside her, rang once at seven places and wrote at each what was expected and what was heard, and then offered four terms out loud, once each, and for having had the end of that route said at the north gate in front of eleven witnesses and entered in the watch book in her own words. **The form asked for keepers as well, and the chapter no longer claims it did not: both halves were asked for on one sheet, and the keeper half is the half the Crown can demand again.**

**The same evening, Serjeant Teale delivered a form at the low gate.** A keeper register: every bell sounded in the ward for seven days, with the hour, the place, **and the name of the person who sounded it**. Oren neither obeyed nor refused. He took the hours and the places and every bell, joined or unjoined, held by anyone, and refused the name, and entered exactly what he did and did not do, and had Ferrow read it back in the morning and signed it. He told Tomas in advance, in the yard, with the door shut: the watch will be a record and will not be a route; if your bell goes, the watch writes the hour and the place and not your name; **that is a protection and it is also a leash**, because the watch has now told every woman in a doorway in advance that it will come and write and not stand there. And then, asked the question he had walked down the hill to ask — *if a bell rang tonight and nobody answered, what would the watch do?* — Oren gave him the shape of it: a bell with no answer is a noise; the watch would write the hour and the place and nothing else; and the harm would not be the four hours, it would be the **page** afterwards that says the ward waited four hours, because a crowd that has waited four hours has been shown that waiting works.

**Day 18 is Nell's chapter, and it is about a burned slate.** The *Witnessed by* column can be published without naming Stroud, and the only shape that works is the shape Tomas has been insisting on: a question any household may put to a Crown clerk about a paper the Crown handed them. Not an accusation, which names a villain; not a claim, which names a source. Mercy Slee wins two thirds of the argument — *any person* becomes *a person who wishes to*, and *dispute* becomes *asked*, and *clerk* becomes *officer of the Crown* because the word has begun to mean one particular young man with ink on his fingers. The price is that the first household to ask becomes a household the Crown has a file on, and Nell works out that it must be Mercy Slee because her house is on nothing, and Mercy Slee says out loud that a girl who asks people first is still a girl who made the decision happen and has not noticed. **The public reading of the column question was given to Mercy Slee on the eighteenth day, in daylight, at the board, in about forty people's hearing, and she said her own address out loud before anybody else could.** And at the sixth hour of that evening the first household to put it to a Crown clerk was **Sena Dunn of house 121**, who nobody had chosen and who is in no book in this town, and who gave her name to be written down because the clerk had already written it down. Stroud's answer took four minutes and ten seconds and was much longer than a clerk's answer should have been: the column is for a person present at the answering, the hand is the house's, it is empty on every Kells return, it is not empty in every district and he is prohibited from saying which — and then, unentered, in a public room, to about thirty people: *the reason that column is empty in this ward is that there is no room at four in the morning.* He said he would not write it down.

**Day 19 is the craft chapter, and it is the chapter where the bell is built correctly.** Sabin Fell teaches four rules and refuses, on principle, to supply a design, and gives Tomas the one he has never given anybody in this county: **take the person most likely to refuse, and ask them first.** Also: the witness matter must be findable, or it is a relic and not a witness; and a carrier may not add anything, which means putting the difficulty in deliberately and letting it travel. Tomas asks Oren, in writing, for permission to cast and bench-test a bell. Oren narrows it to one strike an hour, on the bench, with a leather, not in the lane, with Jerm holding the book, and signs it, and says that he is entering that he permitted a man under a Crown declaration to cast a bell, and that this is the page he will have to defend in a room.

The bell is cast out of the **last of a half-barrel of the shop's own bellmetal out of his father's hearth**, which was never on a Crown inventory because an inventory counts objects and this is a material. Six and a half inches at the mouth, ash stock, iron clapper, no wax, no groove in the crown, the mark VALE KELLS and the date and nothing else. Two failures are on the page: it is **too bright** and goes further than he wants and cannot be made not to, because a man who tunes a bell to stop at a distance is tuning it to a lie; and it **repeats in a corner**, because a bell near hard stone comes back a fifth of a second later, and a thing that comes out twice is a claim and a copy of a claim, and a copy is how a sentence stops belonging to the person who said it. The witness matter is **one bar out of the ward's own drain grating at the low lane**, asked for at the ward board **at the ninth hour**, in daylight, in front of about thirty people, with the whole of the bell's words read out and the whole of its cost stated first, and it was granted before the metal was melted. The board gave it; Mercy Slee wrote the gift, the date, the words and the cost into the ward's book; two more people signed under her; Hask sent word that the grate was his to keep and he would keep it. **He asked before the act and it cost the ward a piece of its own iron and a line in a Crown register forever.**

He asked four people, one at a time, with the same words, having intended to ask three. **Tamsin Rook was first**, because Sabin's rule, and because Tomas had put her fourth on his own list for two days out of pure wanting and had told her so in writing with the hour on it before he asked her anything. She refused at once and gave a reason her own fourth term does not require. **Wenna Cray** accepted with four clauses and made him read them twice. **Hollis Renn** accepted in eleven words and wrote his own sentence on his oilskin board. **Foreman Crane** refused in a minute and signed the refusal at Tomas's request. Four asked, two accepted, two refused. The count is a count of two, and he did not go looking for a fifth.

**And then the extension, in the same day, and it is the batch's expensive mistake.** The water came at the sixth hour of the evening — **a day and some hours early, on a date Tomas had written on a page on the board** — and Renn's gate would not come, and Renn said the cause out loud: the culvert and the ward's drain are one water, and there is a Crown cart across the low-turn culvert under a works order a fortnight old. Tomas had known. He had left the cart out of the sentence because a Crown work in a warning is a sentence that brings a Crown office to the ward, and he had put in a drain he could stand in and prove. **The claim was true and the reason in it was wrong.** He rang once, correctly, at the seventh hour, and Renn and Cray answered once each inside the two hundred yards they had agreed to, and it bought four things: eleven children stopped on the lane, Hask's four men stayed on their dry step, and a carter turned back at the low turn. It did not reach the market, the ferry road, or the mill end of the west road. **At the eighth hour Siss Larke stopped him in the lane with a rope round a box and a boy of fourteen behind her and asked him whether to send the box or the boy**, and Tomas had nothing to offer, and a paper takes a day and a bell takes a moment, and he went back up the hill and got the bell off its frame and carried it into the market lane and **rang it four times and asked nobody**. He sent the true easy part only, four times, having read Sabin's sentence about what a long thin answer turns into and rung it anyway. The market lane emptied: about one hundred and eighty persons in nine minutes, about seventy of them through the north gate in the next hour, a carrier named Rutledge lost a handcart and load stated at thirty-six pounds, and **Perry, the nineteen-year-old Crown road hand standing in the market lane with a book and a lamp because Tomas had written to her on the fifteenth and asked her to be there and write down where his words reached, had her right wrist broken by the shoe of a handcart.** He stopped it himself, at the fifth hour of the morning of the twentieth, on the fish market step, in daylight, in front of about sixty people, and did not call it a false alarm, because the alarm was true and the panic was his fault. He recorded the cost in figures and words, **named Crane and Tamsin Rook in the record as the two whose refusal would have prevented a keeper at the mill end of the west road**, told the keepers before the ward, and gave no explanation for four days.

**And at half past the eighth hour on the ferry road, Ivo Prentice repeated it in his own gate and added four words Tomas did not say: *at first light*.** It is on the Crown's own notice for the twenty-first and in the district's return and now it is in the ward's mouth, whole and small and already confident, and it was repeated four more times that night by people who believed they were telling their neighbours.

**Day 20 is the form.** Nell asks, in writing, before the eighth hour, citing the Crown's own notice of the seventh evening, for the form to be exhibited **today**, and the Crown agrees, in the right answer, in the same room the round will be held in. About a hundred and thirty people come. The Crown exhibits the **summary form** entire and there is no *Witnessed by* column on it, and that is true. **Ansel Stroud then stands up, unasked, puts the full return on the table himself, squares the top edge with both hands, and has its ninth column read aloud.** The column is printed. It is empty. And then the Crown does the thing nobody in the ward had prepared for: it **offers to fill it.** Mrs Corris of house 133, who gave no answer and no name on the tenth and whose thirteen-year-old was taken, and whom nobody had been back to in ten days, says the only sentence that made the Crown's offer survivable for a whole afternoon: ***Nine of our doors. Out of forty-one.*** Then the Crown produces a **supplementary return** carrying the objection, the witness name, **and the reason field the summary form drops** — which answers everything Nell wanted and is the trap, because there is nothing on it to say which hand wrote the reason. The ward refuses in **three columns** — Mercy Slee, Absalom Teage (who turns the argument round and asks for the reason field on the form a house is handed, *today*), and the market court usher, who declines to say a fourth thing about her bell. **Crown Officer Ambrose Rudd then offers Surveyor Collis's certification to any household on the route** — not the form, a person — and says plainly that he is doing it because a commission that refuses to offer is lying, and that the price is her and not him. And at the ninth hour of the evening, **two Crown officers go round about forty doors with a slate and a lamp, politely, and ask whether the household intends to attend in person, and write down every answer.** About forty households said yes. **Nell found out from a boy of eleven, and wrote two lines: the Crown has obtained for free, by being kind, the exact instrument she spent eight days arguing about.**

**Day 21 is the round, and it goes badly both ways.** Three hundred and forty people are in the lane at first light by Oren's count. The form is exhibited entire. The Crown table is under the arcade because the watch compelled a counter on the ninth day and nobody withdrew the compulsion. At **twenty-two of thirty-seven doors** a household put the three questions and had the answers written in the margin in a clerk's own name and initialled; at nine of those the answer was *I am not permitted to say* and that was written down too. **One child is taken. Hattie Wilde, of house 108, aged ten**, and she is never marked before, and the reason is the most frightening document in the volume: the *Witnessed by* column on her line is **not cleared between rounds**, and it holds her mother's name in her mother's own hand, given at that door **two autumns ago and carried forward through every round since**, and her mother has been dead two years, and nobody had ever struck it out. The objection was entered in the margin and initialled, **and the objection did not suspend the renewal.** The Crown took fewer children than on the tenth. It did not take none. And the Crown did it properly, with the form exhibited and a surveyor present, which is worse.

**Tomas spends the whole round writing down what the ward actually did, house by house, in three columns headed — not *position*, not *answer* — *first thing, second thing, third thing*, fifty-one lines, checkable at any door.** He does not ring. He says out loud, at the low gate, that the reason is not law and not courage: a bell cannot be made to say a thing it was not bound to say, and anything else would have been a version, and this ward already has a version on the ferry road. He has said nothing further about the market lane and will not for four days. **Perry returns the paper he wrote to her and he does not take it back.** Oren records three answers in three columns. Tamsin's route is not used and she states four reasons out loud, the fourth of which is *I cannot hear it*, and only nine people hear the difference — **and then, on her own initiative, she says the rest of it out loud and tells about a hundred people to write it down. This is a deliberate change to the Chapter 29 rule that nobody in Kells was to be told, and it is the reason the state files no longer say that nobody knows.**

The Crown's answer is nailed at half past the eleventh hour. It is the shortest notice it has ever put on that board and it does not mention the round. It says the ward's practice is **a matter for the district and no longer for the local court**; it posts the next renewal for **the twenty-eighth day**; and under it, in five words, in a printed notice: *the district will consider the renewal of the ward's own line, of the possession of the ground in which it is laid, and of the persons under seventeen in the Low Ward.* **The Crown has spent nine days building a procedure and has found a cheaper instrument: the ward's own unasked question, asked out loud, in the Crown's hand.** And at the last hour Serjeant Teale reads a fourth paragraph, which is not a notice at all: the district will accept **any** answer to the question whether the ward intends to remove the persons under seventeen from the ward before the next renewal and to whom, and will certify it, and **a certified answer will be a return.**

## Object custody and condition after Chapter 40

- **Tomas's plain carrier — NEW, and the batch's central object.** Cast on the **nineteenth day** in the Vale Bellhouse yard out of the **last of a half-barrel of the shop's own bellmetal out of his father's hearth**, which was never on a Crown inventory. Six and a half inches at the mouth; ash stock; leather-bound headstock; iron clapper; **no wax in the crown, no groove dressed in the crown**; the mark VALE KELLS and the date and nothing else. Witness matter: **one bar out of the ward's own drain grating at the low lane at the foot of the ward**, given by the ward board at the ninth hour on the nineteenth, before the metal was melted, set into the waist inside, cut twice with a file in three places so it can be found in ten years with a lamp. Condition: **too bright, and it cannot be made less so**; it repeats within eight feet of the ash-pit wall. It carries one bounded claim (water above the flat stones at the foot of the low lane, with the hour, the cause, the uncertainty, and the keeper's duty all inside the words). Two keepers: **Hollis Renn** at the low gate and **Wenna Cray** at the ward board and two named doors. Two refusals on the record: **Foreman Crane** and **Tamsin Rook**. **It was sounded six times in the Bellhouse yard on the nineteenth and four times in the market lane.** In the yard: struck with a leather on the bench when the skin came off, twice more an hour apart as the paper allowed, once in the open in the middle of the yard after the ash-pit wall returned the note, once at the fifth hour of the afternoon, and once at the seventh hour, **which is the evening** — the water having come at the sixth hour of the evening. **Six yard entries, not two.** In the market lane, wrongly, four times at about twenty past the eighth hour. **The nineteenth therefore carries twelve register entries** — six in the yard, one at the low drain gate and one at the ward board at the seventh hour and some minutes, and four in the market lane — with *none* in the name column twelve times. **The half-barrel is now empty, and was emptied by the pour, not before it: the cost entered at the board on the morning of the nineteenth was that the barrel holds metal for one bell and not two.**
- **Tomas's trial handbell:** **still in Crown custody**, in a district building, **bound and unspent, never rung, never returned to the Bellhouse.** No custody event in this batch. Tomas still holds the receipt with his margin correction, in his hand, with the Crown copyist's initial, and the initial is not agreement.
- **The fixed stripped Crown survey carrier:** **still in the Kells watch store**, upright in its mounting frame in its reinforced padded cradle, retaining bolts still released from the Chapter 10 transfer, red watch seal on the lower brace. **No chapter in this batch moved it.** The joint receipt on the Crown file **expired at the fourth hour of Day 10 and there has still never been a custody review.** Crown ownership unresolved. **The comparison Oren arranged in Chapter 3 — the courier handbell against the fixed carrier — has still never been made, in any batch.**
- **The millrace bridge bell:** still on the north wall of the yard, still the only Bellhouse bell Tomas may ring, under Oren's no-ringing order re-made by his own hand on Day 10. It is **not named in the bell register in this batch**; the register's seventeenth has one entry (the market court call bell opened a sitting), **the nineteenth has twelve** (six in the Vale Bellhouse yard, one at the low drain gate, one at the ward board, four in the market lane), and the twentieth and twenty-first are blank.
- **Tamsin's stolen courier handbell:** in her possession throughout. **Not rung in this batch at all**, and she states four reasons on Day 21, the fourth of which is that she cannot hear its note, and then publishes that fact to the lane herself at her own price, on the reasoning that a thing said once by the person it belongs to is a record and a thing found out in a lane is a rumour. **The lost note is now known in the ward; the bell, the route and her authority are still hers and nothing has been rescued for her.**
- **The market court call bell:** still the unbound iron handbell of the market court, keeper the usher, no wire, no groove, no wax. It **rings nothing in this batch.** It is named in the district's amended return of the seventeenth as the object of a keeper refusal, and the usher refuses a fourth thing about it on Day 20.
- **The wheelwright's counter bell:** **gone from the bench.** Tomas had it dressed and it was collected; Chapter 40 records "a clean space where a wheelwright's counter bell had stood for eleven days." **The page is not on it and its price is not on the page.**

## The *Witnessed by* column — now public

**Published as a question on the eighteenth day**, in these words, read aloud by Mercy Slee of house 119 at the ward board in about forty people's hearing, with Nell Fenn's name under it and Mercy Slee's, and Oren Pike's beneath:

*Any person in this ward may go to any Crown clerk, in daylight, and ask this question about a paper they have been given: On a Crown form, there is a column headed* Witnessed by. *What goes in it, and is there one on my form? And they may take a witness, and they may write down what is said to them, and the answer must be written down where the person who asked can see it.*

**It names no officer and no office and no person.** The *clerk* wording was struck by Mercy Slee and replaced with *officer of the Crown*. *Any person* was replaced with *a person who wishes to*. *Dispute* was replaced with *asked*.

**Known to, and the state of each:**
- **Nell Fenn** — wrote it, read the price out, published it.
- **Mercy Slee** — the public reader, the first household named, and a co-holder.
- **Oren Pike** — signed the bottom line, the first thing he has put his name under in this town that is not a matter of order.
- **Sena Dunn of house 121** — the first household to put it to a Crown clerk, at the sixth hour of the eighteenth, unchosen, and gave her name to be written down.
- **Mrs Corris of house 133** — put **her own name** in the column on the twentieth, in the margin, in public, and it is the first name in that column in eleven years in this ward.
- **Ansel Stroud** — confirmed the column is **printed**, that the hand is the house's, that it is empty on every Kells return, that it is not empty in every district and he is prohibited from saying which; and said, unentered and in public, that the reason it is empty in this ward is that there is no room at four in the morning.
- **Sabin Fell** — **has never been told any of it.**
- **Nell's other count, the nine *not returned* lines and the six *accompanying* lines, is still unspent and still not public in any room, and Nell has now told a second person that she is holding it and is not going to spend it today.** Do not confuse it with the question about the ward's children, which is a separate silence and which the Crown has now asked out loud.
