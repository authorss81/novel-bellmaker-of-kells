# Continuity State

## Setting and series canon

- The setting is **Mereward**, a low-fantasy river country with nine historic bell wards. Kells is a highland market town where the Kells Water meets the old western road. Sennet is the Crown capital and the sealed First Answer chamber.
- The old **Hearthline** is a federated network built for local answers, physical redundancy, and withdrawal. The Crown's **Quiet Office** later uses Crown clappers to repeat one answer as if many people had freely consented. Those institutional mechanisms remain unrevealed through Chapter 20.
- Bell resonance carries one bounded claim that a living person has accepted responsibility for making. It is not mind reading, objective proof, prophecy, or compulsion. A future warning is possible because someone accepts the duty to warn before the danger arrives.
- Tomas is a skilled ordinary bellmaker who began the volume at Stage 0 (Hand) and is at the first edge of Stage 1 after one private binding in Chapter 7. **He gained no power in Chapters 11–20, none in Chapters 21–30, and none in Chapters 31–40.** His external want is to protect Kells, save the Vale Bellhouse, and find his missing mother. His internal need is to share responsibility and stop confusing usefulness with authority.
- **Tamsin Rook** is the only slow-burn romantic partner. **Mara Vale** is Tomas's estranged sister and a separate family relationship. Oren Pike, Sabin Fell, and Nell Fenn provide ongoing consent, grief, craft, and consequence pressure.
- The final antagonist is Chancellor **Seris Dain**, and the final resolution breaks the central clapper, frees living anchors including Lira, and creates a federation in which rulers cannot compel every answer. No later ending material has been introduced.

## Bell and route rules confirmed in prose through Chapter 30

- The fixed stripped Crown survey carrier is a waist-high, mounted physical object whose missing clapper does not make it self-directing or prophetic; a connected physical route and a narrow bypass drive its sound. It has been outside the Bellhouse since Chapter 10.
- A Crown survey carrier's ordinary survey clapper is distinct from the later political **Crown clapper**. The former was removed; no copied-answer mechanism has been exposed.
- A bound bell requires physically identifiable witness matter, a living keeper's specific accepted duty, and an informed answer. A private handbell may have one answer; a local public signal normally needs three independent answers.
- A future warning can be valid because a keeper accepts the duty to warn before the danger. The bell carries accepted responsibility, not objective truth, and it cannot compel a listener's decision.
- The Bellhouse route is physically connected to route L-4 through old copper service cuts, splices, and bypasses. The route can repeat a claim, but repetition does not prove free consent.
- **New in this batch — the public test carrier.** The market court's call bell is an **unbound** iron handbell: a rope, a striker on a thong, no wire beneath it, no groove in its crown, no wax. Jerm inspected it under, in the loft, and at the beam on Day 8. It was never bound, so it carries ordinary sound only; on Day 8 its job was to **count** the answers while the words and the record did the claiming, and its keeper was the court usher. This distinction — the bell counts, the people claim — is now on the page and must not be quietly reversed by later binding it.
- **New in this batch — a route's words and a bell's reach are different objects.** Chapter 12 sets Renn's written duty (*the words said at the gate are the words the keeper himself can support; nothing is added in the ward; nothing is added above the landing*) against the handbell's waxed crown and its reach, which Tamsin states she has known was longer than her route since March and which is not measured until Day 9. It establishes that a duty governs words while metal governs distance. A keeper may therefore refuse to forward a claim that concerns a thing he does not keep, and that refusal is a correct use of the rule rather than obstruction.
- **New in this batch — withdrawals are a craft record.** The Bellwrights' Measure's road map carries a dated, keyed mark for every ward that has ever taken its answers back since before the Crown laid a mile of copper; **fifty-three** are marked. This count is unrelated to the **forty-one** households on the L-4 answer schedule and the two numbers must never be conflated or read as one. A map shows where a line runs and cannot show whose hand altered it.

- **New in this batch — a mark in a response column is not an answer and can still be used as one.** Chapter 22 shows the mechanism: the officers read the office's own marks off the response column and treat them as the household's answer, beginning at line 22, the Fenn household, read as *six entered, two accompanying*. **Chapter 26 is where the alignment is counted**, on Nell Fenn's slate: nine lines read *not returned* and six read *accompanying*, and every household the Crown took a child from is on one of those fifteen lines, while every other marked door is on a plain line. **The Crown's own notes about people who were not in the house were read by the Crown as the answer.** Chapter 26 establishes the alignment from the ward board and by knocking, not from any office.
- **New in this batch — a bell's reach thins out instead of stopping.** Chapter 29 walks Tamsin's authorized route and rings once at seven points with Jerm and a slate. The bell answers inside the route, above it, forty yards above the landing stage, at the low drain gate itself felt through four feet of stone, and a mile out at the first cottage past the toll shed. **A courier therefore cannot find the edge of her own rule from inside the reach, so the bell cannot be trusted to carry even a keeper's boundary.** This is why she reduces her offer rather than trying to measure her way out.
- **New in this batch — a private handbell can lose the note and keep the strike.** Tamsin can still see a bell move, feel it in the stone, and ring it, and can no longer hear a note, which is the part a courier uses to carry words. **She cannot carry, and Chapter 29 states that no one in Kells is going to be told, because a courier who says her note is gone is a courier whose bell is free. She keeps it from Jerm through the seven-point walk: he is her witness and is on the slate, and he is never told what is wrong. She then tells Tomas alone, at the low drain gate at the fourth hour, as the first of three things given for free — *my hearing is worse than I have told anybody in this town except you*. **On Day 21 she breaks that rule herself, on purpose, and publishes the fact of it in a lane; see the deliberate canon change in the Chapter 40 section below. Nobody else in Kells had been told before that.**
- **New in this batch — the reversal, stated as the chapters state it.** Chapter 25: *"A bell does not know the future. A bell carries a claim that a living person has taken responsibility for."* Somebody in the office marked **OFF. 411** accepted a duty about an L-4 inspection, entered it in the response column of the schedule six days before the inspection order was written, and the bell carried that claim nine days later. **This is a documentary and civic discovery made from pages already in play. Tomas cannot hear or feel it and gains no power from it. How an office gets into a bell is explicitly unanswered and must stay unanswered until it is earned.**

## Bell and route rules confirmed in prose through Chapter 40 (Batch 0004 additions)

- **New in this batch — a carrier may not add anything, and the way to do that is to send the difficulty out deliberately.** Chapter 37: Sabin Fell's rule is that a bell is a mouth, that a thing which comes out of a bell a long way off arrives *thinner, rounder and more certain* than it went in, and that a man who sends only the true easy part has sent **a version and not a claim**. Tomas writes the uncertainty, the cause, his own fault, his own name and the hour **inside** the words of the claim, so that the hard part travels too. Chapter 38 shows what happens when the easy part is sent alone: four strikes of a true sentence in a lane in the dark is a version, and a market lane filled in the rest of it.
- **New in this batch — a carrier is the disagreement, not the bell.** Chapter 37: the fourth rule Sabin Fell has never given anybody in this county is **take the person most likely to refuse, and ask them first** — asked first because the dangerous answer has to arrive while there is still time to be frightened by it rather than argued with. The first answer is a claim; every answer after the first is a claim about the first; and a carrier carried by three people who are all certain is a carrier that has had its teeth taken out on the way to the market. **A bellmaker's own flaw against this rule is wanting an answer, and a wanting man puts the person he wants at the bottom of the list.** Tomas did exactly that for two days with Tamsin Rook, told her so in writing with the hour on it before he asked her anything, and moved her to the top. **The order of the asking is itself a record and the chapters record it.**
- **New in this batch — a plain carrier is one with nothing in it.** Chapter 37: a carrier that may not add anything should be a bell with no claim already in it, no wax, no dressed groove, and only the maker's mark and the date. The work is therefore not in the metal. It is in the four parts of the frame — physically identifiable and **findable** witness matter, a living keeper's specific accepted duty, an informed answer, and a carrier that may not add anything — and the three answers are the hard part, not the object. **Witness matter that no living person could locate in ten years is a relic and not a witness** (Sabin Fell, in the same chapter).
- **New in this batch — a bell's failure modes are ordinary and are recorded as ordinary faults.** Chapter 37: a bell may be **too bright** (it goes further than its maker wants and cannot be reduced, because a man who tunes a bell to stop at a distance is tuning it to a lie about how far it goes), and it may **repeat** (a bell within about eight feet of hard stone returns its own note a fraction later, and a thing that comes out twice is a claim and a copy of a claim, and a copy is how a sentence stops belonging to the person who said it). Chapter 37 also records a route that is not connected: the Bellwrights' cut to the market lane was cut by the Crown's own renewal trench on Day 15. **No chapter in this batch gives Tomas a perception of where a sound went, and Chapter 37 says so on the page: he rang it and did not know where it was heard.**
- **New in this batch — asking before the act has a public price, and Tomas pays it.** Chapter 37: the witness matter was one bar out of the ward's own drain grating, asked for at the ward board at the ninth hour, before the metal was melted, in front of about thirty people, with the whole of the claim read out and the whole of the cost stated first. The board gave it and Mercy Slee entered the gift, the date, the words and the cost in the ward's book, and two more people signed under her. **The cost is that the ward is now physically in a bell that a Crown register records as existing.** The answer to the batch's central question is therefore structural: *Tomas asked before the act and the act was still wrong*, and the wrongness was not in the asking.
- **New in this batch — a Crown form can be answered with a form.** Chapter 39: the Crown's answer to the ward's *Witnessed by* question is a **supplementary return** carrying the household's objection, the name of a person present at the answering, and **the reason field the summary form drops** — and nothing on it to say which hand wrote the reason. And the Crown's other instrument: **a door-to-door slate asking whether a household intends to attend in person**, which produces the same list the ward's procedure produces, and cheaper, and by being kind.
- **New in this batch — the procedure can be obeyed and used against the ward.** Chapter 40: the *Witnessed by* column is **not cleared between rounds**, so a line can hold the name of a person who is dead; the Crown puts that line in front of a door, calls it a witnessed answer, enters the household's objection in a margin in a clerk's own name and initialled, and proceeds, because an objection does not suspend the renewal. **This is the volume's argument at its sharpest: a form exhibited entire, a published procedure and a clerk with a conscience together make a round that is conducted properly and that is not the same thing as a round that is lawful.**
- **New in this batch — a watch captain can comply and refuse at once, and the refusal narrows him.** Chapter 35: Oren Pike accepts a seven-day bell register's hours, places and every bell, and refuses the **name**, on the stated ground that a register of the names of persons who sound bells, made by a Crown office in a ward in the week of a round, is a list of persons who can be removed from a route. He enters exactly what he did and did not do. **It is a protection and it is a leash**: the watch is now a record and not a route, and every woman in a doorway in the ward has been told in advance that the watch will come and write the hour and the place and will not stand there.
- **New in this batch — a list of people who have spoken in public is a list of citizens, and the Crown will rename a document rather than lose it.** Chapter 34: a district return asking for keepers *and* for all persons having public standing was answered with eleven names and dates and a **six-paragraph covering page, of which Oren read the first five to his two men before he signed it and the sixth after, and all six in open court**; the district **kept the list and amended the heading in its own margin** to *persons having public standing in matters of signalling, with dates*, and recorded that no further return as to keepers is required. The return went out with the afternoon rider and came back **the same evening at the eighth hour**, on the district's own horse. **The word *standing* now means, in this ward's records, that a person stood in a lane and was heard.** The chapter no longer says the Crown did not ask for keepers: it asked for both, and the keeper half is the half it can demand again, and the second half is the one it cannot.
- **DELIBERATE CANON CHANGE, Day 21 — Tamsin Rook's lost note is now public, by her own act.** The rule set in Chapter 29 was that no one in Kells was to be told, because a courier who says her note is gone is a courier whose bell is free, and she had told only Tomas. In Chapter 40 she states the fourth of her four reasons in a lane, and about nine people catch that it is the real one, and then, on her own initiative and at her own price, she says the rest of it out loud and tells about a hundred people to write it down, on the stated ground that **a thing said once by the person it belongs to is a record and a thing found out in a lane is a rumour**, and that the next person who wants her bell will otherwise have heard the rumour and not her. **Later chapters may use this.** What is not changed: she has still rung nothing, the bell is still hers, the route is still hers, and nothing has been rescued for her. What is new and live: her deafness is on the ward's record, and any Crown or private party who wants her bell can now price it correctly without her permission.
- **New in this batch — the Crown's instrument is procedural three times over.** Chapter 31: a form field headed *cause stated by*, completed within hours of a foreman's report being copied out fair. Chapter 32: a ground correction answered with a schedule and not a survey, and a Crown deputy who signs his own name against his own works order. Chapter 34: an amendment in a margin. **Nobody in this batch answered a Crown document with a document and won.**
