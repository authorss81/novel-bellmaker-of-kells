# Continuity State

## Setting and series canon

- The setting is **Mereward**, a low-fantasy river country with nine historic bell wards. Kells is Tomas Vale's home; Sennet is the Crown capital and the sealed First Answer chamber.
- The old **Hearthline** is a federated network built for local answers, physical redundancy, and withdrawal. The Crown's **Quiet Office** later uses Crown clappers to repeat one answer as if many people had freely consented. Those later institutional mechanisms remain unrevealed in Chapters 1–10.
- Bell resonance carries one bounded claim that a living person has accepted responsibility for making. It is not mind reading, objective proof, prophecy, or compulsion. A future warning is possible because someone accepts the duty to warn before the danger arrives.
- Tomas is a skilled ordinary bellmaker who began the volume at Stage 0 (Hand) and is now at the first edge of Stage 1 after one private binding. His external want is to protect Kells, save the Vale Bellhouse, and find his missing mother. His internal need is to share responsibility and stop confusing usefulness with authority.
- **Tamsin Rook** is the only slow-burn romantic partner. **Mara Vale** is Tomas's estranged sister and a separate family relationship. Oren Pike, Sabin Fell, and Nell Fenn provide ongoing consent, grief, craft, and consequence pressure.
- The final antagonist is Chancellor **Seris Dain**, and the final resolution breaks the central clapper, frees living anchors including Lira, and creates a federation in which rulers cannot compel every answer. No later ending material has been introduced in this batch.

## Bell and route rules confirmed in prose

- The fixed stripped Crown survey carrier is a waist-high, mounted physical object. Its missing clapper does not make it self-directing or prophetic; a connected physical route and a narrow bypass can drive its sound.
- A Crown survey carrier's ordinary survey clapper is distinct from the later political **Crown clapper**. The former was removed; no copied-answer mechanism has been exposed.
- A bound bell requires physically identifiable witness matter, a living keeper's specific accepted duty, and an informed answer. A private handbell may have one answer; a local public signal normally needs three independent answers.
- A future warning can be valid because a keeper accepts the duty to warn before the danger. The bell carries accepted responsibility, not objective truth, and it cannot compel a listener's decision.
- The Bellhouse route is physically connected to route L-4 through old copper service cuts, splices, and bypasses. The route can repeat a claim, but repetition does not prove free consent.

## Batch 0001 established facts

- Pever is the **Crown surveyor**, not the clerk. He arrived at the Bellhouse an hour after the two porters who delivered the fixed carrier before first light, accompanied by a clerk. Chapter 1 and Chapter 8 agree on this: Tomas asks for "the surveyor who signs for it" when the carrier rings, and the handwritten line on the repair order reads "keep clear of the west road until the surveyor arrives." Do not reintroduce "the clerk who delivered it" — the delivery was by porters.
- The carrier has a child-sized wax handprint, a warped lip, a missing survey clapper, and damage beneath the Bellhouse. A thin copper jumper remains across a near-cut coupling.
- A Crown renewal inspection for the Low Ward on route L-4 was filed three days before the carrier rang. Its heading reads **"dawn, seventh day from posting"**, so the original dawn falls on the morning after Chapter 10. Do not shorten this interval without also re-anchoring the Chapter 10 deadline.

Tomas leaves **without** learning the order's date from the deputy. The order-before-the-bell reversal lands in **Chapter 5**, where Tomas reads the date off the Fenn household fragment itself and checks it against his own day-book, and becomes public and citable in **Chapter 6**, where the clerk writes the date and route onto a public board. Chapter 5 makes no reference to the market-court board, which Tomas has not visited yet; the hidden small line under the L-4 heading is discovered only in Chapter 6. Do not assert precedence in Chapter 2 or earlier.
- Nell Fenn is sixteen, an apprentice who repairs small bells. Her younger brother Jory is eight and her father Edan Fenn is also missing. Nell's counts are stated relative to her own testimony in Chapter 5, which is Day 1: Jory was taken "six days ago" and Edan disappeared "five days ago," which places them on **Day −5 and Day −4**. Both still precede the Day −3 notice, so the Chapter 5 inference holds. Keep both framings straight: witness-relative (six and five days before Chapter 5) and carrier-relative (five and four days before the carrier rang).
- Jory and Edan answered the **previous renewal round**, not the L-4 posting. Chapter 5 anchors this: Low Ward rolls are recounted on a published cycle, the board posts a round a week ahead, and Nell's paper is "a renewal check" while Edan went "to answer a renewal summons." Chapter 4 has Nell say her father went "to answer a Crown summons," not a notice. Do not let any character refer to a posted L-4 notice before Day −3, and do not call the earlier round the L-4 round.
- Tamsin recovered her handbell from the cooper's loft after a child opened the north-gate lockbox with a maintenance key. The handbell is **no wider than her palm across the crown** and was physically altered to widen a Low Ward signal beyond its authorized route. Tamsin retains it. In Chapter 3 she unwraps it once on the top step of the Bellhouse stair, in full view of the lane, and Tomas leans over it from the doorway to read the altered wear without touching the bell. **No watch is present**: that reading is a private, unwatched exchange. The **watch-supervised comparison** that Oren arranged earlier in Chapter 3 remains outstanding and must not be treated as already performed.
- A Crown lock register records a master key signed out under the name “J. Vale.” A later maintenance tag reads “L-4, third watch, J. V.” The echo is recorded as a clue, not proof that Tomas or his family used the key. No witness, name, or suspect is attached to “J. V.” in prose; do not invent one retroactively.
- A service ticket found in the partly cleared survey store says the fixed carrier's clapper was removed and the carrier was disconnected from the active line after a warning was filed; the route was held for maintenance review, and no keeper answer was recorded. This is a physical/mundane clue, not the later copied-answer reveal.
- Halden Crake is acting locally under a Crown inspection commission when he presents the temporary holding writ. Oren and Halden sign a narrow joint receipt for only the fixed carrier. The pending-hearing clause delays the inspection by seven days; it does not transfer ownership, cancel the operation, or authorize seizure of the other bells.
- The **missing local surveyor** is named on paper. In Chapter 8 Oren reads the route docket aloud and says the withdrawn service entry's name is **Iven Sore**; Tomas answers "The Bellwrights' Measure master?" and Oren confirms only that "the name on the service ticket is Iven Sore," that the roll has no current entry for him, and that there is a Crown seal but no living witness to the withdrawal. The live ambiguity is therefore **not** whether the name exists but whether it is genuine: Tomas proposes "a clerk using his name" and Oren answers "Could be." In the bible, Iven Sore is the living master of the Bellwrights' Measure, so if the name is genuine the guild master and the missing surveyor are one man. Do not have any character deny that the name is on the docket, and do not resolve the genuine-or-borrowed question by assumption.
- **Three surveyor roles exist. Keep them distinct.** (1) **Pever** is the delivering and certifying Crown surveyor who worked the carrier on Day 0 and asked for an official mechanic to be sent from Sennet; he is present, not missing. (2) The **docket surveyor** of Chapter 8 is the person whose service entry was withdrawn and who has no current roll entry, named on paper as Iven Sore. (3) Halden's **inspection's surveyor**, "delayed on the north road" in Chapter 2 and explicitly *not* the man who came to the workshop, is a third person and is never named. The rescheduled dawn inspection on Day 10 needs this third surveyor, and Chapters 11–20 may finally show them arriving without retroactively identifying them as the missing docket surveyor.

## Power and costs

- Tomas remains Stage 0 until the Chapter 7 private binding and then reaches only the first edge of Stage 1 (Near Toll).
- The binding chain is explicit: a sliver of the market-court L-4 posting as witness matter; Tomas as keeper; Nell as the one informed private answer. The sliver is wedged into the trial handbell's wooden frame against the crown, never wrapped around the bell and never touching the rim, and can be lifted out with two fingers. Tomas can sense only that Nell's answer is strained. He cannot identify the person, emotion, source, copied answer, or future event.
- Costs paid in Batch 0001: one failed small casting, which was remelted; one split tuning file; one episode of ringing ears. There is no permanent memory loss, deep read, archive impression, or forced-echo detection.
- The trial handbell remains bound to its narrow claim and cannot carry a second claim until the duty is answered or deliberately unmade.
- Chapter 9's perception of the initiating pulse is ordinary craft diagnosis: Tomas has his fingers on a buried bypass and times the vibration against the gate bell. It is not supernatural reading, and the text says so in the moment.
- Sabin deliberately declines to name the shape of a route failure beyond relay distortion (Chapter 4). The failure class that lets one official answer present itself as a whole settlement's answer is still untaught and unexposed, and belongs to Chapters 21–30.
