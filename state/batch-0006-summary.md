## Batch result

A man with a bell in a cradle he cannot ring walks eleven days up a river road with a woman who cannot hear a bell, and the thing following them is **not a conspiracy in a room.** It is **a column, a stamp and a habit**, and the habit is worse than a villain because **it only needs somebody to be competent.** A woman at a water bell is told what the Crown has done with her bell and is told by the man who failed to tell her. A household of six is found answering a Crown form through a signature nobody lifted a pen for. A smith past a county stone says the sentence Sabin Fell said in a room over a fish market, and has never heard of him. A map has a panel cut out of every copy in the same place, and the panel is the one that says where an answer goes. A clause Tomas wrote himself in a watch house at the fifth hour is used on him at the second hour in an inn yard, and his own fifth clause is what bounds it. A district officer answers three of four questions in his own name within the hour and refuses the fourth with a reason, and files the refusal where a person can ask for it, and nobody has ever asked for one. Nine doors refuse, and one of the nine is the man who has been signing for the rest. And a ferry-man says two words on his way up a path and is gone.

**No bell is rung anywhere in these ten chapters.**

## What is established, in the order the chapters establish it

1. **A carrier's book carries who carried a thing and how far, and may not carry the owner, a debt, or a price. A Crown roll carries what a household answered.** They are different objects and were never the same one. *People are not parcels and always were not.* A clerk with a list and a copyist can fill a Crown list in on a road by asking nobody anything, and the chapter that carries this also says the man behind the wheel **cannot remember agreeing to it**.
2. **The Ocklebridge water bell is on a Fourmile schedule as a bell of that ward with the keeper's field empty, and nobody told her for thirty years.** The field is empty because the form has four fields and none of them fits a water bell. **A village can be entered in a schedule it never answered to.** The debt is paid on Day 35, badly, and the woman is right about the paying, and he writes *a receipt is not a permission* in his own book.
3. **A household answers once, in one hand, and the office carries the hand forward for six years, lawfully, because a certified answer is a return.** The instrument is a **supplementary return** with a reason field, a box for *the name of a person present at the answering*, and the small print *a statement returned before the closing hour of the period stated above will be certified*. The district takes it in, stamps it, **sends it back to the house to keep**, and the next year's form arrives with the last certified answer and a line reading **confirmed by the household**. Nobody falsifies anything.
4. **A trade already keeps, for its own reasons, the three things an office needs — who lives where, who buys, and who signed.** A district cart brought a sheet of four wards and four streets into a smith's shop four years ago, lawfully, paid with a Crown document, and the shop copied it into a book and gave it back. **The Crown's roll is not built out of stolen paper.** The clerk who made his own list four years ago is the same clerk a woman with a bolt of cloth described on the thirty-fourth, and nobody joins them up.
5. **A bell is traceable when the metal of it can be shown to have come from a source that keeps a record of what it sold and to whom** — said in a smith's shop past a county stone by a man who has never heard of the Bellwrights' Measure, and said in a Measure room in Kells by Sabin Fell, and **Tomas Vale is the only man in the kingdom who has heard both.**
6. **The fifth panel of the public road sheet — the panel that names the office where a return is addressed and the days it goes out and comes back — is gone from every copy, in the same rectangle, in the middle of a run.** Not an edition, not a fire. A hand, on the plate. Tamsin Rook saw it at a counter and Tomas Vale saw it four minutes late on a barrow, having looked at the tide table.
7. **The method, in Tamsin Rook's words: ask what a document *is*, not who is on it, and then make the clerk write the answer down in his own name.** A thing a clerk holds is not a fact about anything. A thing a clerk has said can be looked at by the next man and by the person you hand it to.
8. **A clause written to stop an office taking a thing can be used by a person with a pen and an hour, and it will work on the man who asked for the clause.** Tomas wrote it at about the fifth hour of the morning of the thirty-third and wrote down the night before that it would be used. It is used at about the second hour in an inn yard four counties on, by a man who gives no name and has no witness, and it is not refused, and the object is produced in its cradle in front of eleven people and nothing is broken. **The fifth clause is what makes the request checkable, because it is the reason a requester must be told out loud, in a room, what he may not do with the object.**
9. **A roll is not a return, and no Crown form in a district that holds one has a field for it.** Marten Cope says so in his own name, refuses the fourth question in writing, and says the office has no instrument for a roll at all, asked for one in the ninth year, was refused, and did not ask again. He files the refusal in a book of two hundred and forty entries **where any person may ask for it, which nobody has ever done before.**
10. **Eight of nine households refuse the question and one of the eight is the man who has been signing for the rest.** Hesk Rann has filled in about nine hundred Crown forms in nine years for people who cannot write, does not charge, has never once been asked, and carries one household's answer between a district and a man two counties over twice a year so that the man signs a form about a household he has not lived in for six years and does not know he is signing. **His hand is on a Crown form, on a smith's bill, and on four returns in a file in Fourmile, and no law anywhere says a man may not be the hand on both.**

## The batch's method, stated four times in four ways and it is the same method

A carrier's book will not carry an owner. A ward's ward-board answer will not merge three columns into a sentence. A yard book will carry a refusal of a name in its own column. An officer's book of refusals will carry a refusal where a person can ask for it. **A refusal is a sentence and a sentence gets merged, and the only defence in this batch is a column of its own.** Tamsin Rook states the batch's version of it in Chapter 59 as three questions put nine times in the same words, the third of which is *may I write your no as a no and not as nothing*, and she says where she got it, and it is not her own.

## The one thing the batch found and did not explain

**Six certified returns in one household's drawer. One name. One hand. Five different dates. And the brother-in-law has not been in the house for six years.** Tomas Vale records that he does not know how the dates got there and that he is not going to guess. **This is a live thread and is not a continuity error.** It is the first thing on the page that a clerk's record cannot account for, and it is the reason the batch ends downstream and not in Fourmile.
