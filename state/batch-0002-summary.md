# Batch 0002 Summary — Chapters 11–20

**Volume:** 01 — *The Bell Before the Crime*  
**Batch:** 0002  
**Status:** Complete

## Batch result

Kells spent the seven-day pending-hearing stay learning that a public warning cannot be built out of one person's usefulness. Tomas began with three blank witness slips and no lawful bell to put them near. The trial handbell is bound and under a no-ringing order, the loose relay bells are named in the same order, and the north-gate watch bell is a Crown instrument on a live line. The only candidate left was the **market court's plain call bell**, an unbound iron handbell the court ushers ring to call an assembly, and the court clerk refused to lend it for words nobody in the room had accepted. That refusal became the design pressure: the bell had to wait for a keeper of standing, which is what produced Saturday's public test.

The four-part test frame — physical witness, keeper's duty, three independent answers, and a carrier that may not add anything — was built by Oren and Tomas in Chapter 11. Sabin supplied the craft rule that governs it: write the claim once, read the same words to each person, write what they actually said, and never treat two matching answers as a stronger answer than a third that disagrees. Five witnesses were invited to the market court. **Bevin Sarr refused**, and his reason was civic and specific: a slip with his name on it saying *public answer* would be filed in the same office as the ward list that already holds his unpaid entry, and a paper in a file is a thing other men read out loud in a room he is not standing in. Oren recorded the refusal in Sarr's own words, unsigned at Sarr's request. Tomas's instinct was to find a wording that turned the no into a yes; he did not.

The flood ran as a three-day arc. On Day 4 Tamsin took the handbell to the low gate and proved that its reach exceeds its authorized route: the wax laid over the older crown groove carries a Low Ward note past the river landing, past its own boundary, into houses that never chose to hear it. She compared that physical fact against **Hollis Renn's written gate duty** — *the words said at the gate are the words the keeper himself can support; nothing is added in the ward; nothing is added above the landing* — and showed that a line about words cannot govern a distance. **Wyman Dace**, keeper of the west road bridge, accepted a promise to walk the west road and report before evening, in his own words on a slate, and explicitly not a promise that the bridge was safe. Renn accepted only his own duty: he would not carry a road claim, and he would not ring until the gate had been walked, the windlass turned twice under load, and he had read the mark himself in the hour before. The gate's pawl was slipping with gravel packed behind the sill, so the ring came late and four houses at the foot of the Low Ward took water to table height. **The gate remains a physical maintenance problem, not a solved device.**

Nell gathered three Low Ward answers on Day 4 in the sequence after Tamsin's morning: Foreman Crane accepted a duty to carry words to the landing and no further, Wenna Cray asked for time until she can find the round paper covering the woman who went looking for her sister, and Garn Fisk refused the route outright and refused to be named. Her mother would not let her answer for the household. Nell published only the fact that three different choices were made, pinned the exact question to the ward board so nobody could say they were asked something else, and refused to let the market-court Crown officer record it. Tomas did not call the refusal cowardice and did not ask her to change it.

**Iven Sore**, living master of the Bellwrights' Measure, appears for the first time. He inspected the trial handbell without sounding it, because the no-ringing order is in force, and entered that limitation himself. He lifted the L-4 posting sliver out of the bell's frame with two fingers — it never touches the rim and is removable by anyone — and called the wedging correct. He offered a certified record, a copy of the Measure's road map, a seat in the room where a route is built, and refused to say a straight word about the withdrawn docket: he can support that he never held the L-4 commission, **cannot support** the rest, and will answer in a Kells Measure room with a witness in eleven days, which the seven-day stay will not allow. The Measure's road map revealed that the craft has kept a dated record of local **withdrawals** since before the Crown laid a mile of copper, forty-one of them; a map shows where a line runs, a hand does the altering. The guild issued a **conditional challenge**, not a ban: the material inspection, plus a published withdrawal procedure lodged at the market court. Tomas refused a master key and any licence making the Bellhouse answerable for every route answer. Sabin was asked for a professional opinion and refused, and the Measure recorded *no opinion offered* — the refusal that keeps his name off the guild record.

Halden escalated. On Day 6 he brought an inventory and sealing order. Oren separated four object classes aloud in the yard, sealed only the eight connected working bells, put his own watch mark on the same wax over the Crown seal so it would take two hands to remove, recorded the rest, and entered that nothing in the inventory moves ownership or makes a private answer into a public one. **The watch-supervised comparison Oren arranged back in Chapter 3 was finally performed** in that yard: Tamsin unwrapped her handbell herself, Tomas read the wear from a distance without touching it, and the record states that the wax widens a signal beyond its authorized route, that this is a fact about metal, and that it is **not** evidence the Low Ward agreed to anything. No custody changed and the route was not disclosed. The miller took his daughter's bell to a Miremarket maker; the chandler's work went with Nell's refusal; the yard door now carries the no-ringing notice, the Crown's wax, and Oren's mark.

Tomas came close to hiding the trial handbell in the casting-room cellar stair and did not, because Tamsin named exactly what a secret removal would do to the record. Oren then required the public account of the Hush rope cut before the town would be asked to answer him, and in the market court in the rain Tomas stated that he cut the frame's lifting line to reach a trapped child, that the cut is the reason the warning ended, and that he cannot prove what the tone would have done had the line held. **Wen Anner**, the child he saved and now a cooper's apprentice of twenty-four, refused to be used as absolution or as condemnation and had it entered that he is angry at both. A Low Ward witness entered that a thin sound followed the frame's fall and that people moved because of what they saw, weakening Tomas's private belief without clearing him. Oren imposed three conditions on any public signal: name the known, the unknown, and the next keeper inside the words; any household or keeper may withdraw with no reason, no fee, and no consequence, recorded as a withdrawal and never as silence; and a signal of this kind is not a municipal act. **Gib Hask** entered the book demanding the Crown's buried line be pulled out of the ward, and Oren recorded that the ward holds both a party wanting the wire gone and a party refusing the loss of its only warning, unreconcilable by a hearing.

On Day 7 the water crested. Tomas measured an undercut at the north end of the west road's low section, found the Crown road gang's cart across the culvert with the foreman's signature on it, and issued one bounded claim naming the second mark, the cart, the undercut, and every sentence about what he could not tell anybody. Three answers came back and none agreed: Dace accepted, Cray accepted only with the Crown's cart stated in it and only as far as the ward board, Renn refused to carry anything. Some families moved goods, some waited and lost a day, and Bevin Sarr made a Crown officer say in front of eleven witnesses that the works order held the cart and that he had used the word *Crown* twice. Tomas's cost was physical: a loss of balance in the crowded lane, ringing ears, and the market's morning trade lost.

The **public three-answer test** ran on Day 8 with the market court's call bell, its keeper the court usher, not Tomas. The claim was read twice, in identical words. Three answers, three withdrawals, and two doors withdrawn. Foreman Crane accepted with a boundary and then used Tomas's own published procedure to withdraw from two doors on his row, Wyman Dace asked for time until he had walked the road again and returned before the court closed with a scoped acceptance entered as an amendment in his own column, and Bevin Sarr refused the route and agreed to have the refusal read aloud unsigned. Wenna Cray and Hollis Renn both withdrew before the count — Cray to the ward board, Renn in four words — so Tomas's three answers were not the three he had collected the day before. **Sena Dunn withdrew in open court before the count**, after her agreement had been put on the page that morning, because two Crown officers and a case-man were in the arcade and her name was already on two rolls; Tomas began to build her leaving into a fourth answer he could use and Nell stopped him — *she is not a silence you get to fill*. The usher counted one, two, three on the iron, including the refusal, and refused to count Dace's late amendment again, so a listener at the back could count the decline with the promises. When a man shouted that the Crown was calling the ward, the usher refused a fourth stroke of Tomas's and Oren answered in words, not sound. **Halden's best argument was entered in the record on his own objection**: he is not there to stop the assembly, he is there because if a child drowns the Crown will send a clerk to ask whether the ward was warned, and he wants a record that says it was, and does not care who rings the bell. **Surveyor Rhea Collis** finally arrived off the north road, eleven days delayed by an unpayable ferry, certified the rescheduled dawn, and stated she was not the officer who certified the carrier and never held the L-4 entry; her line is on the roll with no withdrawal, so the docket name is still only Iven Sore.

By evening the ward's version had already collapsed to two answers both agreeing. A Crown clerk arrived with an order treating the three-answer record as a **single local response** on route L-4 and demanding the slips at the eighth hour. The next morning, **Deputy Crake posted an emergency custody hearing for the first hour of light on Day 10, before the rescheduled dawn, over the whole of the workshop's signal apparatus**, on the ground that a workshop which can sound an answer in the ward without the ward's agreement is more dangerous than before, not less. On Day 9 Tamsin logged the handbell's true reach with Jerm — of eleven people between the north gate and the landing, seven heard it and four did not; of five asked on the ferry road past the authorized end, three heard it; and the second cottage past the toll shed heard nothing at all — refused to let the bell add an answer or turn a refusal into silence, and set a written rule that the bell repeats only, rings only with a named person walking beside it who can say what it means, and rings for the whole of a record or none of it. Tomas asked instead of assigning, promised not to go around her again, and wrote his own habit into the record. She walked the towpath as far as the toll shed and told him a village will hear it late and one house will not hear it at all.

Chapter 20 ends on the evening of Day 9 with the dawn unresolved. Tomas burned a promise sentence that would have covered the whole town, took the duller sentence Tamsin dictated, and watched Oren open the L-4 file in a room with a Crown copyist in it. The file holds six sheets. A **service ticket** now reads, beneath its copy, *answered in office, L-4 ward roll, per local practice* — not *no answer*. A **Crown works order** for the low-turn culvert is dated the day after the ward was warned at the court. And a schedule headed *ROUTE L-4 — RESPONSES ENTERED BEFORE SERVICE* lists **forty-one households**, each with one mark in a response column, entered by an **office number with no signature**, dated the day each round was *posted*. Line twenty-two is the **Fenn household, six persons**, with Jory and Edan among the six and two marked *accompanying* — where the ward's own fragment says three, with a fourth line cut away. The clerk entered what the page says and refused the conclusion. Tomas entered the count, the office number, and the date, and would not draw the line for the town.

## Immediate next obligation

The emergency custody hearing convenes at the first hour of light on Day 10, one hour before the rescheduled dawn inspection, and Surveyor Rhea Collis is due at the north gate at four. The three answer columns, the withdrawal column, the withdrawal procedure, and the L-4 schedule must be in the room before the Crown files its merged packet. Tomas must survive a legal morning without turning one divided warning into one municipal answer, and the schedule's office number has to be turned into a person who can answer.

## Object custody and condition after Chapter 20

- **Fixed stripped Crown survey carrier:** waist-high, mounted, no clapper, child-sized wax handprint, route damage beneath the workshop. In the Kells **watch store**, upright in its mounting frame in a reinforced padded cradle, the retaining bolts still released from the Chapter 10 transfer, the red watch seal tied around the lower brace. Crown ownership unresolved. Never returned to the Bellhouse in this batch. Day 10 is the end of the joint receipt in any practical sense.
- **Tomas's trial handbell:** at the Vale Bellhouse on its bench under a cloth, under the written no-public-ringing order, **never rung** in this batch. Still bound to the sliver of the market-court L-4 posting wedged against the crown, paper not touching the rim, liftable with two fingers; Iven Sore lifted it out and put it back on Day 5. It **cannot carry a second claim** until the duty is answered or deliberately unmade, and the public test used a separate carrier, so it remains unspent. Inspected neutrally by the Measure on Day 5 with the material record filed and the limitation that it was not sounded.
- **Tamsin's stolen courier handbell:** in Tamsin's possession throughout, hand-sized, no wider than her palm across the flattened crown, portable, wax laid over an older signal in the crown groove. Compared **twice** now: once privately on the Bellhouse step (Chapter 3) and once **under watch supervision in the Bellhouse yard on Day 6**, where the wear was read without touching the bell and no custody changed. Logged reach on Day 9: too far and not far enough. Now governed by her written rule. She retains full authority over it and over her route.
- **Other Bellhouse bells:** twenty-six objects on the shelves, of which **eight connected bells are sealed with Crown wax and Oren's watch mark together** and held in the survey store for the term of the hearing. The other eighteen, including the millrace bridge bell, are **recorded, not removed**, and stay under the no-ringing order; the millrace bell may be rung only for its own millrace and only by Tomas or a person he names in writing. The yard door carries three things at once: the no-ringing notice, the Crown wax, and Oren's mark.
- **Market court call bell:** unbound iron, leather-wrapped handle, striker on a thong, no wire under it and none in the crown, kept by the market court in its loft rack. **Keeper: the court usher.** It rang four strokes on Day 8 — one to call the court and three counted answers — and it is now the subject of a Crown claim that it is a connected route.
- **The columned record:** three answers in three columns of the market court book, a fourth column for the withdrawals (Wenna Cray, Hollis Renn, Sena Dunn), a fifth for Foreman Crane's two withdrawn doors, with Wyman Dace's late answer entered as an *amended* line in his own column. Copies sit in the watch archive and in a covered box on the ward board. The court usher rang four strokes on Day 8: one to call the court and three counted answers.
- **Low flood gate:** a physical maintenance failure. Pawl slipping, gravel packed behind the sill. Cleared with one watchman's help; the gate rose late and four houses took water.

## Ordinary-practice costs paid in this batch

The ledger keeps costing Tomas, and customers choose to leave rather than being punished.

- Day 3: the flood bell promised for the first morning and paid for before sunrise stays untestable under the no-ringing order; the day-book's lost column exceeds its paid column.
- Day 5: a Crown officer blocks the bell's trip to the guild at the court arch; Jerm reads the order and lets it pass, and the officer logs a complaint. The Measure's inspection record carries a limitation because the bell was not sounded. The withdrawal procedure takes two evenings of bench time instead of paid work.
- Day 6: the miller takes his daughter's feast bell to a maker in Miremarket, who charges more, because he will not have a Crown mark on the rim at his daughter's feast; he asks for a note when the mark is off and may return. The chandler's work goes when Nell declines to keep it coming. Eight working bells leave the shelves. The **lower store's bellmetal stock was dampened** by the open maintenance channel during the rain and had to be dried, with the lead blooming on it — a direct constraint on any new casting. Tomas loses the days of the flood, the court, the guild, and the inventory as bench time, four days running.
- Day 7: six stalls lose the market day's trade to the closed low lane and the market court must pay them from a fund with three weeks in it; the chandler loses his flour and a household's pig is drowned in a cart.
- Day 9: Tamsin turns down two paid courier runs to pay for the four separate public copies and the slate.

## Working day-count after Chapter 20

The L-4 notice was filed and posted on **Day −3**; Chapter 6 states no elapsed figure, and the only counting rule on the page is the market-court clerk's inclusive addition (*the posting day counting as the first*). The original dawn was **Day 3**. The Chapter 10 pending-hearing clause delayed it seven days, making the rescheduled dawn **Day 10 — ten days after the carrier first rang**. Chapters 11–20 occupy **Day 3 through Day 9**, seven days, one stay, and end on the evening of Day 9 with the dawn still ahead.

Jory and Edan Fenn answered the **previous renewal round**, not the L-4 posting. Nell's Day 4 count places Jory at **Day −5** and Edan at **Day −4**, and she says the L-4 notice was pinned two days after Jory was taken and one day after Edan went. In Chapter 20 the L-4 schedule dates the Fenn response to the day the round was **posted**, which is a Crown page and not a ward record, and no character may treat it as proof of anything but the page.

**Reckoning 619 Hush chronology is fixed and unchanged:** the Hush of Kells happened twelve years before the present. Tomas's rope cut ended a warning that had already reached Ewan Pike, who said it told the ward to clear the lower road; a Low Ward witness in Chapter 16 entered that a thin sound followed the frame's fall and that people moved because of what they saw. Oren's account of the *twelve days* he has spent carrying the belief that the sound simply stopped is now corrected in the record, in his own hand, at his own insistence.
