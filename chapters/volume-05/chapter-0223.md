# Chapter 0223 — A Mend Is the Shape of the Hole, and a Woman in This Reach Is a Name in a Line and Not a Person

It was the first hour of the two hundred and ninth day and Gerta Poynter was mending a net on her own step with the light behind her and her back to the water, and she had been at it since the twelfth the night before, which is not a thing a woman at a door does unless the net is going to be wanted.

There were about eleven nets hung on lines behind her and a basket at her knee and a netting needle through a netting shuttle and a twine that goes through a mesh in a way that a person who has not been shown it cannot watch without following it. She mends the same mesh the same way. She has never once mended a hole from the other side, and when a man has asked her why she has said that the far side of a hole is a different hole.

---

At about the second hour Tomas Vale came up the second mile on foot and the barrow stood a hundred yards down, and he came to the step and stopped about nine foot off it because a woman at a door with a needle in her hand is a woman working and a man with a barrow is not part of it.

"There is no door open," she said.

"There is no door open."

"**Then you are a man standing nine foot off a step in the cold, and there are about four things a man at this step wants and about three of them are about a woman who went into the ground about twelve years ago, and I am going to tell you now that there is nobody at this door who will discuss that, and that is not me being hard, it is that I have had it said to me at about four doors on this water and every one of the four of them was a woman who thought she was the only one who had been asked.**"

---

At about the fifth hour they were sitting on the step with the net across both their knees, because she had said he could sit and had not said he could touch it, and about nine foot of the mend lay between his hands and hers, and neither of them said one word about what a net is for.

"Go on, then. Ask it."

"I am not going to ask it."

"That is the first sensible thing anybody has said at this step in about nine days." She went on with the shuttle. "**You have come up this water with your sleeves rolled and I have seen four of you, and not one of you ever asked for the name. Not one. And that is the finding, and it took me about nine years to be sure of it, and it is a strange finding and it is not about anybody being careful.**"

"What is it about, then."

"**About the shape of the question.** A mend is not a decision. A tear goes apart along the same threads every time you put it down the same way, and so the mend goes in along the same threads every time, and the man who comes to mend it after me puts the same shape in and the same shape would go in again. You can go round nine miles of water and ask nine men whether anybody has ever asked about a woman who went into the ground, and every one of them will say no, and every one of them will be able to tell you the shape of it, and not one of the four of them will have the name in it."

"That is what I am asking about. The shape of it."

"No it is not. The shape of it is what I am giving you and you did not ask for it and I am giving it to you because you sat down and did not reach." She pulled the shuttle through. "**I have been asked about her about four times in nine years and every one of the four times was the same three questions in the same order and the name was never in any of them.** Did anybody see her go. Was there a hole. Was there a man in the row who was there that night. Three questions and a name left out of all three. And I have answered about four of them and I am not going to answer the fifth, and I am not going to answer you either, and if you were somebody else you would get the same, and if you had come up with two men behind you you would get less."

---

At about the sixth hour the light had gone off the water and she turned the net over and he did not get to see the far side of the hole, which she noticed.

"You wanted to look at the other side," she said.

"I wanted to see whether the mend is the same both ways."

"**No. And the reason is that a name in a line is a name in a line.**" She set the needle down. "I will tell you what that is because you have not heard it and I would rather it came from a woman with a needle than from a man with a book. On this water there is a place where a person's name can go so that somebody can ask them to hold a line, and I have seen the page. **It is a column, ruled, in a hand, and a name goes in it, and when the name is in it, that is all that has happened. A name in a column is a standing. It is not a woman. It is not a house and it is not a trade and it is not a person anybody at a door will discuss, and I have known about four people on this reach and I could not tell you one thing about a single one of them, and I know their trades.**"

"That is what I came up this water not understanding."

"It is the only thing anybody comes up this water not understanding and about four of you have got it now and about forty of you have not and are not going to." She picked the needle up again. "**A column holds a standing and a standing is not a person, and a person is not what a column holds, and if you ever want to get from one to the other you will find there is no step.**"

---

There was a gap while she finished a row of the net and put the work down on her knee and turned it over, and he saw the far side of the hole for about as long as it took her to do that, and the mend was not the shape on the near side. It was the same hole, done the other way round, and it took about three times as many passes, and she did not look at him looking.

"That is the other side of a hole," she said. "**It is not the other side of the same mend and I am not going to be asked to explain the difference twice.**"

At about the seventh hour she asked him for it, and she asked him properly, and she watched him while he decided, and she had put the needle down before she did it, which he noticed afterwards and did not think about until much later in the week.

"I am going to ask you for a name, because nobody else is going to and somebody ought to."

"**No.**"

"Thank you." She said it flat, and went back to the shuttle, and did not press it and did not explain. "**I asked you because a man who refuses is doing me a service and a man who gives it to me is doing me an injury, and I would rather have the refusal, and about nine men on this water have given me the other thing and I have had to hold it for them since.** You have a name of a woman in that coat. I have not asked where you got it and I am not going to, and I have known there was one since the second hour, and I am telling you that I knew and that I did not ask, and that I would rather be a woman who knew and did not ask than a woman who did not know, and that is a different kind of woman and I know it is a worse one."

"You know which four people on this reach are in a column."

"I know their trades and I know which way they walk and I could tell you the mile they live on. I do not know a name and I am not going to be given one and I would not use it." She looked up. "**And that is the last of the three questions, and you have had all three of them in about nine minutes, and you did not have to ask for any of them, and now you know what everybody on this water knows, and you are going to go off down this mile with it and there is not one thing you can do with it, and I have watched four men do that already.**"

---

At about the ninth hour of the evening there was a barrow on flat ground above the lower end with the four bolts made fast, and about nine hundred yards up the second mile a woman was on her own step mending a net with the light behind her, and she had said three questions out loud that she had been asked four times in nine years and had given a man with a barrow the whole of the shape of them and had taken a refusal for an answer and had been given nothing for either.

Tomas Vale had a column and a mend and a shape, and the three were the same shape, and the name in the middle of all of them was on a piece of paper in his own coat and he had not said it, had not been asked for it by anybody except a woman with a needle who had asked in order to be refused, and had not written it anywhere tonight, and had been told on a step that the distance between a name in a line and a person is not a step and cannot be crossed by anybody with a barrow.

He sat on the ground at the end of the step for about as long as it takes for the light to go off a net and thought about the four people she could name by trade, and about the fact that he could not have named one of them, and about the fact that he had spent three weeks on nine miles of water working out that a column and a person are two things, and that the woman who had told him so had been mending the same hole in the same shape for about nine years and had not once been asked to explain it, and that the reason he was the second one to be told and not the twentieth was that he had been asking the wrong nine men and had got nine sentences off them and had told none of them he was getting them, and that this was the same fault as the taking and he had not known it was the same fault until about a quarter of an hour ago.

He wrote the six things down first, because they do not change in a week, and then he wrote three lines under a bar and did not cross the bar out, and the three lines were: a mend is the shape of the hole and the shape is not a decision; a column holds a standing and a standing is not a person; and a name asked for is an injury to the woman asked and a service to the asker, which is the first time in a hundred and thirty days he has had a rule that ran the other way from the one he wanted.

He did not thank her. She had given him a refusal that was hers and taken a refusal that was his and there is no form in this kingdom on which either of those is entered, and he wrote down that he had wanted to say something and that saying it would have been a man telling a woman with a needle that he was glad to have been refused, and that this project has produced that sentence in about nine mouths and has never once paid anybody for it.



> *Day three of the ten. The two hundred and ninth, at about the ninth hour of the evening, on flat ground above the lower end of the reach. A step with about eleven nets on lines behind it, a basket, a needle in a shuttle, a hole mended on one side only, three questions in a fixed order with no name in any of them, a barrow a hundred yards down, four bolts, a leaf in a book.*
>
> *Second column, the shape, and she gave it and I did not ask. **A mend is not a decision. A tear goes apart along the same threads every time it is put down the same way, and the man after me puts in the same shape, and the same shape would go in again.** I have been asked about the woman about four times in nine years and every one was the same three questions in the same order, and **the name was never in any of the three: did anybody see her go, was there a hole, was there a man in the row who was there that night.** Nine men on this water could tell me the shape of the asking and not one of the four has the name in it. **Nobody is careful. That is what she said, and she has had nine years to be sure of it and I have had three weeks and I was wrong.** She mends only the near side of a hole and says the far side of a hole is a different hole, and that is her whole trade and she gave it to me for nothing.
>
> *Second column, the column, and what nobody at a door will discuss. **A name in a keeper's line is a standing and not a person. It is a column ruled in a hand, and when the name is in it that is all that has happened.** She has seen the page. She knows four people on this reach who are in one, and could tell me their trades and the mile they live on, and not one name among them, and would not be given one and would not use it. **A person is not what a column holds and there is no step between the two, and I have been treating that as a gap I could put a sentence in.** A woman in this reach is a name in a line and not a person anybody at a door will discuss, and no door on nine miles of water will do otherwise, and I am not going to go and find one and be told no at it.
>
> *Third column, the ask, the refusal, and the rule that runs the other way. **She asked me for a name because nobody else was going to and somebody ought to have, and I said no, and she said thank you flat and went back to the shuttle and did not press it.** A man who refuses is doing her a service and a man who gives it to her is doing her an injury, and about nine men on this water have given her the other thing and she has held it for them since. **I have had a rule for a hundred and thirty days that ran the way I wanted and this is the first that runs the other way, and I have written it out so I cannot lose it.** The name is in a leaf in this coat and has been seventy-five days and I have never said it aloud in a room, and a woman with a needle asked me for it today and asked in order to be refused. The three partial answers are still three and this is not a fourth one. The schedule went into my hands on the hundred and thirty-fourth day and it is seventy-five days in my pocket, and the eleven lines are out of the same day, and none of the three is the not-asking.
>
> *The five clauses stand and I am not going through them. Four bolts made fast at the fifth hour on the hard ground by the barrow and again at the seventh. **The frame has not been in a building since the thirty-third, a hundred and seventy-six days, and there was a net-loft roof thirty foot off that barrow with about eleven wet nets under it and a woman in it, and I did not put a cradle in it, and the reason is not the second clause: a net-loft has no straight floor, and I have not been able to hold a straight edge for a day and a half since the hundred and thirty-second, and there is no form in this kingdom on which a man can be told he may not hold one either.** The carrier in the sack, **a hundred and thirty-two days out of the seventy-seventh, not rung once, and I did not touch it.** My own bell is in a sealed yard in Kells and the bar is on the bell and not on the licence, and it does not travel. **A handbell is in her bag and it is hers and it is not rung, and no person has asked her for a reason at any hour of this day, me included.** Nothing was struck and nothing was tied and nothing went into metal, I did not reach for a thing, and nothing came.
>
> *The register is thirteen. **Nothing was asked for, produced, given or taken on any of the ten days of this week by me, and a column ruled in a hand in a room about four days south of the top of this water is not in my coat, I was not shown the page, I did not ask to see it, and no name was entered on any paper by me.** A net, a needle, a shuttle, a basket, three questions: none of them is a paper. **Nobody was thanked today, and a woman told me a fact about my own conduct that I had got wrong for three weeks and I did not thank her and she did not want it.** Seventy-three days on this road with her and I did not ask her what it was for. Which office is it. **A column that holds a standing and does not hold a person, and a mend that is the shape of the hole and not a choice about it.**
