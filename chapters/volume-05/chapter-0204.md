# Chapter 0204 — The Reach Has Two Names a Mile, and He Walked It Twice to Be Wrong Both Times

It was the first hour of the hundred and ninetieth day and Hob Tarrow was standing at a gate in a hedge at the third mile of the reach with his back to a field and a hurdle in his hand, and he was doing the thing he did every morning of his life there, which was to stand at his own gate and look up the road and decide whether the day was worth the walk down it, and Tomas Vale came up the road with a barrow and four people and stopped on the other side of the road because a man at his own gate in the first hour is a man deciding something.

The road ran out of a mile of flat grass and clay with ditches in it. A cart way left it on the left at a shallow angle and came back two hundred yards on. A foot way went up the bank on the right. The hedge was nine foot of blackthorn and hawthorn with a gate in it, the gate was not shut, and behind it a gate post of oak went down into the ground and did not stop.

"You are early," Tarrow said. "Everybody on this reach is early until about the fourth hour and then late for the rest of the day, and I have never worked out which is the harder habit to break."

"I am not going down the road," Tomas said. "I am going to walk this mile and back and write down what is on the ground, and then put a sheet out of my coat on a table in front of four people and see whether it agrees. And if it does not, I would like to be told which of the two of us is wrong, in that order."

"You have got about nine hours before the light goes off that flat and you have walked one mile already."

"Then I have got about eight."

---

At about the second hour Tamsin Rook went up the bank on the right on her own and was gone for two hours, and nobody asked her where she was going and she did not tell anybody, and when she came back she had a leaf in her hand and was not going to say anything about it to anybody, including him.

That left Tomas with three people on the road, a man at a gate, and a mile that would not hold still.

Ostwick was the word at nine doors, correct, and on the people's own mouths.

Keld was the word on the sheet in his coat. The sheet is folded in three and the crease at the third mile does not go through the rectangle about two hands together that four men looked at in a bench room and did not explain, because the rectangle is somewhere else on that paper. It is fifty-four days in his pocket. He can see wards and numbers and a scale. He cannot read a run, and there is no form in this kingdom on which a man is taught to read one, and so there is no version of being better at one, and nobody he could ask.

Marrowby was the third, and Marrowby is a place four miles up this reach where the carts load.

---

At about the fourth hour the man at the gate was still at his gate and Tomas had walked the mile twice, once up the cart way and once on the foot way above it, and the two walks had not agreed about anything at all, and that was not because either of them was badly done.

"You have written something in a book," Tarrow said.

"I have."

"Then you are not going to tell me what it says, and I am not going to ask, and I would like it noted that that is the first sensible thing anybody with a barrow has done on this reach in about nine years."

"I am going to tell you something and then I am not going to tell you anything else about it, and it is a true thing and it is about the ground and not about you." Tomas took the schedule out and unfolded it along the three creases it already had and held it flat against the gate post with his knee on the corner. "On that paper, this mile is called Keld. At the nine doors of this mile it is called Ostwick. And four miles up this reach there is a place called Marrowby, and the people at Marrowby call the whole bottom of this water Marrowby, which is where their carts load. Three words, all correct, all in use today, and the sheet has one of them, and the sheet is not a lie, because the sheet is not about the ground."

"No. It is about what is owed."

"It is about what is owed. Your district's own man said that in a bench room in front of nine benches' people and he was right and I have it written down."

Tarrow set the hurdle against the gate post, which is what a man does when he has decided to stand still.

"**I am not going to tell you which one to write.** I was born in the last house below Marrowby. All my life the bottom of this water has been Marrowby for carts and Ostwick for doors, and there has never been one name and I never wanted one. Ask a man at a door up there to call it Keld and he will do it, because a man will say a word that is on a paper if it costs him nothing, and then in nine years you will come back down this road with a drawing and be told it is Ostwick, with no way of telling that you were both right."

"That is the worst of it. I cannot tell which of the two is the one on the paper."

"You cannot, and it is not your eyes." He said it flatly. "It is the same fault that is in every other thing you have been looking at for a hundred and ninety days, and an instrument would not help you, because there is nothing in this kingdom that separates a word on a paper from a word on a road. I have looked for one for about nine years and have not got one, and I am not going to be the man who says he has."

---

At about the fifth hour the four of them were in the field gate with the mile laid out in front of them, and Tomas wrote the arithmetic on the back page of his own book, and the arithmetic was true, and the conclusion was refused.

The paper says Keld. Nine doors say Ostwick. Marrowby says Marrowby and means the bottom of the water. **One mile, three names, one of them the name of a place four miles up, and the sheet carries one of the three and is not a lie about the ground because the sheet is not about the ground.**

"The useful thing you have found," Pell Okell said, "is that a drawing is worse than nothing. You would rather had nothing. With nothing you ask somebody. With that in your pocket you walk to the right place by the wrong name and you never find out you were wrong."

"That is the useful thing," Tomas said. "Put it in the third column and not the second. It is about the paper. The mile is the thing that is true."

Mab Sarter had been at the hedge the whole time with a piece of chalk and had not been counting, which for her was worth mentioning.

"There is no ninth fence on this mile," she said.

"Is that a finding."

"It is a thing I noticed and I am telling you because you are writing things down, and I would like one of mine in there. **Nine fences, then a gate, then nothing for four hundred yards and then a fence that is not on the line at all, it is set back for a cart to turn, and if you count fences on a mile you get a different answer every time somebody has moved a gate.**"

"It is in the third column. It is in the third column and it is a finding and it is not about the sheet."

"Isa Trevail had not said anything all morning and said one thing now. "There is a soft place at the top of that mile that is not a soft place. It is a road. It goes up, and it is not on anything, and I know it is not on anything because I have gone up it eleven times with a barrow and have never once seen a man come down it who was not somebody I already knew." She put her hand on the yoke. "You will go up it this afternoon. Do not take the barrow. It is not fit for a barrow and there is nobody on it to hear you say so."

---

At about the seventh hour he was at the top of the mile with the barrow on flat ground and the four bolts made fast, the four people were at the field gate two hundred yards down, and he was alone for the first time since the second hour.

There was a road. It went off the top of the mile to the right, not up the bank and not into the ditch, at the grade a road goes when it is made for carts and has been used by carts, with a hedge on both sides and a gate at the near end that was not shut, and about two hundred yards along it there were cart ruts standing in soft ground with water in them. He could see the whole of it from where he was standing.

He took the schedule out, unfolded it along the three creases it already had, and held it flat against the cradle rail with one hand. There was nothing on that paper at that point at all. Not a panel. Not a rate. Not a line drawn and not a line not drawn. The figures either side of where he was standing went on without a break, which is the shape the rectangle is, and he noticed that and then made himself un-notice it, because a man who has been wrong three times by putting two true things side by side does not get to do it a fourth time on the strength of a coincidence.

He folded it in three and put it back in the inside pocket of his coat.

At about the ninth hour of the evening he was still on the flat at the top of the mile with the barrow against a stone and the four bolts made fast, and the light was going off the clay flat and not off the water, and there was a fire in a yard nine hundred yards back that meant about nine people and a fire and nothing else at all.

He could see a road, and he had a piece of paper, and one of them said there was nothing there, and he could not get nine feet of ground between the two of them.

> *The hundred and ninetieth, at about the ninth hour of the evening, on the flat at the top of the mile. A gate in a hedge, a hurdle, an oak gate post, a cart way, a foot way on the bank, three names, a sheet with three creases, a field gate, a road going right with cart ruts and water in them, a barrow, four bolts.*
>
> *Second column, the three, and none of them joined to anything. **At nine doors it is Ostwick, which is the ground. On the sheet it is Keld, and there is a Keld four miles up, so the sheet uses one word for two miles. And the people at Marrowby four miles up call the whole bottom of this water Marrowby, because that is where their carts load.** The paper carries one of the three and is not a lie about the ground, because the paper is not about the ground. A district's own man told me in a bench room that a sheet is a return and a return wants finding again.
>
> *Second column, the man at the gate. **He is not going to tell me which one to write**, and he was born in the last house below Marrowby and has never wanted one name for a mile in his life. His reason, nearly his own words, because it took me two days to arrive at the end of it: ask a man at a door up there to call it Keld and he will do it, and in nine years I would come back with the drawing and be told I was at Ostwick, with no way of knowing we were both right. **It is not my eyes, he said, and he is right, and it is the same fault that is in everything else I have looked at.**
>
> *Third column, ruled apart. **A drawing is worse than nothing on ground with more than one name: with nothing I ask somebody, and with the sheet I walk to the right place by the wrong name and never find out I was wrong.** Pell said it and it goes in the third column because it is about the paper and not the mile. **Mab: nine fences, then a gate, then nothing for four hundred yards, then one set back for a cart to turn. Count fences on a mile and you get a different answer every time somebody has moved a gate, and that is a finding and it is not about the sheet.** Isa: a road at the top of this mile on nothing, up eleven times with a barrow and never a stranger coming down it, and she told me not to take the barrow, and she is right.
>
> *Third column, two of them and not joined. **At about the seventh hour I stood at the top of this mile and could see a road, and the sheet had nothing at that point at all — not a panel, not a rate, not a line drawn and not a line not drawn, and the figures either side went on as if nothing were there, which is the shape the rectangle is.** I noticed that, then made myself un-notice it, and I want both halves of that written down. A man who has been wrong three times by joining two true things does not get it a fourth time because two of them arrived together. I have to choose which one to believe and I have no instrument. **Two men in about forty days have handed me a thing about a wall and a father's work, neither of whom owed me a word of it, and I did not ask either of them why, and I am not going to ask a third man this week.** Tallow Reach is on no sheet I have ever been shown.
>
> *The five clauses stand and I am not going through them. The four bolts went in at the first hour on flat ground at the top of the mile and had not moved by dark. **The frame has not been in a building since the thirty-third, a hundred and fifty-seven days, and there is a cart shed at the field gate with a roof on it that I did not put a cradle under, and the reason is not the second clause: the reason is that there were carters in it at the fourth hour, and a thing bolted down under a roof comes out from under it with a barrow-load of somebody else's business still in.** The carrier in the sack, **a hundred and thirteen days out of the seventy-seventh, and I did not touch it today.** My own bell is in a sealed yard in Kells and the bar is on the bell and not on the licence, and it does not travel. **Her bell is in her bag and nobody asked her for a reason at any hour today, including me; she went up the bank on the right at about the second hour and came back at the fourth with a leaf in her hand, and neither of us said one word about it, and nine feet off a man is not nine feet off a woman, and I have written that down because it is the one reason I had this month that I would have left out.**
>
> *The register is thirteen, nothing was asked for, produced, given or taken today, no name was entered on any paper by me. **Fifty-four days on this road with her and I did not ask her what it was for, and today was the day I could most easily have done it and did not. I am not writing the not-doing down as a decision, because a man who is not asked what he does does not get to call it one.**
>
> *Which office is it. **Three names on one mile, a gate that is not shut, a road going right that is on nothing, and nine or ten places with people in them.**
