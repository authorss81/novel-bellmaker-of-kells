# Chapter 0212 — A Chain Over the Water and a Man Who Is in Two Places in About Nine Minutes

It was the third hour of the hundred and ninety-eighth day and Cobb Harrow was going across on the far side of the chain for a load of lime sacks that he was not going to fetch, because he went across every morning at about the third hour to look at the far bank, and looking at it is what he is paid for, and there is no form on which either of those two facts is written down.

The crossing was a plank about nine foot by two laid on two sleepers on the near bank and two on the far one, with a chain running from a ring in an oak post on the near side, across the water at about the height of a man's knee, through a ring in an oak post on the far side, and back. There was a windlass on the near post with a handle in it and a pawl, and the chain had come off its pawl eleven times in about nine years and gone into the river twice, and both times he had got it back with a hook and a boat and neither time had told anybody, because the crossing is his and the money is for the crossing.

He went over, took the pawl off, walked the plank, got out, and stood on the far bank looking at four hundred yards of soft ground where about nine beasts had been standing in the night.

Tomas Vale came down the bank with the barrow behind him and Harrow put a hand up before he had got to the water, which is a thing a man does at a crossing.

"Not with that."

"I know."

"You do not know, and I am going to tell you why you do not know, and it is the only time I am going to tell you." Harrow let the hand drop. "**A barrow does not go on a plank that is two foot wide over about ninety foot of water with a chain across the middle of it, and I have watched a man try one with a handcart and go into nine foot of water with his own boots on, and I got him out, and I have never been thanked for that and I am not going to be.** If you want the far bank you go across on your own and you come back when I say you can come back, and I have not got a rule about that and I am not going to make one in front of you."

---

At about the fourth hour he was on the near bank with the windlass turned and the chain slack across the water, and the man with the barrow was on the near bank, and the four people were up the road about two hundred yards with a barrow-load of lime and a yoke and a woman counting fences.

"You are the one who has been up past the stones," Harrow said, and it was not a question.

"Yes."

"Then you have seen him count them."

"He counts them every fourth day."

"He counts them every fourth day and he does not know that I know he does, and I am not going to tell him for about nine years." Harrow took the pawl off and let the chain run two foot and took it up again, which is a thing a man does with his hands while he is talking. "I will tell you the thing about the stones, because you have come with a barrow and a barrow is the sort of thing a man cannot be trusted to be quiet about. There are four hundred yards between me and him, and I cross this about four times a day, and in the four times I see him about twice, and what I see is a man stopping at nine stones and putting a finger on them. And in about nine years I have never once seen him stop at anything else. I have also never once seen him stop at the ninth one longer than the other eight, and I have thought about that more than is good for me."

"He said it is because his hand is on cattle all day."

"That is what he told you, and I have had nine years of a different fact, and I am not saying mine is better than his. **Mine is that a man who counts a thing every fourth day is not counting the thing. He is counting the day. A man who counts a day is a man who has turned something into a habit, and a habit is the only kind of holding this water has ever had, and it is the reason I can run a chain across ninety foot of water and nobody has ever been paid to tell me to.**"

---

At about the fifth hour he had put the barrow on the hard ground and Tomas Vale had been across and back twice, and the second time the man on the far side had said nothing at all, and on the third crossing he began.

"Right. Two things and then I want the windlass back, because I have a load coming at about the seventh and if the chain is wet I get the cost of it out of my own hour."

"Go on."

"**One. A crossing is not a road. A road is there and a crossing is there because somebody is on one side who wants to be on the other, and I have counted, and in a bad month there are days when there is nobody at either end after the second hour, and on those days there is a chain in a river and not a crossing.**" He turned the handle a half turn and let it go. "Now. I have tried to write that down once. I am not a clerk and I got a man to hold the pen. We wrote it out and the sentence said *a crossing is a road that is not always there*, and the man read it back and said that was the wrong way round, and he was right, and we put it the other way and it said *a road that is not always there is a crossing*, and he read that back too and said that was a different mistake. And he is a better hand than me and we have not tried a third time in about nine years. **There is no form on which you can put a thing that is only true on the day it is true, and I have never wanted one until about two years ago and I have stopped wanting one since about the eleventh day of this month, and I am not going to explain the eleventh day.**"

"The eleventh day of this month is a day about four days from now."

"It is, and I would rather you had worked that out than that I had told you, and I would rather still you had not worked it out, and there it is three times in about four minutes and I am a man who says things out loud on a bank and you are going to be told that about me by somebody within the week."

---

At about the seventh hour the load came and the four people were on the near bank with a barrow-load of lime and a yoke, and Tomas Vale had been across and back five times and had not been asked to lend anything, and the man at the windlass had not looked at him once while he worked.

"You have been over five times for no reason I can see," Harrow said.

"I have been counting the sleepers."

"Say that again and I will take the pawl off and put it in my pocket."

"I have been counting the sleepers and thinking about the third one from the near end, and there is iron in it on the underside, and the chain has been on the same line for about nine years and it has not cut the iron, and it has cut the oak on the far post about a foot above the ring." He pointed with his chin. "Somebody has been splicing that oak. Not this year."

"**My father did that.**"

Tomas Vale did not say anything.

"That is not me telling you about a wall and a father's work and it is not a thing to write down, and you are not to ask me why he did it and you are not to go and look for it, and I can see you deciding not to and I would rather you did it faster." Harrow set the pawl home. "It is oak because the chain eats oak. Everything that touches that chain on this reach is being eaten and the only question anybody ever asks me is whether the crossing works. The chain wants a post of iron and a post of iron wants a smith and a rate and a book with a figure in the corner, and the day that happens the crossing becomes a road, and a road has to be there, and I have got about ninety foot of water in which a road would be a lie four days in seven. **That is my term and it is not a principle and I have not thought about it: I would rather have the chain and the oak and the going than the iron and the book, and I have said that to about four men in nine years and three of them wrote it down.**"

"You have said it to three men who wrote it down."

"They wrote it down in their own books about their own ground, and I have never asked to see it and I could not tell you which three, and if one of them is a man you have spoken to this week I would rather you did not go and put my sentence next to his."

---

At about the ninth hour of the evening there was a chain across ninety foot of water with the slack taken up and a lamp on the far post and a barrow on flat ground above the near bank with four bolts made fast, and the crossing had been used eleven times that day and there had been nobody on either end of it for about two hours together, and that had not been written down anywhere and would not be.

Tomas Vale had a sixth sentence and it was the second one that came out of a trade about ninety foot of water, and it did not sit next to the byre-man's about a number that dies with the man, and it did not sit next to the waller's about a mending, and the reason it did not sit next to the first was that the byre-man was talking about a number and this man was talking about a length of time, and a man who is right about a number and a man who is right about a length of time are two people who have not met.

He wrote that down in his own book, in the second column, and then he stopped, and then he wrote one more line under it, which was that a man with a barrow had been across five times for no reason anybody could see and that the reason was about a post his father had spliced, and that he was not going to find out the rest of it.




> *The hundred and ninety-eighth, at about the ninth hour of the evening, on flat ground above the near bank. A plank, two sleepers a side, a chain at the height of a knee, an oak post at each end, a windlass with a handle and a pawl, iron in the third sleeper, oak spliced a foot above the far ring, a lamp on the far post, a barrow, four bolts.*
>
> *Second column, the crossing, and the sentence he got a man to write out once. **A crossing is not a road. A road is there; a crossing is there because somebody is on one side who wants to be on the other.** In a bad month there are days when there is nobody at either end after the second hour, and on those days there is a chain in a river. He had a better hand than himself hold the pen nine years back and they set it down both ways round, and the better man read each one back and said they were two different mistakes, and they have not tried a third time. **There is no form on which a thing that is only true on the day it is true can be put down.** He stopped wanting one on the eleventh day of this month, which is four days off, and told me so that I would work it out and then so that I would not.*
>
> *Second column, his term, and he says himself it is not a principle. **He would rather have the chain and the oak and the going than the iron and the book.** The chain eats oak and everything it touches on this reach is being eaten, and the only question put to him is whether the crossing works. The day it becomes a road, a road has to be there, and he has ninety foot of water in which a road would be a lie four days in seven. He has said it to four men in nine years and three wrote it down in their own books about their own ground, and he cannot tell me which three, and he would rather I did not put his sentence beside another man’s.*
>
> *Second column, the stones, which is not a finding. **He crosses four times a day and sees the byre-man at the nine stones about twice in four crossings, and in nine years has never seen him stop at the ninth longer than at the other eight.** His own account is that a man counting a thing every fourth day is counting the day, and a habit is the only kind of holding this water has ever had. He has never told the byre-man that he watches him and does not intend to for nine years. That is one man’s account of another man’s habit seen from ninety foot of water. It is not the byre-man’s own sentence, the two do not fit, and a thing that does not fit is not a smaller version of a thing that does.*
>
> *Third column, said once, and I did not ask. **A man spliced oak into the far post a foot above the ring nine years back, and it was his father, and he told me in the same breath not to ask why and not to go and look for it.** A man talking about his father is a man talking about his father and I am not going to make it into a finding. **A chain wants a post of iron; a post of iron wants a smith, a rate and a book with a figure in the corner; and the day that happens the crossing becomes a road.** Six trades and six grammars and not one pair of them will sit together. **The three partial answers to the line-question are still three; this is a sixth thing and not a fourth answer, and I am joining it to nothing. The schedule went into my hands on the hundred and thirty-fourth day and it is sixty-four days in my pocket; the name came out of the same day and is sixty-four days in the same pocket, and it is a woman’s name and I have not used it for anything.***
>
> *The five clauses stand and I am not going through them. Four bolts made fast at the fourth hour and again at the seventh, and at the ninth hour of the evening on the same ground. **The frame has not been in a building since the thirty-third, a hundred and sixty-five days, and there is a chain hut four hundred yards off with a shut door, and I did not open it, and the reason is not the second clause: a man running a chain does not want a stranger in his hut at the hour the chain is slack, and I am not going to pretend I did not see the reason.** The carrier in the sack, **a hundred and twenty-one days out of the seventy-seventh, not rung once, and I did not touch it.** My own bell is in a sealed yard in Kells and the bar is on the bell and not on the licence, and it does not travel. **There is a handbell in her bag and it is not rung; she was on the far bank and came across and I did not go over to her, and that is the same silence as not asking.***
>
> *The register is thirteen. **Nothing was asked for, produced, given or taken today, and no name was entered on any paper by me.** A sentence a chain man has said to three men in nine years is a sentence. A log of crossings is a tally on a board. Neither is a document and neither is a fourteenth. **Sixty-two days on this road with her and I did not ask her what it was for.** Which office is it. **A chain across ninety foot of water, oak spliced above a ring by a man who is not alive, and a post of iron that every man who has ever asked about that crossing wants and that the man with the chain would rather do without.***
