# Chapter 0208 — Nine Miles of Two Names and One Wall, and a Fifth Walker for a Day

It was the third hour of the hundred and ninety-fourth day and Sander Vell was walking up the road with a hoe over his shoulder and a dog that was not interested in him, and he had come up from the lower end of the reach at the second hour to see about a wall, and about four hundred yards of ditch, and about nine hours of daylight, and by the fourth hour he was a fifth walker on a road that had had four on it since the first day of the year.

He said so before anybody asked, because he was the sort of man who says things out loud on a road.

"I am here for the wall and the ditch and I am going back down at the ninth hour whatever happens, and if I am still on this road at the tenth hour then somebody has kept me and it will not have been you, because you are a man with a barrow and a barrow does not keep anybody."

"You have not asked whether I want you to."

"You have not asked me to stay and I have not asked you to feed me, and that is the correct arrangement and it is worth a great deal more than a dinner."

---

At about the fourth hour they were on the reach with the barrow and the five of them, and Tomas Vale had been up this road twice in nine days and had learned three things about it that had nothing to do with where it went.

The first was that the second clause of a receipt in five is the reason he could not set the barrow down, and therefore the reason every night of the ten days was solved by a person he was not going to thank. The first night had been nine shillings in a kitchen at a place where a woman took the money and gave him a plate and did not ask him anything.

The second night had been a cart shed with a stove in it and a man who stood in the doorway and would not be asked twice, and said so before Tomas could ask twice. *There is no room. Take the shed. I am not letting it and I am not charging you and I do not want to be told about it later.* He said the last part standing in his own doorway with the door open behind him, and he said it as a man setting out the terms of a thing he had already decided, and then he said the rest, unasked, and Tomas Vale wrote it down in the ordinary way and did not improve it.

"**That room was going out to a carter on the second night of next week and I have told him so this morning and he has gone to my brother's and will be angry with me for a fortnight. I am telling you that now because in about a fortnight a man is going to come and ask me why I gave it away, and I am not going to tell him, and I would rather somebody knew and did not use it than that nobody knew.** There is no form in this kingdom in which a man gives up a room he has let and puts the name of the person he gave it to in a column, and there is not going to be one, and if there were I would not fill it in."

He went in and shut the door, and the barrow stood in the yard with the four bolts made fast and the frame under a sky, and Tomas Vale did not thank him, because there is no form in this kingdom in which gratitude is entered and because a man who has just given away a fortnight's letting and told a stranger about it does not want a stranger's gratitude in his doorway.

The third night had been a room over a shop in exchange for four hours of carrying lime up a bank for a woman with a barrow and a yoke, and the woman had said the price out loud and he had paid it and neither of them had called it a hire, and the price had been said out loud in a room with three other people in it, and none of the three had said anything.

The second was that a fifth walker is still not a company. Sander Vell walked a mile a day on an average day and knew nine hedges and two gates and one bad ditch, and he was the first man in six days who had told Tomas Vale a thing he wanted to know, and when the four of them stopped at the top of the bank at about the fifth hour and did not go on, he went on, and came back at about the seventh hour, and stood at the top of the bank with the hoe over his shoulder and said, "It is a five-minute walk and a four-foot wall with about nine foot of ditch behind it, and the wall is not old, and I have a reason about it that is mine and I am not going to give it to you because you would put it in a book."

"You have not got a book."

"No. But you have one in your coat and it is a book, and the day I have a book is the day I stop telling people things." He shifted the hoe. "Walking the same ground is not agreeing. I have said that twice now and I will say it a third time in nine days and then I will go and mend a wall."

The third was that he was becoming a man who was known.

On the fifth day a woman at a gate said *you are the one with the barrow* before he had said a word, and on the sixth a man at a landing said *I have seen you, you were at my boards on the second day* and had gone on with his own work. On the eighth a boy crossed a road to ask him whether the thing on the barrow was a bell, and Tomas said no, and the boy said everybody says no, and went on.

Being known is worth exactly nothing. There is no form in this kingdom on which a person's face is entered, and being recognised on nine miles of water does not make anybody say yes to anything, and by the ninth day four people on that reach knew what he was carrying and not one of them had asked him what it was for.

He wrote that down in the ordinary way on the flat at the seventh hour, in the ordinary hand, with nothing round it, and then he wrote *and I am going to keep writing it, and one day I am going to stop noticing that it is worthless, and on that day I will be a man who is used to being worth nothing, which is a different thing and not a better one.*

---

At about the fifth hour the five of them came to a byre, and the byre was up a short bank off the road with a door that was open, and inside it was about nine foot of depth of cow and the smell of it, and Tamsin Rook went in and stood with her hand on a post at the back and did not come out for about half an hour.

Tomas Vale stood at the door and looked at the wall of the byre while she was in there, and the wall was not old. It was about four foot of rubble and lime with a brick frame round a door that had been put in later and had been cut to fit, and the cut was clean and recent, and there was a straight edge mark on the rubble on both sides of the opening, because a man who cuts a hole in a wall with a straight edge leaves a line on both sides of it and cannot leave it on one.

He put his hand flat on the rubble and he did not take the straight edge out of the bag behind the cradle and he did not measure anything, and he wrote down afterwards that he had not, and why, and the why was four words and it was that it was not his wall.

Nobody asked her anything while she was in there. He did not go in. Pell Okell went in at about the sixth hour and came out and said that the byre was nine foot deep and that the far wall was soft to about the height of a man's chest, and that the man who owned it knew, and that everybody who has looked at it in about nine years has known, and that nobody has written it down because there is no form on which a man with a barrow may enter a soft place in somebody else's wall.

She wrote one entry that night and it was about the post at the back of the byre, which is heartwood and has been in the ground since before the byre and is nine foot of it, and about the way the floor moves when the animal on the other side of it shifts its weight, which is a thing you find out with a hand and a boot and not by any other means that does not cost money.

---

At about the seventh hour the road came out on a flat and there was a fire going in a yard off it about four hundred yards up, and about nine hundred yards above that, on the flat above the water, there was a thing that was not a building.

Sander Vell saw it first. He put the hoe down in the road and stood in it.

"That is not a building," he said.

"No."

"It has got a wall on three sides and a roof on four posts and a thing on a frame the size of a cartwheel, and there is a man coming down off it towards this road at the pace a man comes at when he has seen somebody he did not expect." Vell picked the hoe up. "He has not seen you yet. He has seen five people and a barrow, and a barrow is a barrow anywhere, and he has come down four hundred yards to find out who is on his road."

Nobody thanked him for saying it. He went back down the reach at about the ninth hour of the evening, as he said he would, and he told nobody what his reason was, and the four who were left were four again, and the number four and a half is not a shape and there is no form anywhere in this kingdom on which a fraction of a walker can be entered.

Tomas Vale stood in the road with the barrow behind him and the four bolts made fast on a stone at the near end of the flat and did not go up the last four hundred yards, because the man was coming down them, and because a man who has kept a thing going for nine years by coming when people arrive is a man who should be allowed to arrive at his own place first.

> *The hundred and ninety-fourth, at about the ninth hour of the evening, on the flat below the node. A barrow, four bolts, a byre with a wall that is not old, a heartwood post, a hoe, a dog not interested, nine hours of daylight, four hundred yards of ditch, a low wall on three sides, a roof on four posts, a thing on a frame, a man coming down at the pace of a man who has seen a barrow.*
>
> *Second column, the three nights. **Nine shillings, a plate, no questions. A cart shed, given, with a fortnight's letting lost and the name of the carter not written anywhere. A room over a shop, paid for with four hours of my own back on a bank with a woman's barrow, the price said out loud, and neither of us calling it a hire.** Every night of the ten solved by a person, and no form in this kingdom in which a man pays a debt of that shape, and none of the three is a document and none is a fourteenth. The second clause is why I could not set the barrow down and so why every night was somebody else's.
>
> *Second column, the five. **A fifth walker is still not a company.** He came up for a wall and a ditch, and told me before anybody asked that if he was still on that road at the tenth hour then somebody had kept him and it would not have been me. He gave me one thing I wanted and would not give a second, and the reason he gave was a book in my coat, and he was right, and the wall is not his to explain. **Walking the same ground is not agreeing. He said that twice and would say it a third time in nine days and then go and mend a wall.** The count is four and a half, and a half is not a shape.
>
> *Third column, ruled apart. **A woman said *you are the one with the barrow* at a gate on the fifth day before I had said a word; a man at a landing said he had seen me on the second; a boy crossed a road to ask whether the thing on the barrow was a bell, and I said no, and he said everybody says no.** Four people on nine miles of water know what I am carrying and not one has asked me what it is for. **Being known is worth exactly nothing: no form in this kingdom on which a person's face is entered.** I will keep writing that down, and one day I will stop noticing it is worthless, and on that day I will be a man used to being worth nothing, which is a different thing and not a better one. Fourth time in ten days, and I am not doing anything about it.
>
> *Third column, the byre. **The wall is not old: four foot of rubble and lime with a brick frame round a door cut to fit afterwards, and a straight edge mark on the rubble either side of the opening, because a man who cuts a hole with a straight edge leaves a line on both sides and cannot leave it on one.** My own straight edge was in the bag behind the cradle and I did not take it out and measured nothing, and the reason is four words: it is not my wall. No form in this kingdom on which a man with a barrow may enter a soft place in somebody else's wall. **I did not go into the byre. She was in there half an hour with her hand on a heartwood post and nobody asked her anything, including me, and she wrote one entry about the post and the floor and not about the number of people on this reach.**
>
> *The five clauses stand and I am not going through them. The four bolts went in at the third hour on the road and again at the seventh at the near end of the flat. **The frame has not been in a building since the thirty-third, a hundred and sixty-one days. Three of the ten days had a roof over me, a floor under me, a door not shut and nobody in the building, and on all three I did not do it, and the three reasons are three reasons and none of them is the clause.** Five other days had a roof and something against it — a four-foot barn mouth, a barrow that cannot go up a stair, carters in a shed, a shut boat shed, a man's shut working room — and I am not calling those choices, because a man who calls an obstacle a choice is a man who wants to have chosen. Nothing was struck and nothing was tied and nothing went into metal. **My own bell is in a sealed yard in Kells and the bar is on the bell and not on the licence, and it does not travel.** I did not reach for anything and nothing came. The carrier in the sack, **a hundred and seventeen days out of the seventy-seventh, and I did not put my hand on it today and have not now.**
>
> *The register is thirteen. Nothing was asked for, produced, given or taken today, and no name was entered on any paper by me, and no room receipt and no hire paper and no arrangement exists. **Fifty-eight days on this road with her and I did not ask her what it was for, and I did not ask her what she had her hand on inside a byre, and those two are the same not-asking and I am counting them as one, which is convenient and is not honest, and I have written that it is not honest.**
>
> *Which office is it. **A soft place nine foot deep in a byre wall, a straight edge mark on both sides of a later cut, a man coming down four hundred yards at the pace of a man who has seen a barrow, and a thing on a frame that is not a bell.**
