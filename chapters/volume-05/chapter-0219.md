# Chapter 0219 — About Nine Hundred Names Over a Landing, and a Line That Goes Into a District's Own Book and Answers Nothing

It was the first hour of the two hundred and fifth day and Bel Tremayne was at her landing with a book on a board that rested on two sawn-off stakes, and she writes the day into it standing up in whatever the weather is, and she has done that every morning for about four years, and the book is about four years old and it is about nine hundred names in it and about nine hundred is a figure that describes the size of the book and not a quantity of anything.

She writes the passage first and the people after, and on a wet morning the passage is the whole of it and the people are counted at about the second hour, and on a dry one she writes as they come off.

Tomas Vale came down the bank with the barrow and stopped at the near end of the boards, and he had been on this landing once, on the second day of this month, and had not asked her anything then, and she had not said anything then, and neither of them had mentioned it since.

"You have come to ask me about the book."

"Yes."

"Then ask, and I will tell you, and I am going to tell you whatever I feel like telling you and not what you came for. About nine hundred names. Four years. One of them is put down twice because a man came off at the far end and then came off again the next week and paid twice and wanted it entered twice. That is the only one twice. **I have never read it out loud to anybody, I have never let anybody read it, and I have never once said a name in it to a person who could not see the landing.**"

---

At about the second hour the light got onto the water and about four people came off and she wrote four lines in about as long as it takes to turn the page twice, and then she came and stood at the near end of the boards with the book shut under her arm.

"Go on, then."

"I am not going to ask what it is for, because you have told me what it is in about four seconds and the answer is a list, and a list is not a question." She looked at him. "**You are going to ask me whether a name is in it, and I am going to tell you why before you ask, because you would have asked and I would have said no and we would both have been politer about it than either of us wanted.** A name in my book means a person who owes a landing a small sum of money."

"Say it."

"**Because a name in my book means a person who owes a landing a small sum of money, and I am not going to be the second woman on this water to hand a name to a man with a barrow.**" She said it flat and without heat. "A woman over at the lower end did give you one. I do not blame her. She keeps two lists and one of those four names is not a person who lives on nine miles of water, and I have thought about that for about four days and I have not worked out whether she was right, and **I am not going to hand you a second one to help you work it out, and if you ask me for it in a week I am going to say the same thing in the same words, and that is not a threat, it is how a person behaves who intends to keep a thing.**"

"Then I will not ask you."

"**No. And that is the first sensible thing you have done on this landing, and I want to tell you why it is sensible, because you will get it wrong.** You are going to walk away from here believing that you were being careful, and being careful with me is a thing you do with a door. I am a person with a book. Ask me and be refused. That is a different object and it is worth more, and the refusal is mine and I would like it to have been asked for."

---

At about the fifth hour they were on the flat above the landing with the barrow on beaten earth and a stone at each wheel, and the four people were on the road and had been for about an hour, and the day had got to the part of it where a man with a barrow begins to explain something.

"You have a sheet in your coat," Bel said, "and I have a book, and I know what you are going to do because everybody does it, which is that you are going to ask to put one in front of the other."

"I was."

"Do not. And I am not being cold about it, and I am not going to explain for four minutes, and I am going to explain for about nine seconds and then I am going to do the rest of the day's work. **There is no form in this kingdom on which two people who both keep a book put one in front of the other.** Not a district's, not a lord's, not a wife's. It is not a prohibition and there is no penalty and nothing would happen to either of us; it is simply that nobody has ever done it, and the reason nobody has ever done it is that the second one always ends up being the one with less, and the one with less ends up being the one whose book is read. So no."

"That is the whole of your objection."

"That is the whole of my objection and it has been the whole of it for four years and I have never tested it on anybody until this morning. **And I will tell you the thing about a drawing while I am at it. A drawing is for a man who already knows what the ground is called. You have a mile and a half of names on that paper and every one of them is a name from a list in a county, and this landing has got nine names for it and I know all nine and I have never asked anybody which one is the right one, and I am not going to start today. Not with a man with a barrow and not with a district clerk and not with anybody.**"

---

At about the sixth hour a man with a flat book came down the reach and stopped at the near end of the landing boards, and about four people came up off the road and stopped as well, and Bel Tremayne put her hand flat on her own book and did not move it.

"Marl," she said. "You have been on this water three days."

"Three days and part of a morning." He did not say it to her so much as to the landing. Then, to Tomas Vale: "I am writing the middle column and I want the reach in it, and I have a day of walking in me and about a third of a bag of bread, and I am going to do it now while the light is on the water, and anybody here can go and do something else."

Nobody went and did something else. That is a fact about about four people and not a judgement on them.

He wrote standing up, on the flat above the landing, with the book on a sawn-off stake the way Bel had shown him without being asked how to hold it, and he wrote one line, and the line was about the ground, and the line said there was ground here and that people live on it.

There was no name on it anywhere. Not at the head of the line, not in the margin, not in the four boxes, and the last of the four is headed for a man and it was left empty and it was going to stay empty, and he said so out loud while he was writing it, in the ordinary voice, as a man says a thing he has said before and will say again.

"That is it," Marl said. "That is the whole of what I have come for and it is going in my district's book in a bench room four days down the water and it is going to be in a drawer by about the fourth hour tomorrow. It is not a name. It is not a household. It is a line in a middle column, and anybody in this county is welcome to come and look at it in about forty years, and about nine men on this water will never know it exists and I am not going to tell them."

---

At about the seventh hour Bel Tremayne shut her book and picked up her board and did not say anything about the line, and at about the tenth hour she was at the landing again with the book open and the passage written and the people after, in the ordinary way, and about four people had come off the water in the four hours since a district's clerk wrote a line about their reach into a book they will never see.

"You have not said anything about it," Tomas said.

"About the line."

"About the line."

"No, and I am not going to now, and I want you to hear the reason and then I am going to go and count the people off the water." She shut the book. "**It has changed nothing. That is not a criticism of him and it is not a defence of the row.** Every man on this water will turn up on the morning he was going to turn up on, and about four of them will say a reach is a reach. **A thing that has been written down is not the same object as a thing that has been entered, and there is a room for one of those and a room for the other and they are four days apart and neither of them has a door into the other.**"

"You do not think it was worth his four days."

"I did not say that, and I would have said it if I had meant it, and I would have said it to a woman who keeps a book and not to a man who carries one." She got the board under her arm. "**He walked four days to write down that people live on ground he has never eaten on. That is worth four days. What it is worth is four days.** And there was not one of us who said anything back."

---

At about the ninth hour of the evening the barrow was on flat ground with the four bolts made fast, and there was a light on in a building about two hundred yards up the bank where a man with a flat book was not, because he was asleep in a barn about nine hundred yards the other way, and there was a woman on a landing with a shut book under her arm who had not been thanked and would not be and had asked for nothing.

Tomas Vale had nine sentences in a book of his own, a name he had not asked about and had been refused, a name in the inside of his coat that he had not said out loud in a field gate or on a landing or anywhere, and a line in a district's own book about a reach where people live, read standing up, in a field gate, in a pencil, in a column that had no name in it.

He did not write the line out. He wrote down that he had not written it out, and that a man with a barrow who copies a clerk’s line into his own book is a man with a fourteenth thing, and that he has thirteen, and that the number is not going to be fourteen because a clerk filled in a box in a field gate, and that a line is a line and is not an answer and does not become one by being in a book.



> *Day nine of the ten. The two hundred and fifth, at about the ninth hour of the evening, on flat ground above a landing. A book on two sawn-off stakes, nine-foot boards, about nine hundred names in four years and one name written down twice, a flat book with a chain, a pencil and not a pen, one line in a middle column, four boxes with the last one headed for a man and left empty, four people who did not go and do something else, four bolts, a barrow.*
>
> *Second column, the book and the refusal, and she gave the reason before I could ask. **About nine hundred names over four years, one of them entered twice because a man came off the far end and then came off again and paid twice and wanted it entered twice.** **She has never read it out loud to anybody, never let anybody read it, and never once said a name in it to a person who could not see the landing.** A name in it means a person who owes a landing a small sum of money, and I did not ask what it was for and she told me in four seconds, unprompted. **She is not going to be the second woman on this water to hand a name to a man with a barrow**, and she has not worked out whether the first one was right, and she would like a man with a book to be asked and refused rather than careful with, because being careful is a thing you do with a door. And there is no form in this kingdom on which two people who both keep a book put one in front of the other.*
>
> *Second column, the line, read standing up, in a field gate, at about the sixth hour. **One line, in a middle column, in a pencil, in a district’s own form about a district’s own ground: that there is ground here and that people live on it. No name on it anywhere.** The last of the four boxes is headed for a man and it was left empty and he said so out loud in the ordinary voice while he was writing. **He said it would be in a drawer by about the fourth hour tomorrow, that about nine men on this water will never know it exists and that he is not going to tell them, and that anybody in this county may come and look at it in about forty years.** And what she said about it, which was almost nothing: **it has changed nothing, and that is not a criticism of him and not a defence of the row.** A thing that has been written down is not the same object as a thing that has been entered, and the two rooms are four days apart and neither has a door into the other. And when I asked her whether it was worth his four days she would not give me a weight: he walked four days to write down that people live on ground he has never eaten on, and that is worth four days, and **about nine of us stood there and said nothing back to a man who had told us the shape of what he was going to do before he did it.***
>
> *Third column, what I did not do and why. **I did not write the line out of his book.** A copy of a clerk’s line in my own book would be a fourteenth thing and I have thirteen, and thirteen is not going to become fourteen because a man filled in a box in a field gate. **I did not write down whether the woman on the yes list is on a landing in nine hundred names; she refused before I asked, and the refusal is the finding.** The three partial answers are still three and the nine are still nine and are not nine answers. The schedule went into my hands on the hundred and thirty-fourth day and it is seventy-one days in my pocket, and the name is out of the same day and is seventy-one days, and it is a woman’s name, and I have not used it for anything.*
>
> *The five clauses stand and I am not going through them. Four bolts made fast at the first hour and again at the sixth. **The frame has not been in a building since the thirty-third, a hundred and seventy-two days, and there is a boat shed at the head of that landing with a roof on it and a shut door, and I did not put a cradle in it and did not open the door, and the reason is not the second clause: a clerk was writing a district’s own line nine foot from my barrow and I was not going to be a thing in a man’s working day twice.** The carrier in the sack, **a hundred and twenty-eight days out of the seventy-seventh, not rung once, and I did not touch it.** My own bell is in a sealed yard in Kells and the bar is on the bell and not on the licence, and it does not travel. **There is a handbell in her bag and it is not rung, and nobody has asked her for a reason at any hour of today, including me, and I did not go over to her and did not think about not going over until afterwards.** Nothing was struck, nothing was tied, nothing went into metal, I did not reach for anything, and nothing came.*
>
> *The register is thirteen. **Nothing was asked for, produced, given or taken today by me, and no name was entered on any paper by me.** A district’s own form, in a district’s own book, about a district’s own ground, read standing up, in a field gate, is reading and is not carrying and is not a fourteenth, and I did not take it out of the room it was written in, because it was never in a room. A book of about nine hundred names over a landing is a book and is not a document, because there is no form in this kingdom that can produce it. Sixty-nine days on this road with her and I did not ask her what it was for. Which office is it. **A middle column with one line in it and no name on it anywhere, and about nine miles of water on which nothing has changed.***
