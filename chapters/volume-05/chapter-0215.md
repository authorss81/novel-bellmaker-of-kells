# Chapter 0215 — A Farrier Who Can Refuse and a Lock Man Who Cannot, Standing in the Same Yard With the Same Question

It was the fifth hour of the two hundred and first day and Collis Mardle was doing the second shoe of a grey cart horse on the near side, and he does about nine a day, and the ninth is the one he will not hurry, and on a day when a stranger with a barrow is in the yard from the fifth hour onward the second one takes longer than it takes.

The yard was a hard standing of about forty foot by twenty-five with a smithy along one side, a roof on six posts over about sixteen foot square of it, a stone trough, a rack of shoes, a fire that was banked and not out, and about nine people in and out of it across the day. There was a barrow standing at the butt end of the yard with a stone at each wheel and four bolts in a thing on it, and the man with the barrow had been under the roof since the fifth hour and had not asked for anything.

Mardle did the shoe. He came round the horse, looked at the near fore, took the shoe off with a setting-off hammer and about four strokes, held it up, and turned it over in the light.

"You have a beast by a cart at about the second hour and a beast by a cart at about the sixth," he said, to the horse's shoulder, not to Tomas Vale. "Both of them splayed and neither of them sprung, and somebody has been standing them on a bank with a chain. I do not know the bank and I do not want to know the bank. **The shoe is not the wrong shape. The shoe is the exact shape the last farrier in this county wanted them. That is a different fault and it takes a different four hours.**"

"How do you know whose it is."

"Because I have been setting shoes off for about nine years and I know the way about four farriers made them, and that is the third one, and he is dead, and his pattern came down to me with a shop that I bought off a man who bought it off him." He set the shoe on the anvil. "**And before you ask, and I can see you have been half a second from it: I am not going to tell you what he said about the pattern and I am not going to talk about the shop, and if I do it will be because you are standing in my yard and a man in a yard has nothing else to do.** So write that down, because I have watched four men on this water get a true sentence out of a man in a yard and go off pleased, and I would rather it went into a book than into a man."

---

At about the sixth hour Rell Dray came in with a barrow-team horse he wanted done at about the fourth and had been about nine minutes late, and he came in as though he had a claim on the yard, which he did, and the two of them had about nine words between them before they stopped.

"Your horse goes behind the grey."

"My horse goes now or it goes in two days and I have lime to move."

"Behind the grey or in two days and I do not care which and I have said that." Mardle did not look up. "You are early for your own work and late for mine, and that is not an arrangement anybody made."

"It is an arrangement I made, about nine years ago, and it is the only arrangement on this water that I am in charge of." Dray put the horse's rein over the rack and then turned round and looked at Tomas Vale, and then said, in the voice of a man who has decided to do something, "You. Are you going to ask him the thing."

"Which thing."

"**The thing.** Not a name this time. Whether he will go on holding it." He leaned against the trough. "Because I am going to be in this yard and I have had about two hours of thinking about a sentence and I would rather he answered it in front of you than that you went up the bank and asked him without me here."

Mardle put the hammer down.

"You have been asked about that once, in words, on a bank, with no form, and you said no before he had finished the sentence, and you told him the whole of why, and I heard the end of it from four hundred yards down the bank on the fourth day of this month." He looked at Tomas Vale. "He is going to tell you he cannot refuse. I am going to tell you I have refused twice. And you are going to put the two of us side by side and it is going to be the third thing you have carried and the two of us do not fit and you are not going to be allowed to say so until I have said it for you. Say it now, while I have the hammer."

---

Tomas Vale asked it in the same words at each of them and got two sentences that were the opposite of one another, and he wrote both down in his own book on the anvil bench, standing up, with the pen held in his left hand because his right had been nine hours on a barrow handle, and he carried neither of them anywhere afterward.

"Will you go on holding a line for this water."

Dray: "**I cannot refuse you and I have told you why and I am not going to tell you why a second time in a yard with a farrier in it. A no of mine has nothing behind it. I have gone about on this water for eleven years with a no in my mouth waiting for somebody to put a box under it and nobody has ever put one there and I am not going to be the one who asks.**"

Mardle: "**I have refused twice and I will refuse you now and the second refusal costs me about nine hours of work a year and the first one cost me a customer I had for nine years, and I can tell you exactly what the refusal looks like from outside, which is nothing, because the only things that count are the things in a book, and a no is not a thing anybody can be told about and neither is the work I did instead of it.** That is the whole of what refusing costs a man with no standing. It is not that people do it to you. It is that it does not happen."

"You are both talking about boxes," Dray said, from the trough, "and you are on opposite sides of it, and I have been saying so for about two hours."

"You have," Mardle said, "and neither of us has said so out loud, because there is no form on which two men put a thing they agree about in front of a third, and because I would not want it in a book and he would not want it in a book and that is the only thing we have got in common this morning."

---

At about the seventh hour the horse was done and the grey was done and there were about four people in the yard and nobody was asking for anything, and the farrier banked the fire and did not sit down.

"You have eight of them by now," he said. "I have heard. Ferry, kiln, drove, wall, door, chain, byre, lock. That is eight trades in about twelve days and not one of them has said a word about anybody else's."

"No."

"**Then here is mine and it is the smallest one and I am telling you I know it is the smallest, because I have been a farrier nine years and there is nothing I know that would matter to a man who is not shoeing a horse.**" He put a shoe in the fire and let it go. "A shoe either holds or it does not. There is no third of it. A horse decides at about the fourth hour what it thinks of a thing, and if you shoe to a pattern, then in about nine days you have a shape on nine horses and a beast that has gone lame on a thing that somebody chose in a room he was not in. **A keeper is a man who cannot be blamed. That is the only definition of one I have got and I did not invent it and I do not want it.** And there is no form in this kingdom on which anybody writes down that a man could not be blamed, and so the only men who can do it are the ones nobody has written anything about, and I have been one of those for about nine years, and I have been one of those with about nine people on this water and none of us has ever compared notes, and I would like to compare notes, and there is no place to compare them in."

He looked at the fire.

"You came to this reach on a barrow and you have been asking people what a thing is for, and every one of them has told you, and I want you to understand that you are the first man anybody on this water has asked anything in nine years and that I have been waiting about nine years for somebody to ask, and that those two things are the same and that I hate that they are the same. **If you go up that water and put eight sentences in a row, you will have made eight men into a faction in about nine days, and I will have said one of the eight, and the faction will be there after I am dead and I will not be.** So do not do it. I am not refusing you the question, I am refusing you the row."

---

At about the eleventh hour the last horse went out of the yard at about the walk with a barrow behind it, and the fire was banked, and the grey was standing in about nine foot of straw with a new shoe on the near fore and the old one in the rack. At about the ninth hour of the evening Tomas Vale was on the flat above the yard with the barrow against a stone and the four bolts made fast.

There were eight sentences. A farrier's was the ninth and it was the one he had been told not to put in a row, which was a new kind of refusal, because the others had refused to say something and this one had refused a thing to do with what he said.

He wrote down that he had asked the same question at two men in a yard in the same words and got two opposite sentences and had carried neither of them anywhere, and that it was the second time in about five days that he had done that on purpose, and that doing it on purpose is his method and is not a discovery and he is not going to write it up as one.

And he wrote down that he had not asked the farrier why his master's pattern had come down to him through two men, and that he was not going to, and that a man talking about his master's work is a man talking about his master's work.




> *Day five of the ten. The two hundred and first, at about the ninth hour of the evening, on the flat above a smithy yard. A hard standing, a roof on six posts, a fire banked, a rack of shoes, a setting-off hammer, a trough, a grey cart horse, a barrow-team horse that came at the sixth and went at about the eleventh, four bolts, a barrow.*
>
> *Second column, one question twice in a yard, in the same words. **Dray: I cannot refuse you and I will not give the reason again in a yard with a farrier in it; a no of mine has nothing behind it, and I have gone about on this water for eleven years with a no in my mouth waiting for somebody to put a box under it and nobody ever has.** **Mardle: I have refused twice; the second costs nine hours of work a year and the first cost a customer of nine years, and from outside a refusal looks like nothing, because the only things that count are the things in a book and a no is not a thing anybody can be told about.** Both are about a box and they are on opposite sides of one. **I wrote both on the anvil bench standing up and carried neither anywhere, and he would not let me say they do not fit until he had said it for me.** Second time in five days I have put one question to two people on purpose. It is the method, not a discovery.*
>
> *Second column, his term, which he says is the smallest thing he has got. **A shoe either holds or it does not, and there is no third of it. A horse decides at the fourth hour what it thinks of a thing, and shoe to a pattern and in nine days there is a shape on nine horses and a beast lame on a thing somebody chose in a room he was not in.** A keeper is a man who cannot be blamed, and there is no form in this kingdom on which anybody writes down that a man could not be blamed, so the only men who can do it are the ones nobody has written anything about. **He has been one of those for nine years with nine people on this water, none of whom has ever compared notes with any of the others, and he would like to, and there is no place to compare them in.** Unasked as well: that I am the first man anybody on this water has asked anything in nine years, that he has been waiting nine years to be asked, and that those are the same fact and he hates that they are.*
>
> *Third column, the refusal, and it is a kind I have not had before. **Every other person on this water has refused to say something; he refused a thing to be done with what he said.** Put the eight in a row and eight men are a faction in nine days, and the faction will be there after he is dead and he will not be. I am going to do as he says and I am not going to tell him I have. **The nine in order: a mending, a box he has none of, a place to find each other at with the use taken out of it, three sentences and no fourth, a figure that dies with the man, a thing true only on the day it is true, a heap that has to be spent, a day of the week, and a man who cannot be blamed. The three partial answers are still three and these are nine things and not nine answers. The schedule went into my hands on the hundred and thirty-fourth day and it is sixty-seven days in my pocket, and the name is out of the same day and is sixty-seven days, and it is a woman’s name, and I have not used it for anything.***
>
> *The five clauses stand and I am not going through them. Four bolts made fast at the fifth hour and again at the seventh. **The frame has not been in a building since the thirty-third, a hundred and sixty-eight days, and there is a roof on six posts over about sixteen foot square of that yard with a fire under it, a horse at the near side and a smith at it, and I did not put a cradle under it, and the reason is not the second clause: a man shoeing a horse does not stop for a thing he cannot set down.** The carrier in the sack, **a hundred and twenty-four days out of the seventy-seventh, not rung once, and I did not touch it.** My own bell is in a sealed yard in Kells and the bar is on the bell and not on the licence, and it does not travel. **There is a handbell in her bag and it is not rung, and nobody has asked her for a reason at any hour of today, including me; she was at the butt end of that yard from the seventh hour and did not come in, and I did not go in either.** Nothing was struck, nothing was tied, nothing went into metal, I did not reach for anything, and nothing came.*
>
> *The register is thirteen. **Nothing was asked for, produced, given or taken today, and no name was entered on any paper by me.** A shoe on a rack is not a document and a streak cut in a stone in a yard is not a document. Sixty-five days on this road with her and I did not ask her what it was for, and a man with a hammer told me not to put nine men in a row, and I have put them in a row in this book, and both of those are true. Which office is it. **A farrier who can refuse and a lock man who cannot, nine foot apart in a yard with one question between them, and nine men on nine miles of water who have never compared notes and one of them who has now asked a stranger not to do it for them.***
