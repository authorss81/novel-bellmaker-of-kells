# Chapter 0230 — Ninety-Hand Nails in a Hinge, and None of Them Doing the Job of Any of the Others

It was the third hour of the two hundred and sixteenth day and Tod Alard was hanging a field-gate hinge on a post with a cold chisel and a hammer, and he hangs about nine of them a week and has hung them for about nine years, and the ninth of them on this one was the last, and he knew it was the last one while he was doing it, and the reason is that the other eight were all still in the post and this one was not.

The forge was a shed against the wall of the row of about nine doors, and the fire was in it and the fire wanted somebody in the room about half the time, and the man was in the room.

The gate he was hanging it on was not the gate anybody used. It was a field gate nine foot off the lane that went down to the water, and it had been off its hinge and lying in the nettles since a spate in the spring, and he had cut the hinge himself out of a bar he had and set the post himself, and the only reason he was doing the job at all was that a gate off its hinge is a gate a drove will come through, and there was a drove that had come through a footway nine foot from that gate nine days ago.

---

Tomas Vale came up the lane with the barrow and stopped about nine foot off the post, and Tod Alard drove the last nail and put the chisel in his belt and looked at the gate for a while without saying anything.

"Nine in it," he said.

"Nine."

"**Eight were in the post and one was not and it is the ninth, and I knew that when I had it in my hand, and a man who knows that a nail is the last of nine has not got a trade. He has got a counting.**" He swung the gate once on the new hinge and it came square. "I will tell you what a hinge is, and then you can go and do whatever you came up this lane to do. **A hinge is nine nails and every one of the nine is holding a different thing. One is holding the leaf to the post. Two are holding the strap. Three are holding the pin out so the strap cannot walk in on the pin. Four and five and six are holding a thing in a hinge that has no name and that a man who mends hinges could not tell you the name of and that he would not be without.** Seven holds the strap flat where it crosses. Eight holds the head. Nine goes last because it goes across the end of the other eight and there is nowhere for it to go until they are in."

"And not one of the nine is doing the job of any of the others."

"**No. And that is the whole of a hinge and it has taken me nine years and about nine thousand hinges, and you have brought it up this lane to a nailer in about nine minutes.**" He wiped his hands. "I do not know what you came about. I do not know what a nailer has got to do with anything that happens on nine miles of water. Say it and I will tell you what I know and then I will go back to my fire, and I am telling you that first because I have got a fire and about four degrees of morning left in it."

---

At about the sixth hour they were sitting on the anvil block with the fire going, and Tomas Vale had the book out and did not open it, and told him instead, out loud, in plain words, in a shed with a fire in it.

"**Nine people in nine trades on nine miles of water have each said one true thing to me in ten days. I cannot make the nine into terms, I have known that for about six days, and this morning I am saying it out loud to a man who is right, and I would rather say it to a nailer than to anybody else on this reach.** They are not a programme and not a set, they do not fit one another, and if I put them in a row and call it a settlement I will be doing to nine miles of water exactly what a sheet does to a mile of ground, and I have been here three weeks and I know that."

Tod Alard looked at the fire for about as long as it takes a nail to cool.

"I have no idea what any of that means," he said, "**and I am not going to pretend to, and I am not going to say you are wrong either, because I have been on nine miles of water for about nine years and the only people I have ever heard a sentence from are the ones who were standing next to me holding a thing that needed holding.** I have a nailer. I have nine nails in a hinge and none of them doing another's job. If that is not a settlement then it is not anything, and I would not know, and I am not going to be told by a man with a barrow in a shed."

"You are not thanked for hearing it."

"I did not hear it for your sake and you are not thanking me, and if you are going to stand there looking as though you have given me something, you can go and find a thing that needs a nail in it, because I have about nine of them and they are not hard."

---


At about the seventh hour he said the other thing, and he said it while he was banking the fire, and he had not been asked, and the fire wanted coal and he had about nine shovelfuls of it in a bucket and used about two.

"**There is a footway over that drain at the head of this lane with two planks in it and a handrail at the bottom, and I could put that right this afternoon. A foot of oak and about nine nails and a spare hand, and about a day's work, and I would do it in about half a day.** And I am not going to do it, and I want you to hear the reason, and then I am going to bank this fire and go home, and I am not going to say it twice and I am not going to say it to anybody else on nine miles of water for about nine years."

"Go on."

"**A thing a man can be blamed for goes in a book. A thing nobody can be blamed for gets done.** That is the trade of the whole of this water and there is not a man on it who could not tell you it. **Putting that footway right is a thing a man can be blamed for. The third year, a child, a horse, a wet week and a man who has decided the footway was the thing he did. So it goes in a book. And there is no book. So it is not done, and it will not be done, and it is not done because there is nowhere to put the blame, and not because nobody wants it done.**" He pushed the fire back with the poker. "And a cooper at this end of the top road loses a day and a half a round and a thatcher at the fourth mile has got a foot and about nine roofs and three weeks, and that is the whole of what this week cost nine miles of water, and there is not one thing on any page anywhere that says any of it. **So it stays. I am the man who could fix it and I am going to let it stay, and in about nine years a room is going to say a reach did not mend its own road, and that will be true, and it will be true because of a nailer with a fire.**"

---

He had gone to the bench and taken the hinge off the gate post and laid it flat and was putting the eight nails he had taken out of it into a bit of split oak, one at a time, in the order they came out of the gate, and he did not say anything while he did it, and the fire went down while he did it, and Tomas Vale watched a man put eight nails in a row in the order they came out and understood, about nine foot off, that the whole conversation of the day had been in the order of the ninth.

"You have been counting since the first hour," he said.

"I have been putting the hinge back on. It is the same thing and it takes the same length of time." He did not look up. "**You are going to say something now about the ninth and about a bar and about a thing not being joined, and I want to get this in before you do. There is a heap of shoes in that corner about the size of a hearthstone and about nine of them are not mended and nobody in this row has asked me for one, and I have not mended one in about nine days and I will not mend one in about nine more, and I could not tell you a reason that would fit in a mouth. That is what a nailer is. That is the whole of what a nailer is, and there is no more of it than that, and if you have got nine trades on this reach and about nine of them are like that heap, then you have got a row of nine heaps and not a reach, and I would rather have said it in the first hour and I did not have the words until about now.**" 

At about the tenth hour the light had gone and Alard had his coat on and the fire was down to a bed, and the nine nails were in a row on the bench and the gate was standing open on one of them, and he left the ninth unhammered.

"You could have gone and found somebody," he said, when the nine were in a row on the bench and the gate was standing open on one nail.

"I could have."

"**I have heard the name of the reach in about nine years off about four people, and about nine of them have never been up this lane.** You could have gone down it and found the baker and the woman with the two pages and the man with the besom, and put your nine in front of all of them in one week, and every one of them would have said aye, and you would have had a reach of about nine yeses and no way on earth of finding out whether they were ayes."

"That is what I am deciding not to do."

"**Then decide it properly and say the price out loud, because a decision without a price is not a decision, it is a preference, and I have met about nine men on this water with preferences and not one of them has ever been wrong about a nail.**" He set the ninth nail in beside the others and did not hammer it. "The price is that the nine die. They die on their own, in nine mouths, in nine trades, and about nine years from now there is nobody on this water who could say that nine people said nine true things. **And that is not a wound and do not write it as one. A wound is a thing that happens to a person. This is a week of a man's labour on the top road and a stack of reed in a yard and a lane where a woman carries nine foot five days running, and none of those three people will ever know what it was the price of, including you.** Now go up to the end of the lane and make your four bolts, and do not come up here to tell me the decision, because I have a fire and I have said the whole of it."

---

He was up the lane and on the flat above the lower end by about the ninth hour of the evening, and the fire was down to a bed by the time he had the barrow on the hard ground with the four bolts made fast, and four miles up the top road the forge door was shut, and at the head of a lane a field gate was hanging square for the first time since a spate in the spring, and a footway over a drain was not mended, and a woman with a pail was going the long way round for the ninth time that week.

Tomas Vale made his decision in the shed with a man who owed him nothing and got nothing out of it, and the decision was that he was not going to go and find a person to say the terms to, and the price of it was a week of somebody else's labour on nine miles of water that would not come back and would not be written down, and the price was not a wound and he wrote it in the plainest words he had.

He wrote the six things down, and the ninth, and the eighty-two days of the schedule and the eleven lines and the name of a woman in the same pocket, and he wrote that he had not said the name and would not and that a form with four boxes in it was in a gravedigger's coat with the day empty in it, and that a clerk had left another day empty on purpose and been right, and that he had a bar in his own book under nine sentences from nine trades and the bar means do not put these next to each other, and that he was keeping it, and that keeping it was the whole of the decision.

Then he wrote that the question had stopped being a sentence and had become a road, and that a road is a fact about the people who walk it, and that he had nine miles of it and about nine people on it who had each given him one true thing, and that he was not going to arrange them, and that the arrangement was the one thing he was good at, and that he was not going to do it, and that there was nobody to hand and the decision had been made in a shed by a man who told him to make it properly.



> *Day ten of the ten. The two hundred and sixteenth, at about the ninth hour of the evening, on flat ground above the lower end of the reach. A forge shed against a row of about nine doors, a fire, a cold chisel, a hammer, a field gate hanging square on nine nails, a footway over a drain with two planks in it and a handrail at the bottom, an anvil block, a barrow, four bolts, a leaf in a book, and a book with a bar in it under nine rows.*
>
> *Second column, the hinge, and the batch's finding in a nailer's own mouth. **Nine nails in a hinge and every one of the nine holding a different thing, the ninth going last across the end of the other eight, and not one of the nine doing the job of any of the others.** He said it had taken him nine years and about nine thousand hinges and that I brought it to him in about nine minutes, and that he had no idea what the rest of it meant and was not going to pretend and was not going to say I was wrong. **I said out loud, in a shed with a fire in it, that the nine are not a programme and not a set and do not fit one another and that I am not going to lay anything over the top of them to make them fit, and I said it to a man who is right, and he told me he was not thanked and that if I stood there looking as though I had given him something I could go and find a thing that needed a nail in it.** Nobody was thanked today and nobody was thanked on any of the ten days.
>
> *Second column, the footway, and the price, and it is the batch. **A foot of oak and about nine nails and a spare hand and half a day, and it is not going to be done, because a thing a man can be blamed for goes in a book and there is no book.** A cooper loses a day and a half a round; a thatcher has a foot and about nine roofs and three weeks; a woman carries nine foot of reed up a lane five days running and knows nothing of it. In about nine years a room is going to say a reach did not mend its own road, and it will be true, and it will be true because of a nailer with a fire. **The terms were true all along. They were true in the hands that gave them and they were true in a man who will not put a footway right, and the cost is a week of somebody else's labour and not a bell, and nothing was rung on any of the ten days and nothing was bound.**
>
> *Third column, the decision, its price, the bar, and the things I cannot fix. **The decision is that I am not going to go and find a person to say the nine terms to. Not the baker, not the woman with two pages, not the man with the besom, not the ford, not the bank.** Every one of them would have said aye and every aye would have been free and about nine of them have never been up a lane at all, and a reach of about nine yeses has no way on earth of being checked. He made me say the price out loud, and it is this: **the nine die in nine mouths in nine trades, and about nine years from now there will be nobody on this water who could say that nine people said nine true things, and that is not a wound. A wound is a thing that happens to a person. This is a week of a man's labour, a stack of reed, and a lane, and none of those three people will ever know what it was the price of, including me.** And the bar: **I have nine sentences in this book with a bar under the last line and the bar means do not put these next to each other, and I am keeping the bar, and keeping it is the whole of the decision, and it is the one arrangement I have left that nobody has to agree to anything.** The six are the six they have been all week and the ninth is about nine roofs and a stack of reed. The three partial answers are still three. The schedule went into my hands on the hundred and thirty-fourth day and it is eighty-two days in my pocket, and the eleven lines and the name are out of the same day, and I have not said the name in a room in eighty-two days and nobody asked me for it today.
>
> *The five clauses stand and I am not going through them. Four bolts made fast at the seventh hour on the hard ground at the head of the lane and again at the tenth. **The frame has not been in a building since the thirty-third, a hundred and eighty-three days, and there is a forge shed roof six foot off that barrow with a fire in it that wanted somebody in the room about half the time, and I did not put a cradle in it, and the reason is not the second clause: a forge floor is a floor a man works sparks across and there is nothing in it a cradle could go inside of, and I was not going to bring a barrow up to a fire in the hour a man was telling me the whole of a hinge.** The carrier in the sack, **a hundred and thirty-nine days out of the seventy-seventh, not rung once, and I did not touch it.** My own bell is in a sealed yard in Kells and the bar is on the bell and not on the licence, and it does not travel. **There is a handbell in her bag and it is not rung, and it was rung once in this whole matter, on her own account, in a market, in front of about nine hundred people, and it is not for me, and nobody has asked her for a reason at any hour of any of the ten days, including me.** Nothing was struck, nothing was tied, nothing went into metal, I reached for nothing, and nothing came.
>
> *The register is thirteen. And it was thirteen on the first of the ten days and the two figures are the same figure, which is the only thing this entry can say about the whole of the week. **Nothing was asked for, produced, given or taken on any of the ten days of this week by me, and no name was entered on any paper by anybody, and a hinge, a chisel, a fire, a gate, two planks in a drain and a bar in a man's own book are not documents, because there is no form on which a man can produce a bar he drew himself.** **Nobody was thanked on any of the ten days, and about nine of them told me true things, and one of them told me four he had told nobody in nine years, and one of them told me not to write a name down, and one of them made me say a price out loud in a shed, and I could not have paid one of them back.** Eighty days on this road with her and I did not ask her what it was for, and she has never asked me, and the counter is a day and not an act. Which office is it. **Nine nails in a hinge, and none of them doing the job of any of the others, and a footway that is not mended because there is nowhere to put the blame, and a bar in a book that says do not put these next to each other, and a road.**
