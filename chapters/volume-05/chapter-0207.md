# Chapter 0207 — What a Man Says to a Stranger at a Door, and Then Says the Thing That Makes It Useless

It was the first hour of the hundred and ninety-third day and Hesta Bray was at her own door under a bank with a hod half full of stone and a shovel, and she was in the middle of putting the stone in, because there is a place under her house where the ground has gone soft in three wet years running and this was the third winter of it and she had been putting stone in it since the first hour with nobody watching, and the fourth door down the row from hers had about nine foot of a wall in it that is not old and that nobody has ever asked her about.

Tomas Vale came down the row at about the fourth hour with the barrow behind him and stopped about nine feet off her hod, because a woman putting stone into a hole at the first hour is a woman doing a job she has decided to do and not a woman waiting to be spoken to.

"You are the one with the barrow," she said, without straightening up.

"That is me."

"You have come down a row of about nine doors at about the fourth hour and you have stopped at the fourth, and there is nothing at the fourth, and that is not a coincidence and I would like you to notice that I have noticed it."

"I have noticed."

---

At about the fifth hour she had the stone in and the hole dressed and a barrow-load of lime beside it, and she sat down on the step with her back to the bank and let him come and stand where he liked, and he stood where he liked for about a minute and a half, and then he said the thing he had come to say.

"I want to know what a person on this reach thinks that thing up the water is for. Not the stones and not the frame. What it is for."

"**A place you can find each other at.**"

He waited for the rest of it and the rest of it did not come for about as long as it takes a woman to decide whether a stranger is going to be disappointed.

"That is the sentence," she said. "That is the whole of the sentence and you may have it, and I have not said it to anybody before and I am not saying it because I believe it, I am saying it because it is what people say about it, and I have had about nine years of hearing what people say about it at about nine doors."

"That is not—"

"**Now sit down, because I have been waiting since about the fourth hour to tell you the other half and you have not let me.** The reach has been doing without it for nine years. It has been doing without it for nine years the way you are doing without a church, and nobody on nine miles of water would notice nine more, and if I said it had gone tomorrow the only people who would notice are the four on the yes list, and the four on the yes list are the only people on this water who have said yes to anything at all."

Tomas Vale sat down on the step nine feet off her.

"That is true," he said.

"I know it is true, I live here." She turned round and looked at him. "So there it is. You came down a row of doors to find out what a thing is for, and I have told you what it is for, and it is for a place you can find each other at, and it has not been needed for nine years, and there is not one person on this reach you could say that to who would do anything about it. Now you are going to sit there and try to find a next question, and I want to tell you before you find it that I have watched nine men find one, and every one of them was a man who had come to put a thing right and could not leave without having begun."

"I have not got a next question."

"You have got one. You have had it since the first hour and you have been standing nine feet off my hod working round it. Ask it or do not, but do not stand there being careful at me, because I have a soft place under this house and I have not finished it."

---

At about the sixth hour he did not ask it, and it cost him the one thing he had come up the bank for, and he wrote that down in the ordinary hand afterwards and did not improve it.

What he had come to ask was whether the four on the yes list would be harmed if the thing up the water stopped being a place, and he had wanted her to tell him they would not, and she would not have told him that, because a woman at a door under a bank with a hole in the ground under her house is not going to promise you that four strangers are safe on the strength of a morning, and he knew that before he got to her and he had asked it anyway, out of habit, and the habit is a man trying to get a promise out of a person who owes him nothing.

He said instead: "There is a lean-to at the end of this row with a fire going in it and nobody in it, and I have not got a bed tonight, and I am not asking you for anything. I am telling you where I am going to be so that if the row wants to know where the barrow is, it knows."

"That is the first sensible thing anybody with a barrow has said on this row," she said, "and it is not a favour and there is nothing to be grateful for, because you have not asked me for anything and there is no form in this kingdom in which a woman who has been asked for nothing can be thanked for it."

Isa Trevail had come down the row and back about the sixth hour and stopped at the lean-to and looked at the fire in it for a while, and said that a fire in a lean-to is put there by somebody who is coming back to it, and that the last three people who had slept under that roof had each left something under it — a chip of a bowl, a bootlace, a wedge — and that none of them had been asked to and that she did not know what the rule was and was not going to invent one.

At the fourth door from hers, Tamsin Rook had been standing since the fifth hour with her hand flat on a post.

It was a post of the kind a bank is held up with: squared, tarred at the top against the weather, set nine inches into the ground with a stone under it, and about nine foot of it above the place her hand was on. The four of them had gone down the row and come back and Tamsin Rook had stopped at that one and had not gone on, and she had her other hand on the wall on the other side of the door, and the wall was not old. Nine courses of brick, and the ninth holding, and the tenth gone since before anybody in that row was born, and she could feel the difference between the two in about four seconds with a hand on each, and it is a difference and not a mystery, and it is the sort of thing three people on this water have said out loud and the other nine hundred have not.

What she wrote in her own book that night, in her own hand, was the post and the brick socket and the stone under it and the ninth course holding and the tenth gone. It was not about the number of people on the reach, because she was not doing a count. Nobody asked her for a reason at any hour of that day, including him, and he was nine feet off her at about the sixth hour with a row of nine doors in between them and did not go over.

---

At about the seventh hour the row was empty and the light was off the bank and Tomas Vale was at the end of it with the barrow on flat ground and the four bolts made fast, and Hesta Bray came out with a blanket over her arm that she was not going to give him and did not offer.

"One more thing, since you are standing there," she said, "and then I am going to bed. Somebody has been asking after a woman on this reach. I do not know when it started and I do not know who and I am not going to guess in front of you. That is all I am going to say about it and I have said it once and I am not going to say it again, and if you go up that bank and ask anybody else about it I will not have said it."

"Does it have a name."

"I do not know if it has a name. I know a person went into that up there and did not come back out and I know somebody has been asking."

"Twelve years?"

"What is that for."

"Nothing. You do not have to answer it and I am not going to say why I asked."

"Twelve," she said, "or near it, and that is the second time and it is the last, and I have never met him and I have never seen him and I am not going to go up that bank to look, and the man who keeps that thing going is not the man anybody would send a stranger to. **He has been at it nine years and he has not once had a stranger sent to him, and I know that because in nine years nobody has come up this bank that was not one of us, and you are the first, and I would not have said that to you at the fourth hour.**"

He wrote the sentence down before the light went altogether, on flat ground above the end of the row, with the barrow against a stone and the four bolts made fast, and he wrote it and did not join it to the three things in a bench room or to a gap in a wall two hundred yards down the reach, and he wrote the number of years in his own hand and did not subtract anything from anything.

At about the ninth hour of the evening he was at the end of the row with the barrow on flat ground and the four bolts made fast, and eight of the nine doors were shut and one was not, and he did not knock on the one that was not, and she did not come out of it, and neither of those two facts was about anybody.

> *The hundred and ninety-third, at about the ninth hour of the evening, on flat ground above the end of a row. A hod, a shovel, lime, a hole under a house, a lean-to with a fire in it, a post tarred at the top, nine courses of brick with the tenth gone, a blanket over an arm that was not offered, four bolts.*
>
> *Second column, the sentence and then the other half. **A place you can find each other at** — and she said she had not said it before, and was not saying it because she believes it, only because it is what nine years of nine doors say. Then the half that was the point: **nine years without it, like a reach without a church, and nobody from here to the top of the water would feel nine more, and the only anybody who would feel anything is a page in a book in a room over a shop three miles down the water, and that is the whole of the noticing there is.** She is right. Agreeing with her is the whole of what happened to me this afternoon, and there is nothing to argue with and nowhere to put the argument.
>
> *Third column, ruled apart. **I did not ask the second question and it is the one I came up the bank for.** What I wanted out of her was the four on the yes list held safe, and she was never going to hand me that. A woman with a hole under her floor does not stake four strangers on the strength of one morning, and I had that straight at the gate and not at her door, which is what I am for. I asked it anyway, out of the oldest habit I have got, and the habit is a man trying to get a promise out of somebody who owes him nothing. **What I gave her instead was where the barrow sleeps tonight, which cost me nothing and told her more.**
>
> *Third column, said once. **Somebody has been asking after a woman on this water and nobody on this row will say who, and she has never laid eyes on him and is not climbing that bank to look. She gave me a figure — twelve years, or near it — and told me it was the second time she had said it and the last, and I am not asking anybody else about it, and I have not written down who the somebody is, and I am not going to look this month.** She also told me, having never met him, that the man who keeps that thing going is not the one anybody would send a stranger to: nine years on it, never once a stranger sent, nobody up that bank in nine years who was not one of them, and I am the first. She would not have said that at the fourth hour and I have written down that she said it at the seventh, because that is the fact.
>
> *The five clauses stand and I am not going through them. The four bolts went in at the fourth hour at the end of the row and again at the seventh, and they were where I put them. **The frame has not been in a building since the thirty-third, a hundred and sixty days. There was a roof at the end of this row with a fire burning under it and nobody in it, and I did not go under it.** The reason is not the clause: a fire left by people who are coming back is a thing a man does not put a barrow inside of, and I would rather sleep nine feet off it. The carrier in the sack, **a hundred and sixteen days out of the seventy-seventh, and I did not touch it.** **There is a handbell in her bag and it is hers, and nothing of hers went today, and nobody has asked her for a reason at any hour of it, including me; she was at the fourth door with her hand on a post for about an hour, and she wrote her own entry afterwards about the post and the wall and not about the number of people on this reach, because she was not doing a count.** Nothing was struck and nothing was tied and nothing went into metal, and I did not reach for anything and nothing came.
>
> *The register is thirteen, nothing was asked for, produced, given or taken today, no name was entered on any paper by me, and no room receipt and no hire paper and no arrangement of any kind exists. **Fifty-seven days on this road with her and I did not ask her what it was for.** Nine feet off a woman and nine feet off a man are not the same distance and I keep writing them as though they were, and she has never once corrected me. My own bell is in a sealed yard in Kells and the bar is on the bell and not on the licence, and it does not travel.
>
> *Which office is it. **Nine doors under a bank, a soft place nobody is paying for, a lean-to with a fire in it, a post, a wall with its top course gone since before the row, and a name that somebody on this water has been asking after and that one woman has said out loud twice in nine years.**
