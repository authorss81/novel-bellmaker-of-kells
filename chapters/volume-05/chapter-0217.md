# Chapter 0217 — A Man Who Says a Thing Three Times and Then Goes and Mends a Wall

It was the second hour of the two hundred and third day and Sander Vell was standing in a gap in a hedge with his hoe over his shoulder, looking at about four hundred yards of wall and about the same of ditch, and the wall was a boundary wall between two holdings and about half of it was standing and the other half was not, and the half that was not standing was his side.

He had come up at the second hour and he had said he was going back down at about the ninth hour whatever happened, and the same dog was with him and was still not interested.

Tomas Vale came up the foot way from the water with the barrow and stopped on the other side of the gap, and the dog looked at the barrow and then stopped looking at the barrow.

"You said you would say it a third time in nine days," Vell said. "It is nine days. I counted them, and I want you to know that I counted them, and I want it understood that counting them does not make this the fourth time."

"I have not asked you for anything."

"You are going to, and I would rather do it in a gap in a hedge than on a bank with the water behind me, so here it is. **Walking the same ground is not agreeing.** Third time. After this I am going to mend a wall and I am not going to say it a fourth time to you or to anybody you send, and if you ask me a fourth time I am going to say the first half again, because the first half is the true half and the second half is the one you would like."

He shouldered the hoe and went into the gap.

---

At about the third hour he was pulling the fallen side out with the hoe and it was about nine courses of a wall built by two men over about nine years, and Tomas Vale stood in the gap and watched because a man taking a wall out is doing a thing that cannot be hurried and cannot be helped.

"Which half is whose."

"That is the question. It is not in the wall." He levered a stone out and set it down flat. "That side of that wall is mine, because the other side is Tomlin Sole's and Tomlin Sole's wife, and I have the wall on my side of the line and he has it on his, and we built it together in about four years and it came down on my half about nine years back in the wet and it has not been touched since, and the other half is standing and has been repointed twice." He put the hoe down and wiped his hands. "**I know it has been repointed twice. I can see the lime in it from here and it is lime off a kiln about four miles down the water and it is newer than the wall. And I have not said one word to him about it in about nine years and I am not going to start now, and I have never once said to him that I am going to mend my half, and he has never once asked me whether I was.**"

"Then it is his business to leave your half."

"It is his business and he knows it and I know he knows it and neither of us is going to do the first thing about it, and we are not unfriendly, and we are not going to build the wall together again, and I want you to write that second sentence down in whatever you have been writing in and I do not want you to improve it." He picked the hoe up. "**A boundary is not a wall. A wall is two men. A boundary is a line with nobody on it, and every man on this reach has held one for about forty years by putting a stone on it in the spring, and not one of us has ever made a form about the putting of the stone.**"

---

At about the sixth hour the four people were up the mile and a man with a satchel and a flat book was on the road above the top of it, and he was not going down to the water and he was not coming to them, and he was standing about two hundred yards off with his book shut under his arm looking at a soft place at the top of a mile that is not a soft place.

Vell saw him first.

"That is a district man."

"Yes."

"He has been on this reach about two days and he has a flat book and he has not asked a soul anything that I have heard." He wiped the hoe on his leg. "Now I am going to say a thing and then I am going to go back to the wall, and I am not going to be standing near you when I say it, because you will want to be able to say I was not trying to stop you."

"Go on."

"**A man with a flat book does not want a road. He wants somewhere to write.**" Vell moved about nine feet further off. "And you have got the road at the top of that mile. You have had it nine days and you have never told anybody about it except three of your own and a woman who keeps a book, and a man with a book is going to come up that hill in about nine minutes and ask you what is at the top of it, because that is what the book is for. **And if you show him, then in about nine days there is a road on paper at the top of that mile and a man in a county who can send somebody to look at it, and it will not be a road any more, it will be a thing somebody is answerable for, and about nine years ago four different men in this county put a thing on a piece of paper and it stopped being mended.** I have watched that happen about nine times. I have never once been the one who showed anybody the road, and I have told two people about it and one of them was a woman who deserved to know and the other one was a man who already knew."

"He has not asked me yet."

"He is going to. And when he does, tell him what the woman said, and tell him a place people live, and tell him about the nine doors and the gates and the two names, and it will be the truth and it will not be enough, and that is the correct answer and I am not going to be able to tell you it is a bad one." He stopped. "**And I am telling you this on the day you came to write down what nine men said, and I want it understood that I know that is what you are doing, and that a man who has been a walker for nine days and who came up for a wall does not think the worse of you for it, and that a man who has been a walker for nine days and who came up for a wall thinks you should be told.**

---

At about the seventh hour Vell was laying the first course and Tomas Vale was in the gap with a straight edge in his hand and did not use it, and the reason was four words and they were the same four words they had been on the fourth day of this month at a byre, and this time he said them out loud to the man on the other side of the wall, which was new, and the man laughed once and stopped.

"You have got a straight edge in your hand and you have not put it on anything."

"It is not my wall."

"It is not your wall and it is not my wall either, it is a boundary and Tomlin Sole's wall and mine, and I have just told you that." He set a stone and tapped it. "**Use it on your own ground or not at all. There is nothing in the world to be gained by a man putting a true line on a wall he does not own, and the only thing to be lost is the thing that made him worth listening to on the nine other days.**"

---

At about the twelfth hour the light had gone off the bank and there was one lamp burning in about four hundred yards at the top of a mile, and Tamsin Rook was in the road above it on her own, standing in the ruts with the water still in them, and she was not doing anything at all for about a quarter of an hour and then she wrote one entry in her own hand, and it was about a road, and about the width of it, and about how water stands in a thing that is made for carts and does not stand in a thing that is made for feet, and it was not about the number of people on this reach and it was not about the reach, because she was not doing a count and she has not been asked to and she is not going to be.

Nobody asked her anything at any hour of that day, including him, and he was nine hundred yards down the bank at the time and did not know she had gone up there, and did not find out until about the ninth hour of the evening from the farrier's boy at the mill, and did not go and look, and wrote that down.

---

At about the ninth hour of the evening the barrow was on flat ground above the lower end of the reach with the four bolts made fast, and a man nine hundred yards up the bank had said a thing three times and then gone and put nine courses of a wall back up, and would not say it again.

Tomas Vale had the nine sentences and had been told by two people on this water not to put them in a row, and had been told by a fourth not to show a man with a book the road at the top of a mile, and he had not done the second thing and could not un-do the first, and he wrote down that the day had contained two refusals aimed at him and one small kindness that was not a kindness and that a farrier had not known about, and that he was going to have to decide in about four days whether the nine sentences were his or anybody's, and that the only person who had ever given him a straight answer to that was the one person on this water who did not know he had been asked.




> *Day seven of the ten. The two hundred and third, at about the ninth hour of the evening, on flat ground above the lower end of the reach. A gap in a hedge, a hoe, a boundary wall of about nine courses with half of it down, about four hundred yards of ditch, a straight edge in a hand and not on a stone, a district man with a flat book two hundred yards off, four bolts, a barrow.*
>
> *Second column, the third time. **Walking the same ground is not agreeing. Third time.** He counted the nine days out loud and said counting them does not make it a fourth time, and that he would not say it a fourth time to me or to anybody I send. He then went and mended a wall, which is what he said he would do in nine days on the fourth day of this month, and a man who says a thing three times and then goes and does a thing is the only person on this water I have not had to argue with.*
>
> *Second column, said once, standing nine feet further off, and he moved so that I could say he was not trying to stop me. **A man with a flat book does not want a road; he wants somewhere to write.** **If I show him, then in nine days there is a road on paper and a man in a county who can send somebody to look at it, and it will not be a road any more, it will be a thing somebody is answerable for, and about nine years ago four different men in this county put a thing on a piece of paper and it stopped being mended.** He has watched that about nine times and has never once shown anybody a road. He also said that he knows what I am doing, that a man who came up for a wall does not think the worse of me for it, and that a man who came up for a wall thinks I should be told. **I have not shown him and I am not going to.***
>
> *Third column, the straight edge. **I had it in my hand at the seventh hour and I did not put it on anything, and the reason is four words and they are the same four words as at the byre on the fourth day of this month, and this time I said them out loud to him, and he laughed once and stopped.** Two refusals came at me today and I have not done the second and cannot undo the first. In about four days I have to settle whether those nine sentences are mine or theirs, and the only man on this water who could tell me is the one who does not know he has been asked. The three partial answers are still three and the nine are still nine and are not nine answers. The schedule went into my hands on the hundred and thirty-fourth day and it is sixty-nine days in my pocket, and the name is out of the same day and is sixty-nine days, and it is a woman’s name, and I have not used it for anything.*
>
> *The five clauses stand and I am not going through them. Four bolts made fast at the second hour and again at the sixth. **The frame has not been in a building since the thirty-third, a hundred and seventy days, and there is no roof within nine hundred yards of this barrow today and the question did not arise, and I am not going to write that up as a reason, because a day with no roof in it is not a day on which I chose not to go under one.** The carrier in the sack, **a hundred and twenty-six days out of the seventy-seventh, not rung once, and I did not touch it.** My own bell is in a sealed yard in Kells and the bar is on the bell and not on the licence, and it does not travel. **There is a handbell in her bag and it is not rung, and nobody has asked her for a reason at any hour of today, including me; she was on the road at the top of a mile at about the twelfth hour with the water standing in the ruts and wrote one entry about the width of a road, and it was not about the number of people on this reach.** Nothing was struck, nothing was tied, nothing went into metal, I did not reach for anything, and nothing came.*
>
> *The register is thirteen. **Nothing was asked for, produced, given or taken today, and no name was entered on any paper by me.** A gap in a hedge, a hoe, a wall and a book shut under an arm: none of them is a document. **A road I have not shown to anybody is not a discovery and I am not going to write it up as one.** Sixty-seven days on this road with her and I did not ask her what it was for, and I did not know where she had gone and did not go and look, and those are the same silence. Which office is it. **A boundary wall with half of it down for about nine years and a man putting the first course back tonight, and a district man two hundred yards off with a flat book who wants somewhere to write and has not been shown anything.***
