# Chapter 0211 — A Man Who Counts Nine Stones and Does Not Want to Be One of the Nine

It was the first hour of the hundred and ninety-seventh day and Emmer Drue was counting aloud inside a byre on the fourth mile, and he counts the same eight beasts in the same order in the same doorway, and the counting takes about as long as it takes a man to say eight numbers and say them low, and about nine years of mornings had put the number in the order of the doorway rather than in the order of the beasts.

He put his hand on the rump of the last one as he said it, the way a man does who is not sure the beast has moved and is not looking.

The byre was up a short bank off the road with a door that was open and about nine foot of depth of cow in it and a smell of it that had got into the lime on the walls about nine years ago and would still be there when the walls came down. The far wall was soft to about the height of a man's chest and the man who owned it knew. The door in the near wall was not old. It was a brick frame round an opening cut to fit afterwards, and the cut was clean, and there was a mark on the rubble either side of it that could not have been made with a free hand.

Tomas Vale came up the short bank at about the second hour with the barrow behind him and stopped at about nine foot, because a man counting aloud in a doorway at the first hour is a man doing the one thing he is certain of doing today.

"Eight," Drue said, before he had looked up. "And it was eight yesterday and it will be nine next week and I will know that on the day and not before."

"You counted them yesterday."

"I count them every morning. You do not count a thing once. That is what counting is: it is a thing you do again."

---

At about the fourth hour they were on the flat ground above the water with the barrow on beaten earth and a stone at each wheel, and the man had not asked for anything and had told Tomas Vale two things before he had been asked one of them.

"I am the third one on a list," Drue said. "A woman over at the lower end asked me about two years ago whether I would hold a line, and I said yes, and she wrote it down in her own book on the table in front of me, and that is the whole of what happened and it took about nine minutes. I am telling you that because you are going to ask me whether I am on a list, and if I wait for you to ask it I will have had a chance to think about how I want to be standing when you do."

"You were on it two years before I came to this water."

"I was on it before I knew there was a water. I did not know what a line was then either, and she said line, and I said yes, and she wrote it, and that is the last of it. I have never been asked anything since and I have never refused anything since, and if you are a man who has come to ask me the second thing I am telling you now, before we start, that there is no second thing."

Tomas Vale stood about nine foot off a byre wall that was not old and did not put his hand out.

"Who is the fourth one."

"I do not know and it is not that I am being careful. I have never asked her. You do not ask a woman who keeps a book what else is in the book; that is a thing you learn in about two years of living near one, and I have learned it."

"You are a man on a list and you are telling me the count is nine and not ten."

"The count is nine stones and about nine people on the water and I have never once put the two together, and if I put the two together then I am a man who has counted a thing in order to say something about it, and that is a different trade and I do not have it."

---

At about the sixth hour Drue came past on his own way back from the low end with four beasts behind him and a stick in his hand, and he came up the last four hundred yards because his way up from the water goes past the flat ground and has gone past it for nine years, and Tomas Vale was standing at the low wall and had been for about a quarter of an hour with his hands behind him, and a man walking four beasts does not stop because two people are standing in his road, so Drue came up to the stones, turned the beasts round against the low wall so that they were not going anywhere, and then counted the stones.

He counted them out loud. He put a finger on each one as he said the number, and he did not hurry and he did not look at the man standing nine foot off, and the number was nine.

"There," he said.

"That is the fourth time I have seen somebody do that."

"You have seen me do it nine thousand times and I have never seen you do it, so we are not in the same position and I would rather you did not put us in it."

"You counted them and nothing was struck and nothing went in the board."

"Nothing was struck and nothing went in the board and I have not put my name in the board and I am not going to, and that is the second thing you came for, and I said you would not get to it so I will give it to you now and it will cost me the rest of the day." He put the stick down against the wall. "**I count them because I am counting things from the first hour to the dark and my hand is on nine hundred beasts a week and there is no ninth stone anywhere else in nine miles. If I write down that I count them then the count is a thing I said, and a thing I said is a thing somebody can ask me what I meant by, and I have not got a fourth answer for that and I am not going to have one by this evening.** A number in a man's mouth dies with the man. A number in a book is a thing with a date on it. I have said mine out loud every fourth day for nine years and every one of them died before the next, and I would like that to go on being the arrangement, and I would like it to go on being the arrangement after you have been here, and you have not asked me for that and I am telling you anyway."

"A man does not count nine stones every fourth day for nine years by accident."

"No. And I am not going to tell you why, and I am not going to tell you what I would do if one of them went, and the reason I am not going to is that the second of those is a keeper's sentence and you have not got a form, and a keeper's sentence with no form behind it is a thing a man in a coat can come up this bank and use. I have seen a man be held to a thing he said on a bank and I would rather not be the second one."

---

They stood on that flat for the better part of an hour with four beasts against a low wall and the counting of them done and the counting of the stones done, and not one word of what the morning had been about was said out loud by either of them, and Drue put his hand on the near one's rump twice while he said nothing at all, and the beasts did not mind him and Tomas Vale wrote none of it down until the dark.

"There is a thing I am going to say and then I am going to walk four beasts down to a gate," Drue said, "and you are not going to get it out of me and you are not going to get it out of anybody else, and I am saying it because you have been careful with me for a whole morning and I have not had that in about two years."

"Go on."

"About two years back a man came up this road with a satchel and a form and he was pleasant about it, and the form had four boxes on it and one of them was for a standing, and he wanted mine." Drue did not raise his voice and did not stop. "**I said yes in the box. I did not say yes out loud on a bank; I said yes in a box, in ink, with a date on it, and that is a different act and I have had nine years to find out how.** The form is not on this reach and it is not in this county and it is not lost, it is in a room with a fire in it nine miles down the water, and the man put it in a book and the book went down the water and the book has gone about twice a year ever since for a reason I am not going to tell you. And I cannot unsay it. I have thought about it in about nine different ways and every one of them ends with a man in a coat standing on a bank with a piece of paper in his hand, and there is nothing in this kingdom I can put in front of him. **So I would like a keeper's standing very much the way I would like a fifth beast that nobody is asking for, and that is the whole of the answer and you did not get it out of me, I gave you it.**"

"You did."

"And I am not going to tell you which room, and I am not going to tell you what is on the second sheet, and I am not going to tell you who the man was, and if you go down that water and ask anybody whether a form came up here about two years ago with four boxes on it, I will find out and it will not be from anybody who tells me."

---

At about the seventh hour the light came up off the water and he could not see the far bank, and the four people on the reach were two hundred yards down at a gate and had not come up.

Tomas Vale had a fifth sentence. He had had one from a man with a besom on Day 195 that would not be made into one, and one from a man at a lock on Day 192 that he did not know whether to call a yes or a no, and a waller's about a mending on Day 188, and a woman's about a place you can find each other at on Day 193 that she had taken the use out of before she had finished it, and now he had a byre-man's about a number that dies with the man. Five. None of them sat next to another one, and the one he had just been given was the only one of the five that came from a man who had put his name in a box two years ago and could not take it out.

He did not have a sentence. He had a wall, and a roof on four posts, and a board with nine years of nothing in it, and five men who had each said one true thing about their own work and had not agreed with each other about anything, and one of them who could not take back the only one of the five that had ever been written down.

He noticed, walking back down to the barrow, that he had not told Emmer Drue he had come to ask. He had come up a bank to ask a man whether he would hold a line, and he had not said so, and the man had answered about the counting anyway, and would have gone on counting if the question had never been asked, and nobody had been told that there was a question.

He wrote that down at about the ninth hour of the evening, on flat ground above the lower end of the reach, with the barrow against a stone and the four bolts made fast, and he wrote it in the ordinary hand with nothing round it, and then he read it back and did not cross any of it out.




> *Day one of the ten. The hundred and ninety-seventh, at about the ninth hour of the evening, on beaten ground above the lower end of the reach. A byre with a door cut in later, eight beasts counted aloud in a doorway in a fixed order, a hazel stick against a low wall, nine knee-high stones counted aloud with a finger on each, four bolts, a barrow, a leaf in the inside of a coat.*
>
> *Second column, the man, and the two things he gave me before I had asked one of them. **He is the third name on the list a woman over at the lower end keeps, and he said so first so that he would not have had time to decide how he wanted to be standing when I asked.** She asked him two years back; it took nine minutes; she wrote it on her own table. He said yes in words and she wrote it, and nothing has been asked of him since. He gave me the second thing before we began, which was a form and a box. He does not know who the fourth name is, told me that before I asked it, and I did not ask him for it and am not going to.*
>
> *Second column, the counting, which is a trade and not a duty. **Nine stones, out loud, on his way to and from the low end, every fourth day, for nine years, and the reason is that his hand is on nine hundred beasts a week and there is no ninth stone anywhere else in nine miles.** A figure in a man’s mouth dies with the man. A figure in a book is a thing with a date on it. He counted them in front of me with a finger on each and would not say what he would do if one of them were gone, because that is a keeper’s sentence and there is nothing standing behind it. Nothing was struck and nothing went in the board, and he has not put a name in that board and never will.*
>
> *Second column, said once, and then he went down the bank with four beasts. **A man came up this road two years back with a satchel and a form carrying four boxes, one headed for a standing, and Drue said yes in the box, in ink, with a date on it.** The form is not on this reach and not in this county; it is in a room nine miles down the water in a book that has gone down and come back twice a year for nine years, and he will not say the room, the second sheet or the man. **That is a different act from saying yes on a bank, and he gave it to me unasked and it cost him the rest of the day. He cannot unsay it and there is no form in this kingdom on which a man takes a standing back out of a box.***
>
> *Third column, ruled apart. **Five of these now, in five trades, in five grammars, and not one will sit next to another one.** A mending. A box a man has none of. A place to find each other at, with the use taken out of it. Three sentences and no fourth. And a figure that dies with the man. A bar between two of them is a thing I invented for a pair I was sure were one sentence, and I am not sure of these, so there is no bar and no row. **The three partial answers to the line-question are still three. This is a fifth thing and not a fifth answer and it is joined to nothing.** And I did not ask whether he would hold a line: I stood nine foot off his wall and never put the word in, and he answered it anyway. **The schedule went into my hands on the hundred and thirty-fourth day, which is Chapter 0148, and it is sixty-three days in my pocket, and I still cannot read a run, and there is no form in this kingdom on which a man is taught, and so there is no version of being better at one. The name is a woman’s name and I have had it in my own hand since the same day, which is sixty-three days, and I have not used it for anything.***
>
> *The five clauses stand and I am not going through them. Four bolts made fast at the fourth hour and again at the seventh. **The frame has not been in a building since the thirty-third, a hundred and sixty-four days, and an open byre door stood nine foot from that barrow all morning with a roof over it and eight beasts in it, and I did not put a cradle in it, and the reason is not the second clause: a byre has nine foot of animal in it and a frame comes out of a byre differently than it went in.** The carrier in the sack, **a hundred and twenty days out of the seventy-seventh, not rung once, and I did not touch it.** My own bell is in a sealed yard in Kells and the bar is on the bell and not on the licence, and it does not travel. **There is a handbell in her bag and it is hers and it is not rung, and nobody has asked her for a reason at any hour of today, including me.***
>
> *The register is thirteen. **Nothing was asked for, produced, given or taken today, by me or by anybody.** A form with four boxes on it that a man filled in with ink two years back is in a room nine miles down the water and is not in this coat and is not a fourteenth. No name was entered on any paper by me. **Sixty-one days on this road with her and I did not ask her what it was for, and I did not notice that I had not.** Which office is it. **Nine stones that a man counts with his finger and will not write down, a box with ink in it that cannot be emptied, and nine miles of water on which nobody was asked anything by me today.***
