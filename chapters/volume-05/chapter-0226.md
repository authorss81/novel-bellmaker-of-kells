# Chapter 0226 — A Sentence Has One Subject and One Verb and One Place, and He Had Nine of Everything

It was the second hour of the two hundred and twelfth day and Merrick Dow was going round a schoolroom with a pointer, and there were nine children in it, and six of them were on the first row doing letters and three of them were on the second row doing words, and the words were the same nine words he has used since before the woman with the book came, and the letters were the same letters, and he does the whole of it in about an hour because a man who draws it out is a man enjoying it.

The room was over a chapel on the second mile and it had a stove in the corner that was lit from the dark to the dark in a season, and there were about nine forms on the wall that had been painted by nine different hands in about nine years and had been painted over each other, so that the wall was a palimpsandt of letters about four deep, and the child on the end row could read all four.

Dow did not look at the door when it opened, and he finished the word he was on, and then he said, without turning round, "If you are selling anything, the third hour is market and the market is at the bottom of the water."

"I am not selling anything."

"Then sit down and do not talk, because I have nine children and about a quarter of an hour of it and neither of those is mine to give away."

---

At about the fifth hour the six on the front row were out in the yard doing letters in the cold on the flat of the outside wall, three of them which was not a word, and Tomas Vale sat on the end of the second row bench with a book open on his knee, and Merrick Dow came in and sat down at the other end of the same bench, about nine foot off, and did not ask.

"I want to read you nine sentences."

"No."

"They are nine trades' sentences. They are all true. They were said to me by nine people in ten days, in nine trades, and I have put them in a row in a book and I have been trying for six days to make them into one sentence and I cannot, and I thought—"

"**You thought I would help you with the grammar.**" Dow did not raise his voice. "I am not going to. I am not refusing you either, and I would like that marked, because you have had four refusals on this water in a week and I can see from the way you are sitting that you have begun to think refusals are the price of talking to people."

"Then what is it."

"**It is that I have got nine children to teach and a sentence to teach them and you have come in here with something that is not a sentence.**" He put his hands on his knees. "A sentence has one subject and one verb and one place. It has a man in it that it is about. You have got nine of everything you have got nine of, and if you set the nine in a row that is not a sentence, it is a list, and a list is the one thing in this kingdom that has never once been read out loud to anybody that understood it."

---

At about the seventh hour the children came in off the yard and went out the other way and the door was shut and there were two men in a room over a chapel and about nine forms on the wall with letters about four deep.

The child on the end row had not gone. He was at the back with a scrap of a board of his own, and he was doing the fourth layer of a letter on the wall with the flat of his thumb and a bit of soot off the stove, and Dow let him, and did not look at him, and Tomas Vale looked at him for about nine seconds and then made himself stop, because a man with a barrow watching a child make a letter is a man deciding something about a child, and he had done that three times in ten days and each time it had cost somebody a morning they had not offered him.

The child made a K. It was a very bad K and it was the fourth one on that wall, which meant three people had been wrong about a K before him and had not scraped it out, and the boy had come along and made it worse and put it on top, and Tomas Vale understood about four things about nine miles of water from watching a child do that, and did not say one of them, and Dow, who had been not looking at the child for a quarter of an hour, said:

"He will be the third one and he will be the only one who can read all four, and there is no form in this kingdom on which a man is thanked for being the third one." 

"Now. I am going to say the rest of it once and then I am going to teach." He turned and looked at the book on the other man's knee without leaning. "**You are not the first man who has come into a room like this with nine true things and wanted one thing.** And the reason it cannot be one thing is not a mystery and it is not because any of the nine are wrong. It is that a sentence can only be about one place. The moment you say the nine together you have not made a sentence about a reach. You have made a sentence about a man with a book, and you are the man with the book, and everybody in the nine is now in a row behind you and they did not choose to be and they cannot get out."

"You have taught this."

"I have taught it every morning for about nine years and I have never once had to say it, and I would have gone on for another nine years without saying it, and you are the first person who has come up nine miles to be told a thing I could have told a post." He got up. "**And the other half, and this is the part you will want to write down and I want written down too. If you take those nine to nine men and you come back with nine the same, then you have not found a reach. You have found a man who repeated you, and the difference between those two is the difference between a road and a cart rut, and I have taught boys the difference for about nine years and it is the only thing I know.**"

---

At about the eleventh hour the room was dark and the stove had been let down and the two of them were still on the bench about nine foot apart, and Tomas Vale had the book open on his knee with the nine in it in nine rows, and he had read it out loud once and had been stopped, and he had not written anything since the seventh hour.

"I have written it down with a bar in it," he said. "Under the last line. And I am going to say out loud that I have done that and why, because you have told me twice today that the difference between a thing and a list is what a man says about it, and I am not going to be a man who does a thing and does not say what he did it for."

"Then say it."

"**A bar in this book means do not put these next to each other. That is all it has ever meant, and I have been reading the other half of it wrong for about a week, and a man in a hole on this water spent about twelve years correcting me before I knew there was anything to correct.** Nine trades have said nine things and I cannot make them one, and I cannot refuse them either, and a thing you cannot join and cannot refuse is not a set of terms, it is nine sentences, and I am not going to walk round nine doors and put them in a row and let a row do the work that nine mouths did."

"And are you going to take any of it to anybody."

"**No. Not one half of it to one person.**" He shut the book. "I am not taking your sentence to the woman at the ford, and I am not taking hers to a cooper, and I am not taking a gravedigger's to either of them, and I have already had four days this week of a man handing me a true thing and me going off to use it, and a woman on this water told me that is taking, and she was right, and I did not thank her and I am not going to now. **I have four clauses about this and I made them out loud in a room I paid for on about a hundred and fortieth day, and the third of them is that I will not take the same words to two people and come back with two the same, and I have not broken it in four days and I am not going to break it tonight to be tidier.**"

"That is four inches long," Dow said.

"What is."

"Your four clauses. It is four inches long and it is the only thing you own that I would put in front of nine men." He stood up and lit the lamp over the bench. "**I was in this room at the twelfth the night before with that chair and the whole reach knew about it and nobody came, and that is a fact about this water and not about me. Sit down and hold the far end. It wants the glue and not the nails.**"

---

At about the ninth hour of the evening there was a barrow on flat ground above the lower end with the four bolts made fast, and about a mile up the second mile a schoolroom over a chapel had a lamp in it and two men in it holding the ends of a chair, and one of the nine forms on the wall had a letter on it at about the fourth layer down.

Tomas Vale had been told, in plain words and without unkindness, that he could not make nine sentences into one, and had been told why, and had been told the reason it cannot be done is that a sentence can only be about one place and the nine places are not one place, and that if the nine came back the same from nine men then the nine men had agreed about nothing and the row had agreed about everything, and he had not argued with any of it.

He wrote the six things down, and he wrote the bar under the nine with a sentence of his own under the bar, and he said that sentence out loud to a man who was right and was not thanked for hearing it, and then he wrote down the third of his four clauses in full, in his own hand, for the first time in about ninety days, and noted that a man who writes his own clauses down is a man who intends to be tempted.

He did not cross out the bar. He had come in with a row and he went out with a bar, and the difference between those two things is the whole of six days, and it is not a resolution, and a bar across nine sentences is not a decision, and a decision is a thing with a price and he has not got one of those yet and knows it.



> *Day six of the ten. The two hundred and twelfth, at about the ninth hour of the evening, on flat ground above the lower end of the reach. A schoolroom over a chapel on the second mile, nine children, six on letters and three on words, a pointer, a stove let down at the dark, a lamp over the second-row bench, a chair wanting glue and not nails, about nine forms on a wall with letters about four deep, a barrow, four bolts, a leaf in a book.*
>
> *Second column, the attempt, stopped, and the stoppage was not unkind and was not a refusal either. **A sentence has one subject and one verb and one place, and it has a man in it that it is about, and I had nine of everything I had.** A row of nine is not a sentence, it is a list, and a list is the one thing in this kingdom that has never once been read out loud to somebody who understood it. He had never had to say it in nine years and I was the first person who came up nine miles to be told a thing he could have told a post. **He marked that he was not refusing me and would like it marked, because he could see I had begun to think refusals were the price of talking to people, and that is the fault of four days and not of the four people.**
>
> *Second column, the half he wanted written down. **A sentence can only be about one place. The moment the nine are set in a row they are not a sentence about a reach; they are a sentence about a man with a book, and I am the man with the book, and the nine are now in a row behind me and did not choose to be and cannot get out.** And then the part that is the finding of the ten days: **if I take the nine to nine men and come back with nine the same, then I have not found a reach, I have found a man who repeated me, and that is the difference between a road and a cart rut.** I have the third of four clauses made out loud in a room I paid for on about the hundred and fortieth day, and it is that I will not take the same words to two people and come back with two the same, and I have not broken it in four days and I did not break it tonight to be tidier.
>
> *Third column, the bar, said out loud and said why. **I have written the nine with a bar under the last line and I have said to his face that I have done it and why. A bar in this book means do not put these next to each other, and that is all it has ever meant, and I have been reading the other half of it wrong for about a week.** A thing you cannot join and cannot refuse is not a set of terms; it is nine sentences. **I am not walking round nine doors and putting them in a row and letting a row do the work that nine mouths did, and I am carrying neither half of tonight to anybody: not his sentence to the woman at the ford, not hers to a cooper, not a gravedigger's to either.** The three partial answers are still three. The schedule went into my hands on the hundred and thirty-fourth day and it is seventy-eight days in my pocket, and the eleven lines are out of the same day, and the name is a woman's name in the same pocket and is seventy-eight days, and I have not used it for anything and nobody has asked me for it today.*
>
> *The five clauses stand and I am not going through them. Four bolts made fast at the fifth hour on the hard ground by the chapel wall and again at the eleventh. **The frame has not been in a building since the thirty-third, a hundred and seventy-nine days, and there is a schoolroom roof thirty foot off that barrow and nine children under it and a man teaching, and I did not put a cradle in it, and the reason is not the second clause: nine children at about the fifth hour is nine people in a room I cannot get out of in a hurry, and a cradle is a building-sized thing for a bell and there is nothing in that room a cradle could go inside of.** The carrier in the sack, **a hundred and thirty-five days out of the seventy-seventh, not rung once, and I did not touch it.** My own bell is in a sealed yard in Kells and the bar is on the bell and not on the licence, and it does not travel. **There is a handbell in her bag and it is hers, it is not rung, and nobody has asked her for a reason at any hour of any of the six days of this week, including me.** Nothing was struck, nothing was tied, nothing went into metal, I did not reach for anything, and nothing came.*
>
> *The register is thirteen. **Nothing was asked for, produced, given or taken on any of the ten days of this week by me, and no name was entered on any paper by me, and a book of my own with nine rows in it is not a document, because there is no form in this kingdom on which a man can produce what nine people said to him on nine days.** Nine children, nine words, a chair, a pointer, a lamp: none of them is a paper. **Nobody was thanked today, and a man taught me the whole of the thing I got wrong in about a quarter of an hour and then held the far end of a chair.** Seventy-six days on this road with her and I did not ask her what it was for. Which office is it. **A list of nine true sentences, and a bar, and a man who says the bar is the sentence.**
