# Chapter 0216 — A Woman Who Keeps Two Lists Tells Him He Has Been Taking Things Down Without Asking

It was the first hour of the two hundred and second day and Wilna Crease was beating the second mat out on the outside stair of her shop, and she had been at it since before the light, and Tomas Vale came up the stair with no hands in his pockets and stood at the top of it, which is what a man does when he has decided the person on the stair has already seen him.

"Four days," she said, without stopping.

"Four days since I was on this stair."

"Four days since you were on this stair, and the shop has been open every one of them, and I have watched a barrow go up and down this reach four times from a counter, and there is a man on this water who has told me something about you and I have been sitting on it for four days deciding whether to say it and I have decided, and I want it understood that I decided, and I am not going to be talked round."

"Go on."

"I am going to finish the mat first, because if I stop halfway it takes a week to start again." She went on. "**A man came to that shop about nine days back and told me he had said one true sentence to a stranger with a barrow and had said it to nobody else in nine years, and he did not give me the stranger’s name and I did not ask him for it.** He did not come to warn me about anything. He came because I keep a book. He said *you are the only person on this water who writes things down and I would like somebody who writes things down to have this one.*"

---

At about the third hour she had the mat out and the shop open and the barrow was on the hard standing by the butt end, where it had been every night for eleven days, and she came out and stood at the bottom of her own outside stair with her arms folded and let him come down.

"Go on," she said. "You have a book and you have been writing in it. Say what the nine are."

"I am not going to say them to you."

"That is the first sensible thing anybody with a barrow has said on this stair in about four days." She unfolded her arms. "**Here is the part I have had for four days, and it is not clever and it is not a lesson. Four days ago I asked myself whether anybody on this water was carrying sentences off this reach, and the answer was no. So I asked nine people I chose, one question of about nine words. Three said no without stopping. Four stopped. And the four that stopped were not frightened and not offended and not even surprised, and that is the part.** A man at the byre who has been on my list for two years, a woman with a kiln, a man with a drove, a man with a chain. The same answer in four different sets of words, and not one of them asked me why."

"Which means what."

"It means you went and got something out of four people and told none of them that you were getting it, and every one of the four noticed, and not one of them mentioned it to anybody, and **the only one who said anything said it to me, and it took him a whole day to decide to, and he decided because I keep a book.**"

She turned and went up two steps and came back down, which is what a woman does when she is not finished.

"So here is what you have done, in your own words, so that you cannot get it out of me and put a better version in your book. You have four sentences from four trades. You have not asked any of the four whether you might have them, and you have not told any of the four that you had them. And in about nine days one of those four is going to be in a room with somebody from an office, and what will be said about that man is that a stranger with a barrow came and took a true sentence off him and went away. **That will be true. And I could not correct it and neither could he, because there is nothing either of us could produce.**"

---

At about the fourth hour she let him up the stair into the same room, the counter with nothing on it, the table, the book open in two places, the cold stove, the chair pushed back.

He did not take the book out of the inside of his coat and she looked at the inside of his coat and then at his face.

"Sit down. I have four things and they are all no, and I am going to say all four in about nine minutes and then I am going to shut this book, and I want you to know that the first three are not about the four trades and the fourth is about them and you are not going to enjoy it."

"Say them."

"**One. I am not going to be the woman who says this reach does not want a keeper.**" She said it flat and did not raise her voice. "A man in a room a mile and a half from here would be glad to have a woman who keeps a book over a shop say that, because it has a mouth and a trade and no office, and it would be the only sentence anybody has about this water in nine years. And I am not going to say it. Not because it is untrue. **Because a position wants a holder, and there is no box in this kingdom with a woman who keeps a book over a shop on it, and the four trades have not said a word about the capital and neither have I, and I am not going to speak for them.**"

"Two."

"**Two. I am not going to hold a copy of your nine, and I am not going to leave you the second page of mine, and I am not going to put a line about you in my book.**" She put her hand flat on it. "I gave you that rule about the refusals a fortnight ago and I am giving it about this now, and the rule is the only thing I have got. A list that lives in one room on one table is read by the people who come into that room. Everything else is a thing that gets produced."

"Three."

"**Three. And I am not going to help you write it so that any of them sounds good.** Four men, four sentences and about eight hundred yards of bank between them is the honest way round, and it reads like nothing at all, and I am not going to tidy it for you. If you put nine of them in a row in that book I will not be one of the people who told you they went together."

"And the fourth."

She shut the book.

"The fourth is that about four in five of anybody I could ask would say no, and I have written nine refusals down about my own ground in two years and have not given you the other thirty-odd. **Three of the four trades you have been to this week are not on my list and nobody has asked them, and the byre man is on it, and he is on it because I asked him two years ago and not because you did.** A person who has not been asked has neither said no nor said yes, and what you have done is go to three people who have never been asked anything and a fourth who has, take a sentence off each, and carry it up and down this water in a coat. Go on ten more days and you will have about nine, and you will not have anybody. **There is no form in this kingdom on which a man collects people, and I have just watched you try to do it with a notebook.**"

---

At about the fifth hour she had the book shut and her hand on it, and outside the window four people went up the reach with a barrow-load of lime and a yoke, and they did not stop.

"The last of it, and then I am opening the shop." She looked at him. "**What are you going to do about the four days?** Not the nine sentences. The four days. A man who does that four days running is not taking sentences, he is taking a measurement, and there will be a fifth day and a sixth, and on about the ninth day one of those four will be in a room with somebody from an office, and that is not a thing you put right by saying sorry."

"I have not thought of that."

"No. And I would like it noted that I am aware of what I have just done, which is tell a man with a barrow about a thing in his own conduct that he had not worked out, and get nothing for it." She stood up. "I am not doing it for you. I am doing it because a man trusted me with one true sentence about nine days ago and I would rather be the one who tells you than the one who watches."

---

At about the seventh hour there was a man at the end of the reach with a satchel and a flat book under his arm, and a bag of bread and a stone bottle, and he had come down off the top of the water on foot and was standing in the road looking at the barrow against the butt end as though he had been looking at it for some time.

Wilna Crease came out of the shop and looked at him for about as long as it takes to count a room.

"How do you know."

"He has not asked me a question in four minutes and he is looking at the ground, and there are two of those kinds of person on this water and one of them is me." She shut the door to about a hand’s width. "**He has come up the water. People do not come up the water. He is going to want the ground for a book, and there is going to be a box in that thing with a word on it, and if the word is a name then he has come to the wrong nine miles and I am not going to tell him so.**"

"You could tell him the ground."

"I could, and it would be the first time in four years that anybody has put anything on this reach in a book that could be found again. And I am not going to, because the moment I start telling a district what a mile of my ground is, in about nine years I am a woman who tells districts what her ground is." She looked through the gap. "**Go and talk to him. Do not take him up to the wall or the byre or a kiln, and do not tell him about the nine. And when he asks you what this reach is, you say a place people live, and that is all it says, and it will not be enough, and it is what there is.**"

---

At about the ninth hour of the evening there was a barrow on beaten ground above the lower end with four bolts made fast, a light in a shop window, and a man with a satchel asleep in a shed at the end of the reach who had walked about nine miles that day and was not going to be asked anything by anybody tonight.

Tomas Vale had been told he had been doing a thing for four days, by a woman who keeps two lists, in a room over a shop, and he had not argued with a word of it, and the nine sentences were still in his book and were now also a thing done to four people, and the four were the four she had asked and he had not.

He wrote that down in the ordinary hand with nothing round it, and then he wrote the thing he could not put anywhere: that a woman who owed him nothing had spent four days deciding to tell him a fault and got nothing for it, and that she had done it because a drover came into her shop and gave her one true sentence for no reason except that she writes things down, and that the whole arrangement rested on a woman who writes things down and a man who has a book, and neither of those is a standing.

He wrote down the third one separately, because it was the only one of the four that had cost her something to say, and because he could not think of anywhere else to put it. He wrote down that she had said them in the order she had told him she would say them in, and that he had numbered two and three out loud like a man reading a list of defects in a thing he had asked to have built, and that she had shut the book before the fourth and given him the fourth anyway, and that it was the only one of the four he had not wanted to hear.

> *Day six of the ten. The two hundred and second, at about the ninth hour of the evening, on flat ground above the lower end of the reach. Two mats on an outside stair, a shop, a book shut on a table, a hard standing with a barrow on it, a man with a satchel and a flat book asleep in a shed at the end of the reach, four bolts.*
>
> *Second column, what she had been sitting on for four days. **A drover came to her shop about nine days back and told her he had said one true sentence to a stranger with a barrow and had said it to nobody else in nine years, and he came because she is the only person on this water who writes things down.** **A man told a woman who keeps a book so that the sentence would be somewhere, and that is the whole of the arrangement this reach has: two people who keep books, and neither of them has a standing.** She had been deciding four days whether to say it, and she said she decided.*
>
> *Second column, what she found out and how. She asked nine people she chose, over four days, one question of about nine words, and did not say what it was about. Three said no without stopping and four stopped. **The four who stopped were the byre man, the kiln woman, the drover and the chain man, and none of them was frightened or offended or even surprised, and that is the finding, and it is not a good one.** Every one of them had noticed and not one mentioned it to anybody. **The only one who said anything said it to her, and he decided because she keeps a book.** In about nine days one of those four will be in a room with somebody from an office, and what will be said about him is that a stranger with a barrow came and took a true sentence off him and went away, and it will be true, and neither of them can produce anything that says otherwise.*
>
> *Second column, her four nos, in the order she gave them. **One: she is not going to be the woman who says this reach does not want a keeper, and the reason is not that it is untrue, it is that a position wants a holder and there is no box in this kingdom with a woman who keeps a book over a shop on it, and the four trades have said nothing about the capital and she is not going to speak for them. Two: no copy of the nine, no second page of hers, no line about me in her book. Three: she is not going to let me write it so that any of them sounds good; four men, four sentences and eight hundred yards of bank between them is the honest way round, and if I put nine in a row she is not one of the people who told me they went together. Four: three of the four trades are not on her list and nobody asked them, and the byre man is on it because she asked him two years ago and not because I did, and if I go on ten more days I will have about nine and I will not have anybody.** The third one is the only one of the four that has cost her something.*
>
> *Third column, the district man at the end of the reach. **He has come up the water and people do not come up the water; he is going to want the ground for a book, and there is going to be a box in that thing with a word on it, and if the word is a name then he has come to the wrong nine miles and she is not going to tell him so.** She is not going to tell him the ground either, because the moment she starts telling a district what a mile of her ground is, in about nine years she is a woman who tells districts what her ground is. She told me to go and talk to him and not to take him up to the wall or the byre or a kiln and not to tell him about the nine, and that when he asks what this reach is I am to say a place people live, and that it will not be enough, and it is what there is. **The three partial answers are still three and these are nine things and not nine answers. The schedule went into my hands on the hundred and thirty-fourth day and it is sixty-eight days in my pocket, and the name is out of the same day and is sixty-eight days, and it is a woman’s name, and I have not used it for anything.***
>
> *The five clauses stand and I am not going through them. Four bolts made fast at the first hour on the hard standing by the butt end of a shop and again at the fifth. **The frame has not been in a building since the thirty-third, a hundred and sixty-nine days, and there is a room over a shop with a stove in it and about nine people in it by the fourth hour, and I did not put a cradle on that stair, and the reason is not the second clause: I was being told what I have been doing and I did not want to be a thing in a room while it was being said.** The carrier in the sack, **a hundred and twenty-five days out of the seventy-seventh, not rung once, and I did not touch it.** My own bell is in a sealed yard in Kells and the bar is on the bell and not on the licence, and it does not travel. **There is a handbell in her bag and it is not rung, and nobody has asked her for a reason at any hour of today, including me.** Nothing was struck, nothing was tied, nothing went into metal, I did not reach for anything, and nothing came.*
>
> *The register is thirteen. **Nothing was asked for, produced, given or taken today by me, and no name was entered on any paper by me.** The five things I cannot fix: nine houses of a front row wet and two hundred and twenty-nine dry, forty people in a yard never told anything, a barge of nine tons of dressed stone upright in seven foot of water, a dressed sill half in and about nine days, and a rate to be set in a fortnight by nine men who will not agree. **Sixty-six days on this road with her and I did not ask her what it was for.** Which office is it. **Nine sentences taken off four trades in four days by a man with a barrow who told none of them, and a district’s own clerk asleep in a shed at the end of a reach where nobody comes.***
