# Chapter 0203 — Two Lists of the Same Length, in a Room Over a Shop

It was the first hour of the hundred and eighty-ninth day and Wilna Crease was beating a mat out on the outside stair of her shop with a birch rod, one arm's length of it at a time, the way a person does a thing that has to be done before the street is awake, and when she had done the mat she went up her own outside stair and unlocked a room with a table in it, a shop bell on the stair, and nothing else in the world that a keeper would want.

The room was over the shop. It had a counter along one side with nothing on it, a table with a book open on it, a cold stove, and a chair pushed back as if somebody had got up out of it in a hurry and would be back.

Tomas Vale came up the outside stair at about the third hour with the barrow left at the bottom of it — the second clause of a receipt in five is the reason he cannot set it down, so he is on foot and he is not a man on foot — and he came up with his hands empty, which you can see from the bottom of a stair and which she saw.

"You have left a barrow at the bottom of my stair."

"I have."

"Nobody leaves a barrow at the bottom of my stair. They put it on the hard standing by the butt end, because the butt end is nine foot of brick and a barrow leaned on a brick wall is a barrow that is telling the truth about itself."

"It is on the butt end. I did not know that this morning and I know it now."

"You did that well and you did not practise, which is the part I am interested in." She put the rod in the corner. "You have come up that stair to ask me something and you have decided what the first three words are going to be, and I can see the decision happening in your mouth, and I would rather you said the question and let it sit there than said it the way you have planned to."

He said the question. It was about who on this reach would hold a line, and how many of them there were.

"Sit down," she said, and did not sit herself, and stood at the table with her hand flat on the open book. "I am going to tell you the number and you will have to do something with it and I want to watch you try. **There are four names on this page and there are four names on this page, and they are the same four and they are not the same four people.**"

---

At about the fourth hour he had it, and it was worse than the arithmetic he had been carrying.

The left-hand page had a heading in a good round hand she had written herself, and the heading was the names of people who had said they would hold a line, and there were four. The right-hand page had a heading, and the heading was the names of people who had said they would not, and there were four.

"And about four in five of the people you could ask would say no," she said. "Four in five. I have asked a good many of them over about two years and I have written down nine, and I am not going to tell you the other thirty-odd because they have not said anything to me and a person who has not been asked has not said no, and you may write that down as the whole of what I have."

"You kept the second page."

"Of course I kept the second page."

"Who asked you to keep the second page."

"Nobody. That is what I am telling you." She turned round and put both hands on the table. "There is no form in this kingdom on which a person records a refusal, and there is no form on which anybody could ask me for one, and I have written nine refusals in my own book in my own hand about my own ground, and four of them are names of people who will put a hand on a gate this winter if somebody stands next to them, and the other four are names of people who will not, and I have kept them both, and the reason is that **a book with only the yeses in it is a book that tells you what a place can do, and a book with both in it tells you what a place is, and I would rather know what a place is.**"

"They are not the same four."

"They are the same four and they are not the same four people." She put a finger on each page in turn. "The four on the left are not braver than the four on the right, they are not a party, and they have not agreed about anything except turning up when it is turning-up weather. Two of them would tell you to your face that the other two are wrong about the rate, and one of them is a man who cannot read. **A person who says yes and a person who says no are the same kind of animal at different hours of the same year, and I can show you which hour of which month for every one of the four, and that is not a form either.**"

"And you."

"I am not a keeper and I do not want to be one and I could not be one if I tried." She said it without any weight, the way a woman says what her own hands are for. "I am a woman who keeps a book over a shop. If somebody wanted to make me a keeper of a line they would want a box to put me in, and there is no box anywhere in this kingdom with *woman who keeps a book over a shop* on it, and if there were one it would be on a form, and the form would be in a room, and the room would be a mile and a half from here."

---

From the fourth hour on, people came up the outside stair one at a time, because a room over a shop is where people on a reach go when they have nothing to do. There was a man who took his shoes off at the bottom and put them at the top. There were two women who had come from up the reach and who sat on the floor. There was a boy who came for the counter and was sent away again by his own mother. There was a man with a spade he put outside the door and did not pick up again. There were four from the lower end of the water, and two of those were Isa Trevail and Bel Tremayne, and Bel Tremayne came in with a book under her arm the way she goes everywhere.

By about the sixth hour nobody in that room knew why Tomas Vale was in it, and about four of them had guessed, and none of them asked.

The two women who kept books sat nine feet apart with both books shut, all evening, and neither said one word to the other about a book. Bel Tremayne said out loud at one point that there is no form in this kingdom on which two people who both keep a book may put one in front of the other, which is why nobody ever does, and neither of the two of them looked up.

---

At about the seventh hour he had been in that room four hours and had not asked for anything, and Wilna Crease shut the book and squared it and said, "You have been sitting there for four hours being careful and I have decided to stop being careful back, so here. **The second page. Take it. It is nine names of people on this water who will not do a thing, and I have not had a reason to keep them before this afternoon and I would like somebody to have them.**"

He looked at the book and did not put his hand out.

"No," he said.

"You have not looked at it."

"I have looked at it from where I am sitting and I could read it from here and that is the reason I am not going to touch it. **If that page comes out of your book and into my coat it stops being nine people refusing a thing and becomes nine people recorded as refusing a thing by a man with a barrow, and I could not tell you afterwards who wrote it down or why, and neither could anybody else, including you.** That is not a reason about you. It is about what a piece of paper is when one man gives it to another man in a room, and I have fourteen things in my life that I can name and the number is not going to be fifteen because a woman on a reach was kind to me this afternoon."

"You have counted your own things."

"I counted them once and I have been wrong about it three times since."

"Then I will keep it," she said, "and it will be on this table tomorrow, and you have not had it, and you did not refuse it for a reason that makes you better than anybody else. You refused it because you know what a coat is for. **Nobody in this room is a coward and nobody in this room is a hero, and eight of us have said a thing out loud about this water, and not one of us will be thanked for it, because there is no form in which gratitude is entered.**"

"Neither will I."

"I know. Sit down again, you have not heard the whole of it."

---

At about the seventh hour the room emptied, the two of them last, and by about the ninth hour of the evening there was a fire going in the shop below and the reach going past outside in the dark.

"There is one of the four you already know," she said.

He did not say anything.

"**I am not going to say it again and I am not going to say where I had it from, and if you ask me where I had it from I will put the book under the counter and we will both sit here.**"

He sat there. She said a name. It was a woman's name, and he has had it in his own hand since the hundred and thirty-fourth day, and he copied it out three times, and it is on a leaf in the inside of his coat, and it is also in a column in a district's own book nine miles behind him, in a clerk's own fair hand, in a cell with no box and no name in it.

He did not say it back. There were two people in that room and one of them was a woman who keeps two lists, and he was not going to say it out loud in a room, and he wrote it nowhere at all, which is the first time in fifty-three days that he has written nothing at all about anything.

"That one is not a man on this water," he said.

"No. And I have never met her, and that is all I am going to say about her, and she is not the reason I keep the second page."

A room over a shop, a counter with nothing on it, a table, a book open in two places, a birch rod in the corner, a cold stove, a chair pushed back, nine people in it by the end of the day, two books shut nine feet apart, four bolts.*
>
> *Second column. **Four on one page and four on the other, and the two pages are not the same four people.** About four in five of anybody I could ask would say no. I asked a good many over about two years and wrote nine down, and I am not giving you the other thirty-odd, because a person who has not been asked has not said no. **A book with only the yeses in it says what a place can do. A book with both in it says what a place is.** Nobody asked me to keep the second page. There is no form in this kingdom for recording a refusal, and none on which anybody could ask me for one, and I have written nine of them in my own hand about my own ground. **A yes and a no are the same kind of animal at different hours of the same year, and the four who said yes are not braver, are not a party, and have agreed about nothing except turning up.**
>
> *Second column, her. **She is not a keeper, does not want to be one, and could not be one: a keeper of a line is a thing somebody can be put in, and there is no box in this kingdom with a woman who keeps a book over a shop on it.** Two women in that room kept books and shut and stayed shut, and neither of them looked up when the other said there was no form for comparing notes.>
> *Third column, ruled apart. **She offered me the second page and I did not take it, and I want it written that I did not refuse it out of manners.** The moment that page leaves that table and goes into a coat it stops being nine people saying a word and becomes a column with a man in front of it, and after that neither of us could say who wrote it or why. **The register is thirteen and it is going to be thirteen at the end of this day, and I have known what fourteen is for fifty-three days and there is no form in this kingdom on which a man counts his own papers.** Neither of us is a coward and neither of us is a hero, and all eight people on those two pages have said a thing out loud about this water, and nobody is thanked, because there is nowhere to put it.
>
> *Third column, the name. **I am not writing it, I did not say it back, and she is not going to say it a second time or say where she had it from.** It is a woman's name and I have had it in my own hand since the hundred and thirty-fourth day, and it is in a district's own book nine miles behind me in a cell that has no box for a name in it, and I have carried it about fifty-five days and have not used it for anything, and I did not say it out loud tonight with one other person in the room. **A sentence in a book is a sentence somebody can read in forty years and act on, and this is the first time in fifty-three days that I have written nothing at all, and I am counting that as the better outcome and not as the worse one.**
>
> *The five clauses stand and I am not going through them. The four bolts went in at the fourth hour and I went back and looked at them at the seventh and they were where I had put them, and **the frame has not been in a building since the thirty-third, a hundred and fifty-six days, and there is a room over a shop with a stove in it eleven foot above my barrow and I did not put a cradle on that stair, and the reason is not the second clause: a barrow does not go up a stair, and I have written down that I did not want it to, because a man who only counts the honest reasons will start calling the other ones dishonest.** She said *nobody is thanked* out loud at about the seventh hour and meant it, and there are eight names on a table in this building that nobody is thanked for. The carrier in the sack, **a hundred and twelve days out of the seventy-seventh, and I did not touch it, and there is a handbell in her bag that is hers and nobody in that room heard it and I did not ask.** My own bell is in a sealed yard in Kells and the bar is on the bell and not on the licence, and it does not travel. Nothing was tied, nothing went into metal, I did not reach for anything, and nothing came.
>
> *The register is thirteen, and nothing was asked for, produced, given or taken today, and no name was entered on any paper by me or by anybody else. **Fifty-three days on this road with her and I did not ask her what it was for, and she was not in that room and did not come up the stair, and I have not asked her why not.**
>
> *Which office is it. **Two pages, four names each, nine names of refusals in a woman's own hand, and no form anywhere in this kingdom for a person who has said no.**
