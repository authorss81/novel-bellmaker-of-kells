# Chapter 0202 — A Node Is Not a Building With a Door, and a Wall Going Up Beside It

It was the fourth hour of the hundred and eighty-eighth day and Bos Ottery was building a wall in a stone yard four hundred yards below the top of the reach, and the wall was nine courses of a field wall with a footing course of two under it, and he was putting the ninth course on with a piece of limestone about as long as his forearm, and a boy called Tam Pym was sitting on the finished part of it with his legs hanging over the other side and a piece of string in his hands.

The yard had a lime cart in it and a barrow-load of chain against the wall behind, and a second wall that was finished and had a building against it, and above the yard, up the bank on the reach side, there was a thing that was not a building: a square of flat ground with a low wall round three sides of it, nine stones standing in a line across the middle of it about the height of a man's knee, a thing on a frame the size of a cartwheel with no bell in it, and a roof on four posts over a four-foot square of the near corner.

"Ottery," Tomas Vale said, from about nine feet off, with the barrow behind him. "I am not here about the wall."

"You are stood in my lime. That is about the wall. Move left about four foot and then you are not."

He moved left about four foot.

---

At about the fifth hour the yard was a working yard and there were nine people in it or near it, and a yard is not a room and there is no door in a yard, and a man who wants to be asked a thing in a yard is a man who has to ask it in front of nine people.

"I am going to ask you once and I am going to tell you now that I will not ask you a second time," Tomas said, "so if you have decided in advance that you are not going to say it, you can go on with what you are doing and lose nothing."

"That is either very decent or very clever."

"It is a promise and it costs me something and I would rather have made it before than after."

Ottery set his stone down and wiped his hands on his thighs, which is a thing a man does when he is going to talk for longer than he expected to.

"Right. Three things. Take them or leave them and do not ask me to make them into one, because I have tried that and I am a waller and not a man who makes sentences out of other people's trades."

"Go on."

"**One. It is stone set in the ground, and I laid it, and I know how it is laid and I will not tell you how because it is my trade and you would use it wrong.**" He put his thumb at the foot of the nearest standing stone without touching it. "That is the truth of it as far as my hands go, and a man who wants it to be something else is asking a waller to be a clerk."

"And the second."

"**Two. It is a place a man comes back to.**" He said it more slowly. "That is not mine, that is out of a man called Fennick who keeps a tally at the upper landing and I have been drunk with him twice, and I have never had a reason to argue with him, because a place a man comes back to and finds the same is the whole of a tally, and a tally is not a thing and is not a building and does not want a roof. **You could have nine of them along this reach and no two of them a yard apart and every one of them true.**"

"And the third."

Ottery was quiet for about as long as it takes to rub a face with the back of a hand.

"**Three. It is a place a person's name goes.**" He said it and then said the rest of it, and the rest of it was not slower. "There is a board on the post of that roof and there has been one since before the roof was on it, and it has a keeper's column ruled on it, and that column has had nothing in it for about nine years, because I have been building in this yard nine years and I have read it every time. I am not going to say whose name was last in it. That is a person's business and it is not in a column of mine."

Three things, and not one of them would sit next to another one. The first was about hands. The second was about a tally, and a tally is the same nine times, and nine places a mile apart that are each one place are not a network. The third was about a board on a post, and a board is a thing you can be put in, and nothing in the three of them made the other two necessary.

"What is the third one for, then. If nobody has put a name in it for nine years, what is it for."

"That is a question for somebody who has been asked something, and I am a man who lays stone. If I told you what that board is for I would be telling you what a man is for, and I have not got that authority, and there is no form in this kingdom on which anybody could hand it to me this morning."

---

At about the sixth hour Tam Pym came down off the wall with the string round his hand and stood next to the man with the barrow, and the man with the barrow did not ask him anything, and the boy talked anyway, which is what boys do and is the reason grown men have learned to wait.
"That board's not for names."

"You have not been up there in nine years."

"My mother's been up there. She said the column's for when somebody stands in it, and the standing is the thing that goes in it, and a name is not a standing, and a name is what you write on afterwards so a person can be cross about it later if the standing was no good."

Ottery said nothing to that at all, and after about a minute he said, "That is better than what I said and it is not mine."

"It is not yours," Tomas said, "and it is not a keeper's column either, and I am not going to write it down as one."

---

At about the seventh hour there were nine people in that yard and the man who would have known was in it, and Tomas knew which man that was because the boy had said the roof was there longer than the wall, and because there was a man at the far end of the yard who had not been building anything, had not been asked to build anything, and had been on the near corner of the flat ground above the wall for the whole hour with a broom in his hand that he had not used.

He was not asked.

There is no form in this kingdom on which a man in that yard could have asked him, and nothing was written down at any hour by anybody recording that he had been asked, and if a stranger came along in forty years with a piece of paper and said *a man asked him in a stone yard on the hundred and eighty-eighth*, there is nothing anywhere that would say whether it was true or not.

And the part that was hardest, and the part Tomas put in the book afterwards, was that the man would have answered. He had a broom and no work and he had been on that corner for an hour, and Tomas Vale has spent fifty-two days learning that the way to get a true sentence is to ask a person in the ordinary voice, and he stood nine feet off him and did not, and wrote down that he did not.

He also wrote down a gap. In the line of stones there were nine and there should have been ten, and between the fourth and the fifth there was a space about two hands across, and the footing under it was continuous, and the stones either side were bedded at the same depth with the same bed as the rest, and the ground inside the space was packed and walked on and no stone had ever stood there.

Ottery laid the last of the ninth course and came and looked at where Tomas was looking.

"That," Tomas said.

"That," Ottery said, "is where I did not put one, and I have not been asked and I am not going to be, and if you are going to write down that I did not answer you, write that down, and I will not sign it, because there is nowhere to sign it."

"Neither of us is going to get an answer about that today," Tomas said, "and I am not going to pretend to you that the reason is manners."

"No. The reason is that the man who knows is standing at the end of my yard with a broom and there are nine of us here, and you have not got a form, and if you had got a form it would have a box on it, and he would have had to write something in the box, and then it would not be a gap in a wall any more, it would be a thing a district has an opinion about." He put his hand on the coping he had just laid. "You would have made it worse by asking in front of these nine, and I am not saying you would have meant to."

---

The wall had gone up nine courses in six hours. At about the ninth hour of the evening there was a light on the water and the reach was the colour of a wet road, and Tomas was on a bank above the yard with the barrow on flat ground and the four bolts made fast, and about nine people were asleep in four buildings around a yard.

"Nobody at this yard is going to be the keeper of that," Ottery said, coming up past him with a barrow of his own, on his way to nothing in particular.

"No."

"**My reason, and it is not a principle, and I would like it wrote down as a thing and not as a belief. A thing nobody owns gets mended. A thing a man owns does not.** Nine years that roof has been up and four different men have been under it and not one of them has mended the ridge of it, and it will want mending in a wet winter and it will get mended, because the second the district puts a name on that board, the second it is a thing somebody has to get permission to touch, and the asking goes in a book, and a thing in a book stops."

"That is a good reason."

"It is a fact and you may have it." Ottery went on up the bank a few steps and turned round. "Do not come back and tell anybody I said it as though I had thought about it. I have not thought about it. I have watched it happen nine times and I have laid about four hundred foot of wall."

Tomas wrote it down in the second column of his own book on the bank, in the ordinary hand, with the waller's words and not a word of his own, and read it twice, and could not decide whether it was a rule or an excuse, and wrote that down too, and did not leave out the second of those two.

> *The hundred and eighty-eighth, at about the ninth hour of the evening. A stone yard, a lime cart, a wall nine courses high, a barrow-load of chain, a boy with a piece of string, a flat ground with a low wall on three sides, nine knee-high stones, a roof on four posts, a board on a post with a ruled keeper's column and nothing in it for nine years, a broom, a gap two hands across in a footing that goes on under it, a bank, four bolts.*
>
> *Second column, the three, kept apart and not joined. **A waller will say that it is stone set in the ground and that he laid it, and he will not say how it is laid because it is his trade and I would use it wrong.** A tally-keeper two counties of drinking away says it is a place a man comes back to and finds the same, and on that reading nine of these a mile apart are nine places and not a network. The same waller says a third thing about a name going into a column on a post, and will not say whose name was last there. **Three trades' grammars and not one of them will sit next to another one.**
>
> *Second column, a boy, and what his mother said. **The column is for the standing and the standing is the thing that goes in it, and a name comes after, so that somebody can be cross later if the standing was no good.** I am writing the boy's report of a woman and not the woman's sentence, and I am not calling it a keeper's column, because a boy on a wall has not told me what the column is.
>
> *Third column, ruled apart. **I did not ask the man with the broom and I have written that I did not, which is a new kind of entry and I do not like the look of it.** He would have answered. I was nine feet off him for an hour with a barrow. There is no form in this kingdom on which anybody in that yard could have asked him and no paper anywhere that would say afterwards whether it was done, and the waller gave me the reason unasked: with a form there would have been a box, and the box would have had to have something in it, and then it would have stopped being a gap in a wall. **Ten stones where there should be nine, or nine where there should be ten, with the footing continuous under the space and both neighbours bedded at the same depth.** The bay in my schedule is a bay nothing was run into; this is a space where nothing was laid. I am not joining the two and neither of us was asked about either.
>
> *The five clauses stand and I am not going through them. **No bell was struck by anybody in that yard today and nothing was tied and nothing was counted into metal, and I did not reach for anything and nothing came.** Four bolts made fast at the fourth hour and again at the seventh, and **the frame has not been in a building since the thirty-third, a hundred and fifty-five days, and there is a walled yard with a roof on it two hundred yards off that I did not put a cradle in, and the reason is not the second clause: the reason is that a yard is where a man lays his stone and a barrow with a cradle in it does not come out of one the same.** My own bell is in a sealed yard in Kells and the bar is on the bell and not on the licence, and it does not travel. That carrier in the sack, **a hundred and eleven days out of the seventy-seventh, and I did not put my hand on anything today, and there is a handbell in her bag that is hers to spend and that I have not asked her about.**
>
> *The register is thirteen. **I have produced nothing and asked for nothing and been given nothing and taken nothing, and nobody was thanked today.** I promised a man I would not ask him twice before I had asked him once, and that promise is the only thing I have asked anybody for in fifty-two days, and it cost me a question I was holding since the fifth hour. **A thing nobody owns gets mended and a thing a man owns does not, and the asking goes in a book, and a thing in a book stops.** I have set that down in the waller's own words and I cannot tell whether it is a rule or an excuse, and I have written down that I cannot tell. **Fifty-two days on this road with her. I did not ask her what it was for, and I did not notice that I had not.**
>
> *Which office is it. **Nine courses of field wall, a board with a ruled column and nine years of nothing in it, nine people in a yard, and not one form between any two of them.**
