# Chapter 0224 — A Baker Who Says Yes About His Own Morning, and the Yes Is Not the Reach

It was the first hour of the two hundred and tenth day and Hal Vessey was drawing the second batch out of a brick oven that had been shut since the dark, and he has drawn the second batch out at the first hour for about nine years, and the reason he has never once been asked about the hour is that everybody on nine miles of water is asleep at the first hour, so the asking is not available.

The bakehouse was a low room with a bench down one side and a trough in the floor and about nine loaves on the peel in a row, and the whole of it smelled of a thing that had been doing the same work in the same room since before he took it.

Tomas Vale came in the door off the lane with the barrow behind him and stopped about nine foot off the bench, because a man at an oven at the first hour is a man in the middle of a thing, and a barrow does not stop a man, and it never has.

"You're stood on my flour," Vessey said, without turning round. "Move a foot and I'll not say anything about it and you can ask me the thing you came to ask, and I'd rather do it that way than have you stand there being careful."

---

At about the fourth hour the first batch was out and the oven was shut and they were sitting on the bench with a cloth between them, and Tomas Vale had the second clause of a receipt in five in his mouth and could not get it out and put it down on the bench instead.

"I cannot pay for anything with the barrow. That is a clause, and it is the second of five, and all five stand. So I am telling you that what I have got to offer is that I will not stop being at the end of your lane for a week and that I am about as useful as the fourth wall of a bakehouse."

"**Say what you want.**"

"I want somebody to take me up the water with a cart. Not up to the top. Up four miles and as far past that as the day will do. I cannot pay him and I cannot pay you and I have no paper that would make anybody owe me a day, and I am asking anyway, and I have asked four people this week for things and got four answers that were no."

Vessey wiped his hands on the cloth and looked at him for about as long as it takes to put a batch in.

"You are asking me for a cart and a day and you have come to the man on nine miles of water with the only thing in it that is standing still on a working day, and that is not cleverness on your part, that is just the road." He got up and went to the trough. "**Say what the yes is, because I have one and I am not going to let you have it without knowing which one.**"

---

At about the sixth hour he had said it and Tomas Vale had it written on the back of his own hand with a pencil a cooper would have lent him and had not.

"**The yes is that a cart stands in a field at about the fourth mile with nothing in it and the driver sitting on a gate, and a cart that stands is a cart that is losing, and that is the whole of the reason and there is no reach in it.** He is a carter. He has a horse and he has a cart and about half of what he is owed, and he will take a day of anybody's paying for it in coals, and this week's coals are at the low end at the top of the water and that is a different water, and he told me about nine days ago that he would go up and look at them, and he is going up on a day of the week that is not this one and has told nobody but me, and I am telling you because a man who tells me things and then tells you things is a man running a list, and I have a list already and it is about nine and I have not asked to see it."

"You said you have a list."

"I said the wrong word. I have a row of about nine men who would put a cart up for a day. **It is not a list because a list is written and mine is not written, and if it is not written it dies with the winter, and that is what I want it to do.** You can have the one. You cannot have the nine. I have said the one out loud and I would like the one taken and I would like nobody to be told there were nine."

---

There had been about nine people in the lane in the time the two of them had been in the bakehouse, and every one of them had slowed at the corner by the shop, and none of them had come in, and Vessey had looked at the fire every time and had not said anything about it, and Tomas Vale had noticed the slowing and had not mentioned it and had thought about it twice.

At about the seventh hour he said the arithmetic without being asked, and he said it the way a baker says a thing about a bake.

"**About four in five of anybody I could ask would say no.** Not because of you. Because of what the no is: a no is a thing you have to have an account of, and there is no box in this kingdom with *a carter of the second mile* on it, and a yes is a thing you can give away, so say yes and it is over. **There is a woman over at the lower end of this reach who has been keeping about two years of that, on her own table, in her own hand, and she has two pages, and the two pages are the same length, and that is arithmetic and not a scandal.** I have never asked to see either page and I am not going to and I would tell you not to either, and I will tell you why, and then I will not say it again: the day a page of a woman's refusals goes into a man's coat it stops being about nine people saying a word and becomes a column with a man in front of it, and I have watched that happen to a man once and it took him about four years to be able to say a true thing in a room again."

"You have not asked the four in five either."

"I asked four of them on the last go round and three of them said no and one said yes, and the yes was a cart that was standing, and about four in five is what I have got from watching two years of a road and not from asking anybody, and **a man who puts a figure on a thing that was not measured is the man this whole reach cannot stand, and I have been one, and I have stopped.**"

---

At about the tenth hour the carter came up the lane with the horse and the cart and did not come into the bakehouse, and stood at the gate about nine foot off, and Vessey went out and stood beside him and the two of them said about nine words to each other that Tomas Vale did not hear and was not near enough to hear and did not try to hear.

He stood inside the door with the cloth in his hands and listened to the cart move a foot and stop, and thought about how much of the morning had been about a cart standing in a field that had not been standing in a field on the day before, and how the whole of the yes had turned on a horse that wanted to be somewhere else, and that a man with a barrow had gone into a bakehouse at the first hour of a day and had come out with a day given to him by a trade that had its own reasons, and that this was the only time in a hundred and thirty days that anything had been given to him by anybody on the reach, and that it had come with an instruction attached to it about what not to say, and that he had been given the instruction and the thing in the same breath and had not been thanked for either and would not be.

"That is him," Vessey said. "**Ask him what the day is for. Do not tell him what you want it for, and if he offers you a place to sit and a hand with the loading, take both, and if he offers you a bed, do not take the bed, and I am telling you that about a bed because a man who sleeps in a place on nine miles of water is a man somebody will say has come to live on the reach, and in about nine years that is the only sentence anybody will have.**"

Tomas Vale went out to the gate. The cart was a plain two-wheeled one with a shaft down and about nine inch boards, and it had been standing in a field and it was not a fine thing and it was not a clean thing, and there was about a shovel of coal in the bottom of it already, which was not there at the seventh hour, and neither of them said anything about who put it there.

"Hal says you are going up to look at coals."

"I am. I am going up to the low end at the top of the water and I am going on the day after tomorrow, and I am not going past the ninth mile whatever anybody says, and there is nothing in that but coal." He took the reins. "**You can have the cart to the ninth mile and a hand with the loading, and there is no bed, and I am not going to be asked why, and if anybody on this reach asks you what I am doing I am going coals and you can say so or you can not, and I would rather you not, and I would rather that than a thing said about you instead of about me.**"

---

At about the ninth hour of the evening there was a barrow on flat ground above the lower end with the four bolts made fast, and a man with a horse and a two-wheeled cart at the top of the second mile, and a baker shutting a shutter, and about nine people on nine miles of water who were never asked anything by anybody about any of it.

Tomas Vale had a yes, and it was about a cart standing in a field and a driver's own coal, and it was not about him, and he had asked for it and had been given it and had been told the price of being given it, which was that nobody was to be told there were nine.

He wrote the six things down first and they do not change in a week. Then he wrote, on one line, what a settlement said when it was asked, and he ruled a bar under it, and he wrote under the bar that he had asked four people this week and been refused four times and had not thanked one of the four, and that the five that had happened to him today was the first yes and the yes was about a cart standing still, and that a man who turns a cart standing still into a column of nine is doing the thing this reach has been refusing to do since before he came up it, and that he was not going to do it, and that the price of that is that the one is the one.

He did not thank the baker. Gratitude is not entered anywhere in this kingdom, and a yes a man gives about his own morning is not the sort of thing a person can be thanked for, and the baker told me outright that he would rather have the one taken than be thanked, and I wrote both halves of that down, because a man who is told what is better by a person with a cart has been given advice and there is no form for advice on nine miles of water either.



> *Day four of the ten. The two hundred and tenth, at about the ninth hour of the evening, on flat ground above the lower end of the reach. A brick oven shut since the dark, a batch drawn at the first hour, a bench, a trough in the floor, a cloth, a pencil borrowed and given back at the seventh hour, a gate, a horse, a plain two-wheeled cart and about a shovel of coal that was not there at the seventh hour, four bolts, a leaf in a book.*
>
> *Second column, the ask, made properly. **I asked for a cart and a day to go up the water four miles and as far past as the day would do, and I said the whole of what I have to offer first: that I cannot pay with the barrow, that the second of five clauses is the reason, and that I have been refused four times this week by four people who were not cruel.** He would not give me the yes until I knew which yes he had. **A man with a barrow stops nobody and never has, and it is a fact about barrows and not about him, and about nine of them stopped me this week for free.**
>
> *Second column, the yes, and its shape. **A cart stands in a field at the fourth mile with nothing in it and the driver on a gate, and a cart that stands is a cart that is losing, and that is the whole of the reason and there is no reach in it.** The carter will take a day for it in coals, and this week's coals are on a different water, and he is going on a day that is not this one and told nobody but Vessey. **I have the one. I do not have the nine, and I asked for the nine and was given the one, and the difference was not made for me and not refused to me; it was the shape of the question and I did not know the shape until I was inside it.** He asked for the one to be taken and asked that nobody be told there were nine.
>
> *Second column, the arithmetic, and he gave it and did not measure it. **About four in five of anybody he could ask would say no, and it is not about me: a no is a thing you must have an account of and there is no box in this kingdom with a carter of the second mile on it, and a yes is a thing you can give away.** He asked four last time and three said no. **The only list on nine miles of water is a woman's two pages of her own asking on her own table and they are the same length, and he told me not to ask, because the day a page of refusals goes into a man's coat it becomes a column with a man in front of it.** I am not going for the page. **The six things I cannot fix are the six they have been all week.**
>
> *Third column, ruled apart, and the one thing I have to write plainly. **A yes that is about a man's own morning is not an agreement, and a settlement is not a thing that can say yes.** Nobody signed anything, nobody was asked to sign, and there was nothing to sign, and about nine people on this water will never know any of it, and about four have not been asked and have therefore said nothing. The three partial answers are still three. **I asked four people this week and was refused four times in four grammars and was not thanked for being refused, and today I asked a fifth and got a yes about a cart standing in a field, and I am not going to carry it anywhere as an answer to anything.** The schedule went into my hands on the hundred and thirty-fourth day and it is seventy-six days in my pocket, and the eleven lines are out of the same day, and the name is a woman's name in the same pocket and is seventy-six days, and nobody asked me for it today.
>
> *The five clauses stand and I am not going through them. Four bolts made fast at the fourth hour on the hard ground by the gate and again at the seventh. **The frame has not been in a building since the thirty-third, a hundred and seventy-seven days, and there is a bakehouse roof within nine foot of that barrow and a man in it, and I did not put a cradle in it, and the reason is not the second clause: a bakehouse floor is a floor a man kneels on and there is no straight edge in this kingdom I could put across it, and there is no form on which I can be told I may hold one either.** The carrier in the sack, **a hundred and thirty-three days out of the seventy-seventh, not rung once, and I did not touch it.** My own bell is in a sealed yard in Kells and the bar is on the bell and not on the licence, and it does not travel. **There is a handbell in her bag and it is not rung, and nobody has asked her for a reason at any hour of today, including me.** Nothing was struck, nothing was tied, nothing went into metal, I did not reach for anything, and nothing came.
>
> *The register is thirteen. **Nothing was asked for, produced, given or taken on any of the ten days of this week by me, and a page of a woman's own asking in her own hand on her own table is not a document and I have not asked to see it and I am not going to.** A hire, a row of nine men who would put a cart up for a day, a word at a gate: none of them is a paper. **Nobody was thanked today, and the two men who gave me the most are the two I spoke to least, and I wrote both of their instructions down because a man who is told what to say by a person with a cart is a man who has been given advice and there is no form for advice on nine miles of water.** Seventy-four days on this road with her and I did not ask her what it was for. Which office is it. **A yes that was about a cart standing in a field, and a column of nine that nobody wrote down and that I am not going to be the reason gets written.**
