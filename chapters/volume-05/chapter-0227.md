# Chapter 0227 — A Thatcher With Two Bones in His Foot, Seated, Splicing Reed He Cannot Put on a Roof

It was the third hour of the two hundred and thirteenth day and Wyman Ash was sitting on an upturned pail in his own yard splicing reed, and he was splicing because it is the only part of a thatcher's trade a man can do sitting down, and he was doing it at the third hour because he cannot start at the second any more, and it is the third hour, and not one person on nine miles of water has asked him about the hour.

The reed was in a stack against the north wall and it was about nine foot high and it had been cut in the week before the water came and stood in the wet on its butt ends, and it was going, and there was a roof on the fourth house of the row that wanted it on and did not get it, and there had been a roof like that for six days.

There was a ladder against the gable about nine foot off him and he had not looked at it since the dark before.

---

Tomas Vale came up the lane with the barrow and stopped about nine foot off the pail, and looked at the foot, and did not ask about it, and Ash put the first split reed down on his knee and said the thing about the second hour.

"**Six days ago I was up this ladder at the first hour and I was on a roof at the second, because I have been on a roof at the second for about nine years and the light is what it is.** I came down that bank at the dark with a bundle on the pole and the bundle is the light one and I could have carried four of them and I carried one, and I went the short way along the bank over the drain instead of round the two miles, and I have done that same short way a hundred times in nine years and it is a bank and not a road and everybody on this water knows it is a bank and not a road including me."

"How does a man break a foot on a bank he has walked a hundred times."

"**You put your weight on the wrong part of it.** There is a line of alder under that bank from the first post to the second hut and it has been going about nine years and it goes in about the middle of where the ground looks soundest, and it is the soundest-looking ground on the whole stretch, and that is what alder does." He turned his ankle round. "Two bones. The one on the outside and the one under it. The farrier looked at it on the second morning and put it back where it goes and gave me a stick and said nothing, and I have thought about the fact that he said nothing for six days and I would have said something."

---

At about the fourth hour a woman came up the lane with a bundle on a pole over her shoulder, and it was about nine foot of reed, and she came up to the yard, tipped it off the pole against the stack, and picked the pole up and went down the lane again, and nobody asked her anything and nobody thanked her and neither of the two men in the yard watched her go, because watching is a thing a man does when he is about to say something about it.

"That is six days' worth," Tomas Vale said.

"That is five days' worth and there is one more at about the second hour." Ash had not stopped splicing. "**I could carry that over the bank in about a quarter of an hour, and I have carried it over that bank about a hundred times, and I will not be able to carry it over a bank or round two miles for about nine days, and after nine days I will be able to walk on it and I will not be able to stand on a ladder for about three weeks, and after three weeks the reed in this stack will be about nine inches of nothing.**"

"So the roof does not get done."

"**The roof gets done in about nine weeks, at a cost, if the thatcher can stand.** And I am the thatcher and there is no other thatcher on nine miles of water, and I have said that out loud to nobody until just now, and I would have gone on not saying it for about nine days, and you have not asked me a question and I have said it, and I would like that marked, because it is the fourth time in six days that a man has got a true thing out of me by standing still."

---

At about the fifth hour the light came up off the water and he had a pile of spliced reed about nine inches high on the pail beside him and had not put a single splice on the stack, and Tomas Vale understood that the splicing was the only thing in the yard that had a use.

"Who has been doing your work for you this week."

"**Nobody. That is the answer and it took me a fortnight to get it and I have been angry about it for a day and a half.** The fourth house wanted a thatcher and there is no thatcher, so the fourth house has a man in it with a hammer and a bit of tin, and that is what happens. It is not anybody's fault and it is not a thing anybody has done to me. **The ground here is about nine miles long and I have been the only man on it who goes up a ladder, and that is not a fact anybody has ever put anywhere, and there is no form in this kingdom that would take a fact like that if I did, and I have wanted to be taken for about nine years and there is not a box in this kingdom with a thatcher of the fourth mile on it.** I have asked a woman once and she wrote nothing down and there is nothing in her hand that says I asked."

"Then how do you eat for three weeks."

"**I do not, and that is the whole of your week.**" He put the knife down. "**You have been on this water three weeks and you have been carrying a book about a settlement's week, and the settlement's week is mine, and it is not a week, it is three weeks without a thatcher on about nine roofs, and about four of those roofs are letting, and one of them has a woman and three children in the top of it, and they are not going to be wet, because I have been up on them at the first hour with a hammer and my own tin and I have not rung anything and nobody has rung anything and nobody is going to.**"

---

He had tried the ladder twice before the light and both times he had got his weight onto it and the whole of it had gone about an inch to the left, which is what a ladder does over a yard when one of its feet has rotted through on the side that is not against the wall. He told her about the ladder in the plainest words he had, and the plainest words were the ones about the rotted foot, and he did not use the word *cannot* in any of them.

"You have not been up it today," Tomas Vale said.

"I have been up it twice today and I came down twice and you saw neither and I did not tell you, and that is the fourth thing in a day I have not told you about, and I am not going to apologise for it, because a man who apologises to a stranger for what he has not told him yet is a man who has decided to be a good visitor, and I have not got the days for a good visitor." He put the reed down and moved the stick. "**The bit I want in the air is not *I am hurt*. It is *I cannot get on a ladder for three weeks*, and there is nobody on nine miles of water I would say it to, and I have known that since about the fourth day and I have been saying the other one to everybody.** That is not a lie. It is a man telling the truth in the order that keeps him able to work, and I have been doing it for nine years and there is no form in this kingdom that says you may not do it."

At about the tenth hour the light had gone and he got his hands on the ladder with the stick against it and stood up, and Tamsin Rook came up the lane with the last bundle about nine foot of reed on the pole and put it down against the stack without being asked and stood at the gate and did not offer and did not leave, and the two men did not discuss it and one of them saw the other one see it.

"Sit down."

"I am going to sit down." He did not sit down. "**I am not going to be helped up a ladder by a woman with a pole and I am not going to be helped up it by a man with a barrow, and I am saying that before either of you offers, and I would like it written down that I said it first.**"

"Written down," Tomas Vale said.

"Not written down. **Written down is the thing I have just told you not to do.**" Ash got his hand off the ladder. "**You have not asked me one thing all day and I have told you a man's whole trade and the number of roofs and the fact that I am the only one, and that is a thing I have not said to anybody in nine years, and you did not ask, and I am telling you now that I would have said it, and that is not a gift to you, it is what happens to a man who lives on nine miles of water and does not get asked.** And here is what I am not going to let you do with it. **You are not going to write my name in your book. You are not going to go and find somebody and say that a thatcher at the fourth mile is owed something, because there is no form in this kingdom that has a place to put an owed-to, and if you carry it up this water you have made me into a thing you are carrying.**"

"I had not thought of doing that."

"You had not thought of it, and in nine years somebody will." He got back on the pail. "**And the last of it, and I am telling you and you did not ask. The bank is not a road. That is all it is. I did not fall off a ladder and I did not fall in the drain and I did not fall into anybody's keeping. I put my weight on the wrong part of a bank that everybody on this water knows is a bank and not a road, on the sixth day of it, after a hundred times of being right. And if you write one word about me on that page, write that one.**"

---

The four bolts were made fast at the hard ground by the gate of the yard at about the ninth hour of the evening, and about a mile up the lane a man was sitting on an upturned pail in his own yard with a stick leaning against a ladder and about nine inches of spliced reed on a basket beside him, and about nine feet of a stack of reed behind him that was going every hour it was wet, and the fourth house of the row had a bit of tin on it put up by a man with a hammer, and nobody had rung anything.

Tomas Vale did not write a name. He wrote the whole day out in the ordinary hand and it was the longest single entry he has made in a hundred and thirty days, and it had no trade in the title of it and no settlement in it, and it was about a man on a pail and a stack of reed and the difference between a bank and a road, and he wrote down that he had wanted to write a name and had not, and that the reason he had not was not that a name cannot be written in a book, and that there are books full of names, and that the reason was that a name in a book is the beginning of carrying it, and that he had been told so on the sixth day of this week by a man with a barrow of nothing in his hands and nothing said about it out loud.

He wrote that nobody was thanked today and that a woman carried nine foot of reed five times in six days up a lane and was not thanked, and that he had watched her do it three times and had not said a word, and that a man had told him to his face not to write a name down, and that he was going to obey a man he had met nine days ago in a matter that was nobody's business but his own, and that this was the correct order of things and it was the fifth time in six days and he had stopped finding it strange.



> *Day seven of the ten. The two hundred and thirteenth, at about the ninth hour of the evening, on flat ground above the lower end of the reach. A yard on the fourth mile, an upturned pail, a stack of about nine foot of cut reed going in the wet, a ladder against a gable, a stick, a knife, a foot with two bones in it put back where they go, a fourth house with a bit of tin on it, a bundle on a pole five days running, four bolts, a leaf in a book.*
>
> *Second column, what happened, and it is one sentence and it is not anybody's fault. **Six days into a two-mile detour, at the dark, with the light bundle on the pole, I took the short way along the bank over the drain, which I have done about a hundred times in nine years, and I put my weight on the wrong part of a line of alder that has been going about nine years and that goes where the ground looks soundest.** Two bones. **A bank is not a road and everybody on this water knows that including me, and I had been right about that bank a hundred times and I was wrong about it once, and the once is the one that is in my leg.**
>
> *Second column, the week, and the number is not a week. **A thatcher's trade is a roof, and a roof is done from above, and I cannot stand on a ladder for about three weeks and can walk in about nine days, and the reed in that yard is nine days of cutting and will be about nine inches of nothing in three weeks.** One thatcher on nine miles of water and about nine roofs, and the fourth house has a man with a hammer and my own tin on it. **About four of the nine are letting and one has a woman and three children at the top of it and they are not going to be wet, because I have been on them at the first hour with a hammer, and there is no form in this kingdom that has a thatcher of the fourth mile on it and I have wanted to be taken for about nine years.** The settlement's week is my week and it is three weeks, and I said that out loud today to a man who had not asked me, and I would like it marked.
>
> *Second column, the two things I was told and the one I was given. **He was told not to write a name in a book, and he said written down is the thing I have just told you not to do, and he is right, and the reason is that a name in a book is the beginning of carrying it and there is no form in this kingdom with a place to put an owed-to.** And a woman carried nine foot of reed on a pole up this lane five days running and put it down against the stack without being asked, and **nobody asked her anything, and nobody thanked her, and I watched a man see her do it three times and say nothing, and I am not going to be the man who says that was a thing about her or about him.** Nothing was rung. Nothing is bound. The tin is on the fourth roof and it is not good and it is not a bell and it is the only thing anybody on nine miles of water has put over a hole this week.
>
> *Third column, the six I cannot fix and the ninth that is not mine. **Nine houses of a front row wet and two hundred and twenty-nine people dry; about forty people in a yard who were never told anything; a barge of nine tons of dressed stone upright in seven foot of water; a dressed sill half in and about nine days; a rate to be set in a fortnight by nine men who will not agree; about four people on nine miles of water who do not know a district's clerk came up here; and about nine roofs on this mile with no thatcher on them and a stack of reed in a yard a man cannot get at.** The three partial answers are still three. The schedule went into my hands on the hundred and thirty-fourth day and it is seventy-nine days in my pocket, and the eleven lines are out of the same day, and the name is a woman's name in the same pocket and is seventy-nine days, and I have not used it for anything and nobody has asked me for it today.
>
> *The five clauses stand and I am not going through them. Four bolts made fast at the fourth hour on the hard ground by the gate of a yard and again at the tenth. **The frame has not been in a building since the thirty-third, a hundred and eighty days, and there was a thatcher's own roof about thirty foot off that barrow, and I did not put a cradle in it, and the reason is not the second clause: he told me not to write a name down, and a barrow brought up to a yard in that hour would have been a thing I did in the same hour I said I would not.** The carrier in the sack, **a hundred and thirty-six days out of the seventy-seventh, not rung once, and I did not touch it.** My own bell is in a sealed yard in Kells and the bar is on the bell and not on the licence, and it does not travel. **There is a handbell in her bag and it is not rung, and it is hers, and nobody has asked her for a reason at any hour of any of the seven days of this week, including me.** Nothing was struck, nothing was tied, nothing went into metal, I did not reach for anything, and nothing came.
>
> *The register is thirteen. **Nothing was asked for, produced, given or taken on any of the ten days of this week by me, and no name was entered on any paper by me, and a bundle of reed on a pole, a stick, a bit of tin on a roof and a foot with two bones in it are not documents, because there is no form in this kingdom on which a man can produce a sore foot.** **Nobody was thanked today. A man told me four things he had told nobody in nine years and a woman carried his reed five days running and I was thanked by neither and I am not going to be able to pay either of them back and I did not try.** Seventy-seven days on this road with her and I did not ask her what it was for. Which office is it. **Two bones in a foot, put back where they go, and nine roofs on this reach that are not going to be done, and nothing anywhere on nine miles of water that could have rung about it.**
