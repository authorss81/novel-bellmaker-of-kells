# Chapter 0213 — A Kiln Four Miles Down the Water, and a Wall That Is Standing for a Reason Nobody Booked

It was the second hour of the hundred and ninety-ninth day and Mave Crale was banking a kiln with about nine tons of sods on the windward side, and she banks a kiln every morning of the warm half and has banked one for about nine years, and the sods are cut with a piece of chalk so that you can see at a glance which ones are her sods and which ones are somebody else's, and there is a row of them along the top of the bank with the chalk face out that has been there since before the reach had a name for itself.

Tomas Vale came up the cart way with the barrow behind him and stopped at the end of the row of sods, because a woman putting sods on a bank at the second hour is doing the thing that decides whether the fire is a fire or a heap.

"You will not smell it," she said, without turning round. "Ask me one thing and I will answer it in the middle of a sod."

"You have been told about me."

"Four people have told me about you in about nine days and none of them told me anything good, and I have not asked them for anything good, because the ones who tell you the good things are the ones who want something." She put a sod down with the chalk face out and banked the foot of it with the heel of a spade. "Ask."

---

At about the fourth hour the kiln had been going about two hours and she had four people round it, and she made him stand off the windward side, and she told him what she was going to tell him before she told it to him, which is a thing a kiln woman does because a kiln cannot be stopped for a conversation.

"I have decided I am going to tell you one thing about a wall, and I have decided it because you are going to stand in front of a wall in about nine days and if you get there without knowing what is in the mortar you will stand in front of it the way a man stands in front of a field."

"There is a low wall on three sides of that ground and it is not old."

"It is not old and it is about nine foot of rubble in a course and up and lime in every joint, and the lime is off this reach." She did not look at him. "**I have never been up to that wall in my life and I am not going now, and I know what is in the mortar because I can tell a lime from a stone by the way it goes under water after nine years, and there is a face on the inside of the third course from the corner nearest the water that has not gone soft and has not gone hard either, and that is a lime that was made well and used well.** Four of us burn on this bank. Nine years is about eleven kilns of ours in that wall and I have laid none of them, and nobody has paid anybody for any of them, and the wall stands."

"Stands because the lime was good."

"Stands because the lime was made by four people at kilns who were not asked to make anything for it and are not answerable for it and are not going to be. That is the whole of the reason and it is four inches long and I have said it to about nine men in nine years and eight of them wanted it longer." She set the spade down. "I am not saying that to get anything. I am saying it because you have a book and I do not, and if you go up there and see a wall that nobody can pay for, you are going to write *this is unsustainable*, and it is not. It is the most sustainable thing on this water and it will outlast every kiln on this bank including mine."

---

At about the fifth hour she had a hod of the burnt stuff out of the kiln and was sieving it on a frame of about nine foot, and the sieve is the trade's whole argument in one object: what comes through the sieve is lime and what stays on the sieve is a lump that has been in the fire too long and is a stone and not a lime, and there is no way of telling which of those a lump is until it is through.

"You are going to ask me what a keeper is," Crale said. "Everybody does, after a day. I have got one thing and I have got it in the only words I have." She shook the frame and the dust came off it into the air so that the light on the water went through it. "**A thing that has to be spent every day cannot be looked after.** A man put in charge of a heap of lime is a man blamed for the heap. He keeps it dry and roofed and counted, and in nine months the heap is stones and it is his, and the only way out is to spend it, and spending it is the one thing he is not allowed to decide. So that man is not a keeper. He is a man with a heap. I have had four heaps burnt by four men in nine years and I have not been sorry for one of them and have not said a word to one of them, and there is no form on which a woman says a word to a man about his heap, and I have not wanted one."

"That is not about a keeper."

"It is not about a keeper and it is the only half of a keeper I know that is true, and the other half I have never seen at all." She stopped sifting. "**The rest of it is a man at a wall with a besom, and I know about him, and I am not going to say what he does, because if I say it you will put mine next to his and mine and his are not the same shape and he knows they are not the same shape and I am not going to be the woman who made a man find that out.**"

---

At about the sixth hour the four people were on the cart way with the lime and the yoke and the woman counting fences, and the barrow was on the hard standing above the kiln yard, and there was a drying shed nine foot off it with three sides and a roof and no door on the near side and about four tons of burnt lime in it under sacks, and it was the first open thing with a floor and a roof and nobody in it that Tomas Vale had stood next to in eleven days, and the lime in it was the reason.

That is a different reason from the eleven days before it, and he wrote it down in his own hand at the end of the day and did not improve it, and the difference is that in the other eleven cases the obstacle was a person, a step, a beast, a boat or a shut door, and this one was a substance that a roof would spoil, and there is no form in this kingdom on which a man is told he may not put a barrow under a roof, and a woman with a kiln would be within her rights.

**He did not put it in, and he did not go in, and he stood about nine foot off for about four minutes, and the four minutes were not a decision. He has written that down and he has written that a man who calls a thing he did not do a choice is a man who wants to have chosen, and he is not going to write it a fifth time.**

Tamsin Rook was in the kiln yard for the better part of an hour with her palm flat on the cold iron door of the firing pit and did not move off it, and she wrote one entry that night about the heat coming up through the iron into a hand, and about how far up the door a hand can be put before it stops being information and starts being a decision, and it was not about the number of houses on this reach and not about the number of people on it, because she was not doing a count. Nobody asked her anything at any hour of that day, including him, and he was nine foot off the frame end of the cart way for most of the hour and did not go to her.

---

At about the tenth hour the kiln was done and she was drawing the burnt lime out of the fire-hole into a barrow of her own with a shovel, and it is the hottest hour of the whole day and the only hour of it that is work in the proper sense, and the man with the barrow asked her the question he had been carrying from about the fourth hour and got the answer he had expected and had wanted not to have.

"Will you go up and look at the wall."

"No."

"Will you go up and look at the wall and say what is in the mortar."

"No, and I would like you to hear the whole of why, because you have written down six things now and I have watched four men on this water say a true sentence to you and go on with their day, and you have not once said to any of them that you were writing it down." She put the shovel down. "**I do not care. I want it clear that I do not care. What I am telling you is that I have stood here eight hours with a fire on, and there is a woman on this bank who keeps a book with a name in it that you would like, and there are four men on this water who have each said a true thing to a stranger and not one of them has said two.** That is a network. I am not saying it is one. I am saying that you have got nine people on nine miles of water who have each said one true thing and not one has said two." She picked the shovel up again. "I have burnt nine tons of sods to tell a man with a barrow what is in a wall he has not reached. Do not thank me for it. There is no form in this kingdom in which a woman is thanked for a morning's work and I have not had a form made about it and I am not going to start."

She picked the shovel up again.

---

At about the ninth hour of the evening he was on flat ground above the kiln with four bolts made fast and about nine hundred yards of bank between him and the water, and the kiln was banked and cold and the row of sods with the chalk face out was the only thing on the whole yard that looked like it had been done on purpose.

He wrote down that he was not going to carry the lime-woman’s sentence up to the besom-man, not because it would not fit — it would fit, it was about the same wall — but because she had told him not to, and because a man who goes up and puts two true sentences side by side because they are about the same wall is a man who has made them one.
> *Day three of the ten. The hundred and ninety-ninth, at about the ninth hour of the evening, on flat ground above a lime kiln. A bank of sods, a row of sods with the chalk face turned out, a fire-hole, a shovel, a sieve of nine foot, a drying shed with three sides and no door on the near side and burnt lime in it, an iron door to a firing pit, four bolts, a barrow.*
>
> *Second column, the wall, given to me unasked and said to nine men in nine years. **The low wall on three sides of the flat ground is about nine foot of rubble and up with lime in every joint, and the lime is off this reach, and there is a face on the inside of the third course from the corner nearest the water that has gone neither soft nor hard, which is a lime made well and used well.** Eleven kilns of four people’s burning are in that wall in nine years, laid by none of them, paid for by nobody, and the wall stands. **It stands because the lime was made by four people at kilns who were not asked to make anything for it and are not answerable for it.** Four inches long, and eight of the nine men she has told wanted it longer. She has never been up to that wall and is not going.*
>
> *Second column, her term, and it is the first of these that is about a substance and not about a habit. **A thing that has to be spent every day cannot be looked after.** A man put in charge of a heap of lime is a man blamed for the heap: he keeps it dry and roofed and counted, and in nine months it is stones and it is his, and the only way out is to spend it, and spending it is the one thing he is not allowed to decide. Four heaps have been burnt by four men in nine years and she has not been sorry for one and has said nothing to one. **The other half of a keeper has never once been in front of her, and the half she knows belongs to a man at a wall, and she will not say what he does, because then I would put hers beside his and the two are not the same shape and he knows they are not.***
>
> *Third column, the two she refused. **She would not go up and look at the wall, and would not go up and say what is in the mortar, and gave the whole of why without being asked.** She has stood eight hours by a fire and counted up what I have been doing on this water for eleven days. Nine people on nine miles of water have each said one true thing to a stranger with a barrow and not one has said two. **She said I have got a network and that she was not saying it is one, and I have set her sentence down as she gave it and I am not going to argue with it and I am not going to go and find out whether she is right.** She said do not thank her for a morning’s work.*
>
> *Third column, the shed, and the reason is a different kind. **Three sides, a roof, no door on the near side, a floor, four tons of burnt lime under sacks, and nobody in it: the first such thing I have stood next to in eleven days.** A roof over that lime is how you spoil it, there is no form in this kingdom on which a man is told he may not put a barrow under a roof, and a woman with a kiln would be within her rights. **I stood nine foot off it for four minutes and the four minutes were not a decision and I am not going to call them one.** She was in that yard for the better part of an hour with her palm flat on a cold iron door and wrote one entry about heat coming up through iron into a hand, and it was not about the number of houses on this reach. **The schedule went into my hands on the hundred and thirty-fourth day and it is sixty-five days in my pocket, and the name is out of the same day and is sixty-five days, and it is a woman’s name, and I have not used it for anything.***
>
> *The five clauses stand and I am not going through them. Four bolts made fast at the fourth hour and again at the seventh, and at the ninth hour of the evening on flat ground above the kiln. **The frame has not been in a building since the thirty-third, a hundred and sixty-six days.** The carrier in the sack, **a hundred and twenty-two days out of the seventy-seventh, not rung once, and I did not touch it.** My own bell is in a sealed yard in Kells and the bar is on the bell and not on the licence, and it does not travel. **There is a handbell in her bag and it is not rung and nobody has asked her for a reason at any hour of today, including me.** Nothing was struck, nothing was tied, nothing went into metal, I did not reach for anything, and nothing came.*
>
> *The register is thirteen. **Nothing was asked for, produced, given or taken today, and no name was entered on any paper by me.** A row of sods is not a document and a sieve is not a document. **Sixty-three days on this road with her and I did not ask her what it was for.** Nine houses of a front row are wet and two hundred and twenty-nine are dry, forty people in a yard were never told anything, a barge of nine tons of dressed stone is upright in seven foot of water, a dressed sill is half in on a second pair of gates and is about nine days, and a rate is set in a fortnight by nine men who will not agree. I have fixed none of the five and am not going to. Which office is it. **A wall two miles up the water with eleven kilns of unpaid lime in it, four people on a bank who are not answerable for it, and nine miles of water on which nine people have each said one true thing and not one has said two.***
