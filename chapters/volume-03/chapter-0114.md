# Chapter 0114 — The List, And The Man Behind The Counter

The store is four streets and four flights above the arch, on the top terrace, and he went up at about the third hour of the hundredth day of the month and he was not stopped at any of the four flights by anybody, and he has written down that this is now the second building he has gone into in this city and that both times nobody stopped him, and that he is not going to make a habit of noticing it, because a man who keeps a count of how often he is not refused is a man who is waiting to be refused.

**The room is about eleven feet by sixteen. There is a counter across the front of it. There is a book on the counter, open. There is a man behind the counter. There is a shelf behind the man, and the shelf goes the whole way up the back wall and there is a second one under the window, and there are about nine hundred things in a city like this and a store like this and he did not see one of them and he was not shown one.**

The man behind the counter was called **Osmund Tarrant** and he was not slow.

---

He looked up once, read the slip out of Tomas's coat before Tomas had it out, and said:

“**That is my number. You have it right and I did not expect anybody to.**”

“**I did not expect to be here either.**”

“**Then you have come at a good hour and not a bad one, and I will tell you what a store is, because you have got the look of a man who has been told about one by somebody who did not work in one, and it takes as long as it takes to read one line and stop, and then you can decide what to do with your morning.**”

And he said it, in the ordinary voice, and he did not put anything on it, and Tomas Vale wrote the whole of it out flat afterwards because it is the second time in a hundred days that a man has described this room to a stranger without being asked and both times the man was right.

“**A district has a store. There is one in every district and ours is this one and I am in it. Somebody in a room brings an instruction to the store and the instruction has a number at the top of it and a district on it and a day on it. I go along the shelf. I find the thing the instruction says. I give the thing to the man who brought the instruction. And I write in this book what I gave out, and the number, and the district, and the day. That is a store.**”

“**And nobody has ever asked you what the things are for.**”

“**Nobody has ever asked me what anything in this room is for, including me, and I have been behind this counter for nine years, and there is no shelf in here that is not a number and a row.**”

---

**And then he said the thing, and he said it while he was writing a line in the open book, and he did not stop writing while he said it.**

“**You are going to ask me about the column. Everybody who has ever been in this room has asked me about the column, and there are not many of you, and it is the last one.**”

“**I have not asked you.**”

“**You have not, and that is why I am going to say it. There is a column in this book and it is between the fourth and the fifth and it is headed what the thing is for, and it has been empty every day I have been in this room, and it was empty before me, and it is not going to be filled in after me, and I have not filled it in, and I would like it written down by somebody, and I have been waiting nine years for somebody to write it down.**”

“**There is a smith two counties back who says the same thing.**”

Tarrant looked up for the second time that morning.

“**Says it how.**”

“**He says there is a column on the form for what a thing is for and nobody has ever filled it in in his hearing. He has fitted the same hand of work to three bells in his county in nine years and he would not tell me what it does, and he told me about that column by finding it with a finger and putting the finger on it, unhurried, and would not give me a theory when I did not offer him one.**”

“**Then he and I have said the same thing in two counties and neither of us has ever heard of the other, and that is the whole of what a column is. A column is a thing two men say the same thing about and never find out.**”

---

**And then Tarrant told him why he had not filled it in, and it is the reason, and it is four inches long, and it is the same reason a man in a village four counties back has never ruled a column of his own.**

“**I was told when I came into this room that the column was for a reason. That is all I was told. Nobody has told me what the reason is in nine years. **And I do not fill in a column in a book that I keep on behalf of a district for a reason I cannot answer for, and if a man came in here tomorrow and said what is in this column, I could not stand in front of him and say I put it there, and I have not put anything in it, so I can.** That is why it is empty. It is not a secret and it is not a conspiracy. It is a man who will not write down a word he cannot read back.**”

**And Tomas Vale thought about Abner Tole in a lean-to at a ford, who had wanted to rule a column of his own about nine times in nine years and had not, because a column a man rules for himself is a thing he has to be right about, and about four hundred notices, and about nine lines in a margin saying *I said it out loud at the board*, and there being no column for that either.**

**And he wrote down in his own head, and not in the book, that there are two kinds of empty column in this kingdom and he has now been in front of both of them, and that they are made of the same thing, which is a man who will not write a word he cannot be asked about.**

---

**And then the man offered him a sheet.**

It was a printed sheet, folded, the store's own copy of the form a district instruction comes in on, and Tarrant put it on the counter face up the way a clerk does, and he said:

“**Take it. It is printed. It costs this store nothing and there are about nine hundred of them in a drawer under the counter and they go in the fire every year. There is no number on that one and it is not a copy of anything, and there is nothing on it about you, and if you walk out of this room with it nobody will ever know you have got it.**”

---

**And Tomas Vale put both hands flat on the counter and said no, and he gave the reason out loud, unasked, without stopping the entry he was halfway through, and the man behind the counter did not say a word to it.**

“**No.**

“**You may ask me why, and I will answer it in about four seconds and then I am going to look at your column for a while and be quiet.**”

“**Say it.**”

“**On the eighty-first, in a town I have left, at about the sixth hour, in a room over a draper's shop, a man in a form and a satchel took five sheets off a table in about three hours, one at a time, and receipted them one at a time, and every part of it was lawful, and I stood there and let it happen, and the law that let it happen was a clause of a receipt I asked a clerk to add to in a watch house in another county, because I was right.**

“**A paper off a table in a room, and a paper off a public board in a market town with a drawing-pin in it, are the same act with a different name. **And I have been in four counties and I have watched it go wrong twice and I have got exactly one life, and a sheet of printed paper that nobody can account for is a sheet I cannot account for, and there is nobody in this room who could account for it for me, and you have told me the column is empty because you will not write a word you cannot read back, and I am not going to be the man who starts it up in a coat.**”

He put the sheet back on the counter.

“**That is four seconds and I am not going to improve it.**”

Tarrant took the sheet, folded it, and put it in the drawer under the counter without any expression at all, and said:

“**You are the third person in nine years who has refused one. The other two were women and one of them was right and one of them was not, and I have not thought about either of them since.**”

---

**And then Tomas Vale asked for the one thing he wanted, which was the shelf, and was refused in one sentence, correctly, by a man who did not enjoy refusing him and did not pretend to.**

“**Show me the shelf.**”

“**No.**”

“**Why not.**”

“**Because a store shows a shelf to an instruction, and you have not got an instruction, and if I opened that wall for a man with a barrow today then in about four months there would be nine men with barrows at my counter and I would be the man who opened it the first time, and there is a clerk four streets and four flights below me who will not walk a stranger up to this room, and he is right, and I have heard of that, and I do not know his name and I would not use it if I did.**”

“**You have heard of him.**”

“**There was a man at my counter on the ninety-fifth who asked what a paper was and got the whole of it, and there is one at the arch who does not walk strangers up four flights, and if a clerk is right then the shelf is shut, and the shelf is shut, and I am not going to talk to you about what is behind it, and I am not going to tell you it is nothing.**”

**And he said the last of it with his hand flat on the open book, in the way a clerk does, and Tomas Vale wrote it down word for word and it is the sentence the hundredth ends on.**

“**Whatever is on that wall came off it on somebody else's instruction and went into somebody else's hands, and the district's business in this room is to know what went out and when, and the thing a man came up four flights to ask me is not a thing I can answer, and I have never pretended to anybody that I could, and if you had come up here a week ago with a reason filled in by a person with a standing in a district I would have opened the wall and gone along it in front of you and named every row.**”

---

**And before that, at about the fourth hour, he put a question that had a date on it, because he is a man who has learned that a question with a fact at the end of it is the only kind worth asking.**

“**There is a notice on your terrace that says a list in this room is open on the third hour of a day.**”

“**Yes.**”

“**The day it names is not tomorrow. It is the day after tomorrow, and there is a hearing in this city on that day at the second hour, and a district has written the day of a hearing on a board about a list of wires and fittings.**”

“**A district writes days down.**” Tarrant did not look up. “**And I will tell you what is true and it is not interesting. **That list is open at the third hour every morning this store is open, and it has been for the nine years I have been in this room, and it goes out on the counter at the third hour whether a district has written a day on a board or not, and the day on that notice is the day a district decided to write it down. There is nothing in this room that is shut on any morning of the week and there never has been.**”

“**Then why does the notice say it.**”

“**Because a list that is always open is not a thing a district can put a date against, and a form has four boxes and one of them is a day.**”

He put the pen in the drawer.

“**If you want to know what in this kingdom is not open, it is about nine hundred things a year that come off the wall behind me, and every one of them is shut, and the way it is shut is that nobody may look at it without an instruction, and an instruction is a piece of paper with a number on it that somebody has had to bring here first.**”

**And then he said the rest of it, and it is the sentence he has gone over in his own head about once a month for nine years, and he said it without any weight on it at all, and Tomas Vale wrote it down word for word:**

“**And the reason that is the shape of it and not some other shape is that a man is not allowed to be angry at a list of wires. You can be angry at a man. You cannot be angry at a thing that came off a wall on somebody's instruction, because there is nobody in it, and if there is nobody in it then there is nobody to be angry at, and so the list is always open and the wall is always shut, and both of those are correct, and I have never once been able to make them both correct at the same time in my own head, and I have tried about once a month for nine years.**”

---

**And the column, at about the fifth hour, on his own, standing up, for about an hour, with a man behind the counter writing lines in a book and not once looking at him.**

> *About nine hundred entries a year. He said near enough, and then he said the true thing, which is that it has been between eight hundred and eleven hundred every year he has been in that room, and nobody has ever asked him for a better figure than near enough, and a man who wants a better figure than near enough is a man who wants a number he can put on a sheet.*
>
> ***And the column, from the first page to the last page of the four the book holds open on the counter, empty. Nine hundred times a year, a column, and a reason asked for at the door and never given, and a man who will not fill it in because he cannot read it back.***

**That is the whole of it. Fifty-eight days he has carried the question how an office gets into a bell, and a smith named a store and a clerk named a fitting, and neither of them would say what it does, and here is the inside of the room, and there is no plan in it and there is no man in it who wants anything and there is a book with a number against every line and a date and a district and a column for what the thing is for, and the column is empty from end to end, and that is not a secret and it is not a conspiracy and it is not a wall.**

**It is a column. And he cannot use it, and he is not going to be able to use it, and there is nothing in this room he could carry that would tell him what a coupling and a jumper do, and he did not ask, and he was not asked, and about nine hundred of these go out of this room every year on somebody's instruction and go into about nine hundred pairs of hands and not one of the nine hundred people who carry them has ever been in this room.**

---

**He is inside a building.** He has been inside two in this city and neither time was he refused, and the frame in its reinforced padded cradle is four streets and four flights down in the open under a tarpaulin on the flat with the four retaining bolts made fast at every place it has been set down since the thirty-third, and there is no clause about where a man may be and one about where a cradle may be, and the two do not have anything to do with each other, and he noticed that about a minute after he came in and he has written it down because it is the first thing in a hundred days that has been in his favour by accident.

He wrote the last entry of the hundredth at about the ninth hour of the evening on a step at the foot of the top terrace, with the frame in its cradle four feet off him and the bolts made fast, and he filled the first, second and third columns and left the fourth as it was.

> *The hundredth. **A column with nine hundred empty cells in it, and it is not a secret, and it is not a conspiracy, and it is not a wall.** I have carried *how does an office get into a bell* for fifty-eight days and the inside of that room is a book with a number on every line.*
>
> *Second column, and the part I am not going to improve in the morning: **a store is a room, a counter, a book, a man, a shelf and an instruction, and I have got all six of them in one room and I have seen none of the six actually do anything, because they do not do anything in front of people. They have done about nine hundred times this year and they will do about nine hundred times next year and there is a man in this city who writes down every one of them and goes home at the sixth hour and does not know and is not suspicious and is right.***
>
> *And the column. He was told when he came into that room that the column was for a reason and nobody has told him what the reason is in nine years, and he has not filled it in, and he gave me the reason at his own pace and did not shorten it, and it is the best reason anybody has given me in four counties: **a man will not write down a word in a book he keeps for somebody else that he cannot answer for when somebody stands in front of him and asks.** And there is no column for that, and there is no form for that, and it is why the column is empty, and it is not a door and it is not a wall.*
>
> *And there are two empty columns now in front of me and they are the same thing. A man in a village who wanted to rule one about how far ahead a bell goes and did not, in nine years, and a man in this city who will not fill one for what a thing is for, in nine years. **Two men, two counties, one sentence, and neither of them has ever heard of the other, and that is not a coincidence and it is not a conspiracy either — it is a column, and I have now seen both of its ends and there is nothing whatever at the middle.** I wanted a plan with a room round it. There is a column and two men who will not write in it.*
>
> *And he offered me a sheet. **A printed sheet, folded, out of a drawer under the counter, about nine hundred of them a year, and he said there is nothing on it about me and nobody would ever know.** And I said no in about four seconds and gave him the reason and he put it in the drawer without a word, and he told me I am the third person in nine years who has refused one, and one of the other two was right, and he has not thought about either of them since. **A paper off a table in a room in a town I have left, and a paper off a board in a market with a drawing-pin in it, are the same act with a different name, and I have got one life, and I am not going to be the man who starts it up in a coat.***
>
> *And I asked to see the shelf and he said no in one sentence and he was right, and he said the shelf is not shut because of me, it is shut because I have not got an instruction, and that the clerk four flights below him will not walk a stranger up and he has read about that clerk, and **if a clerk is right then the shelf is shut.** And then he said the sentence I am going to carry, and I am writing it out in my own arrangement and not his: **that wall is shut and the list on the counter is open, and both of those are the same rule seen from two ends — a thing leaves that wall on somebody else's instruction and goes into somebody else's hands, and what this room is for is the going out and the day, and not one other thing, and he is not going to be asked what any of it is for because he does not know either.** Not what it is. Not what it is for. **What went out and when. And that is a clerk doing his job, and I asked him for the one thing he has not got and he told me so instead of pretending, and I am not going to be thanked and he is not going to be thanked and neither of us is going to be thanked by anybody.***
>
> *Third column, ruled apart, and I am merging nothing: **about nine hundred entries a year, near enough, and every one of them with an instruction number, a district and a day, and not one of them with a reason. And about nine hundred of them went out of that room into about nine hundred pairs of hands, and not one of the nine hundred people carrying one has ever stood where I stood this morning, and I have no way of finding out a single one of them and no form on which to put the question.** I cannot use any of it. I have a number I can recite and a column I cannot fill.*
>
> *All five clauses stand. **Nothing rung, and nothing shown to me, and nothing named.** Four bolts, and the frame has not been inside a building since the thirty-third and I have been inside two and it makes no difference to the frame, and I have written that down because it is the only thing in a hundred days that has gone in my favour without me doing anything. The carrier in the sack untouched, twenty-three days, and I do not know where either of my ringings went and nobody is going to tell me and I have stopped wanting to be told. My own bell is in a sealed yard in Kells. **Thirteen documents, and a printed slip that is the Office's, and a form I cannot fill in, and a sheet of printed paper I was offered for nothing and refused in four seconds, and a drawer under a counter with about nine hundred of them in it that goes in the fire every year, and I have written all of that down and the total is still thirteen.**
>
> *Which office is it. **It is four streets and four flights up and it is a room with a counter, a book, a man, a shelf, an instruction, a number and a column, and about nine hundred of them a year, and I have got to the end of it and there is nothing in the room that wants anything.** Tomorrow at the third hour the list in that room is open on the counter and anybody may look at it, and I am going to look at it, and I am not going to take it, and the day after tomorrow at the second hour, four streets off, there is an upper room where a man is asked to produce things, and I have got nothing in my hands and no reason in my coat to put in the box for one.*
