# Chapter 0113 — What A Wharf Knows About A Wire

He was on the grain wharf at about the fourth hour of the ninety-ninth day of the month, and there were nine tons of grain on the floor and no way of telling a cart on the Wharf Road that the floor was clear, and that was the whole of the morning.

**The gate bell was on its frame, nine feet of it, sound, unbroken, and it could not be rung from anywhere except under the frame, and the man who used to ring it from the granary loft was standing on the loft with a hand on a rope that did not go anywhere.**

“A wire,” said Nevin Holt.

“Yes.”

“**Cut on the eighth, by the district's men, on the trench, lawfully, and I have not seen a paper and I have not wanted one.**”

---

There had been a trench along the wharf road for about a fortnight and it was down to the length of a man's arm and it was going to be a drain. The district had put a notice up on the flat about it, and Tomas had read it on the ninety-fifth on his way in with the barrow, and the notice had said, in the ordinary shape, that wires crossing the line were to be cut and joined again by the district's own men.

**There is no form in this kingdom on which a person may object to a wire being cut, because a wire is not a house and not a thing anybody keeps, and the notice said *wires* and not *wires belonging to anybody*, and every word of the notice was true and about nine hundred wires in this city have a word like that over them.**

**And a bell is not a wire. A bell on a frame over a gate on a wharf has a man who strikes it and a reason he strikes it, and none of that is cut by a trench, and the bell on the frame went on being a bell every day since the eighth, and it did not know, and it is still there, and it is the only thing on this wharf that has not changed.**

---

**And a man who has not slept and is holding a barrow is cheaper than a man who has.**

Holt said that in the ordinary way, at about the fifth hour, with his hand on a stack, and it was not cruelty, and Tomas Vale wrote it in his own book that evening as the truest thing anybody had said to him in a city of nine thousand people.

“**You have not slept. You have got that cut on your hand from yesterday and you did not look at it. You will move nine tons for nothing and you will not stop and you will not ask me a single question about the wire, and that is worth four days to me, and I am not going to pretend it is anything else.**”

He moved nine tons.

**Four hours, on boards, with a barrow that was not his and a sack that was not his and a hundred and eleven trips, and by the sixth hour his hands had stopped being hands and become the two ends of a thing, and there is no craft in this trade that is worth more than that and no man in it who has ever earned a place by it more cheaply.**

He did not ask about the wire. He asked about the cut.

---

**And the cut is the whole of the chapter, and it is about four inches long.**

He got down on the loft floor at about the seventh hour with the lamp and looked at the end of it where it came out of a lead sleeve at the wall, and he did not touch it, and he said what it was before he was asked, which is a thing a maker does and cannot be talked out of.

“**That is not a break. That has been cut. Somebody has had a tool on it and gone at it from above and it is a clean face and there is a burr on the underside where it started to tear as it went, and the tool was not a chisel and it was not a spade.**”

“**I watched him do it,**” said Holt. “**I watched a man put a cutter on it and I watched him look up first to see who was watching and I have thought about him looking up for a day.**”

“**He was right to look up. It is a wire in a trench and the notice says wires.**”

---

**And then Tomas Vale said the thing a bellmaker says, because he could not help it, and it was the right thing, and it was nine words long.**

“**I could join that in an hour. Two turns and a solder and a wipe of pitch and it would carry for nine years and you would not think about it again.**”

“**Yes.**”

“**And you are not going to have it done.**”

“**No.**”

And then the man told him why, in about nine seconds, standing on a loft floor with nine tons of grain on the floor below and a lighter booked and a list of about thirty joins in a room four streets away.

---

**“That is the whole of it and I have not got anybody to be angry at and you are going to take it away from me so take it properly.**

**“A wire that snaps is a fault. A fault is a thing that goes in a book. A wire that is cut by the district and joined again by the district is not a fault, it is a repair, and there is no fault in it anywhere, and the only paper in the world about that wire is the notice they put up on the flat, and the notice says the bell will not sound on that day, and it was right, and I have not got one grain of complaint against anybody.**

**“And if I put a solder on it myself, with my own hands, in my own loft, on my own account — then I have a bell on a frame over a gate with a mend in the line that is not in any book and cannot be entered in any book, and there is no form in this kingdom on which a man may say *I have mended my own wire and here it is*, and so I have got a fault that nobody can see, and a fault nobody can see is a fault I have to be asked about some morning by somebody with a reason.**”

He put his hand flat on the loft floor.

“**So I want them to cut it. I want them to cut it and I want them to be nine places down a list of about thirty, and I want it to be the ninth day, and I want the whole of it to have a date and a foreman on it and nobody's name on it, and I will stand here for nine days with nine tons of grain on the floor and a cart on the road and a bell on a frame that will not do anything.**”

“**And the day after the ninth day, when it is joined, that wire is a wire in a drain, and I could not tell you in a court this afternoon whether it had ever been cut at all.**”

---

**And Tomas Vale stood on a loft floor and understood the whole of the Office in one morning from a man who was not in it and had never been in it and was not going to.**

He wrote it down that evening and he has not been able to use it since, and it is the best thing anybody has said to him in three counties:

> *A bell you can blame. A wire you cannot.*

Because here is what it is. **There are about nine hundred bells in this city and about nine hundred men and women standing near them, and every one of those people can be asked, and can refuse, and can be wrong, and can be gone. And there are about nine hundred wires, and not one of them has anybody near it, and not one of them can be asked anything, and not one of them has ever had to be.**

**That is not a conspiracy. That is a drain.**

And a man at a counter four streets and four flights up has a book with a column in it, and the column is for what a thing is for, and it is empty from end to end, and a man in a village four counties back has a board and a book and nine years and wants a column for how many days ahead a bell goes and has not ruled it. **Everything he has been afraid of in a hundred and seven days is a wire and a column and a habit. None of those three is a person.**

**And that is the relief, and it is not a comfort, and he wrote both of those down in the same line and meant both of them, and the reason it is not a comfort is that a thing with no person in it cannot be answered, and he has spent eleven weeks learning that a person can be asked and can refuse, and he has just found out that the most important part of the whole of it cannot.**

---

**And he was wrong on the ninety-seventh, and he wrote that down, and it is the only time in a hundred and seven days that he has gone back and crossed out a thing he wrote in his own hand with no pressure on him at all.**

> *On the ninety-seventh I wrote down **a bell is a man standing next to it**, out of a wharfman's mouth, and I wrote down that I did not believe it. I was wrong to disbelieve it, and I have got the reason now, and the reason is a wire, and the reason a man does not mend the wire is that the mend would be a fault and the fault would be a book and the book would be a question with his name at the bottom of it.*

**A man who mends his own wire makes a thing that has to be answered for. A man whose wire is cut by a district and joined by a district has made nothing, and has answered for nothing, and is owed the whole of it back, and is standing on a loft at about the seventh hour of the ninety-ninth day of the month waiting nine days for it and it is the most correct decision anybody has made in four counties and a city and he is not going to be thanked for it by me because he did not do it for me.**

**That is the seed, and it came in a wharfman's mouth and not out of a book, and it is a sentence about a delay and about a lighter and about a cart on a road, and I am not going to make it into anything else. It is nine days old and I cannot use it. I do not even know yet what it is for.**

---

He went down off the loft at about the eighth hour and the foreman on the trench was in the road with a hod of chalk and a list on a board, and Tomas did not go and look at the list, and he wrote down that he did not look at it, and the reason is that it is a list of wires and wires do not have names on them and he would have got nothing from it that he had not got off a wharf floor in four hours, and the second reason is that a man with a barrow standing in a road reading a district's list is the exact shape of the thing he has been doing for eleven weeks and he is not going to do it in a road four streets from a bell on a frame.

He slept in the net-loft with a rope nine feet long beside his head and did not touch it.

**The frame in its cradle was under the tarpaulin thirty feet off with the four bolts made fast, and the bed it sits in is longer than this loft and there is no arrangement of this morning that changes that.**

He wrote the last entry of the ninety-ninth at about the ninth hour of the evening on the loft edge with the river under, and he filled the first, second and third columns and left the fourth as it was.

> *The ninety-ninth. **What a wharf knows about a wire.** Four hours, about a hundred and eleven trips, nine tons, no pay, a cut on the same hand as yesterday, and my hands stopped being hands somewhere about the sixth hour and I did not notice it happen and that is the only thing I have got today that I could keep.*
>
> *A district's trench, a notice that said wires, a clean cut from above with a burr underneath, and a bell on a frame over a gate that has gone on being a bell every day since the eighth and does not know and has not changed. **A cut wire does not stop a bell. It stops a wire. The bell on the other end of it does not know and goes on being a bell, and there are about nine hundred of those in this city, and every one of them is a separate object with a separate person near it, and not one of the persons is the person who could be asked about the wire.**
>
> *Second column, and this is the finding of the ninety-ninth and I am not going to improve it in the morning: **a bell you can blame and a wire you cannot.** A man who mends his own wire makes a fault, and a fault is a book, and a book is a question with his name at the bottom of it. A man whose wire is cut by a district and joined by a district has made nothing and answered for nothing and is owed all of it back. **A man at that wharf would rather lose nine days and a cart than own a mend, and he is right, and there is no form in this kingdom on which he could put *I mended it myself and here it is*, and I have been eleven weeks learning that a person can be asked and can refuse and I have just found out that the most important part of all of it cannot be asked anything.***
>
> *And everything I have been afraid of in a hundred and seven days is **a wire and a column and a habit. None of those three is a person.** A man with a book in a village wants a column for how far ahead a bell goes and has not ruled it in nine years. A man with a book in this city has a column for what a thing is for and has never filled it in. A foreman in a road has a list of about thirty joins and no names on it, because a list of wires does not carry names. **That is not a conspiracy. That is a drain.** And it is the relief and it is not a comfort, and the reason it is not a comfort is that a thing with no person in it cannot be answered, and I have come four counties and seventy miles to answer things.*
>
> *And I was wrong on the ninety-seventh. **A bell is a man standing next to it.** I wrote that down out of a wharfman's mouth and wrote down that I did not believe it, and I was wrong to disbelieve it, and I have got the reason now and the reason is a wire, and I have gone back over nine days of my own book and put a line under that sentence in my own hand with nobody asking me to.*
>
> *Third column, ruled apart, and I am merging nothing: **he is waiting nine days, on a lighter he has booked, on nine tons on a floor, for a repair he asked for and could do himself in an hour, and it is the most correct decision anybody has made in four counties and a city, and he is not going to be thanked for it by me, because he did not do it for me and would not want it.** And I moved nine tons for him and I was not thanked and I did not ask, and that is the correct arrangement and I want it in the book because in a month I am going to want to be thanked for something and I will not have earned it.*
>
> *And I did not go and read the foreman's list, and I wrote down that I did not, and the reason is a wire list and a barrow and a road and about thirty joins and nothing on any of it that I did not get off a loft floor in four hours.*
>
> *All five clauses stand. **Nothing rung, and I did not ring anything, and I did not touch the wire, and I said what the cut was and I was not asked to and I would have said it again.** Four bolts, the frame out of doors on the flat under a tarpaulin, and it has not been inside anything since the thirty-third. The carrier in the sack untouched, twenty-two days, and I do not know where either of my two public ringings went and I am not going to find out and nobody is going to be told them as a favour. My own bell is in a sealed yard in Kells and I am not going to it. **Thirteen documents, and the printed instruction I carried off a trestle on the ninety-third is Crown paper and cannot go in a coat, and a fair copy of Crown paper is the Crown's from the moment it is made, and I have not asked anybody for a copy of it and I am not going to.**
>
> *Which office is it. **It is a format and it is also a drain, and I have got both halves now and neither of them is a room, and I am not going to write the second one down twice.** Four flights up there is a room with a counter and a book and a man and a shelf, and the list in that room is open at the third hour on the hundredth, and a hearing is four days off, and the man in the road with the chalk has about thirty joins on a list and not one name on it, and I could not have asked him a single question, because he is not the person who would have to answer it.*
