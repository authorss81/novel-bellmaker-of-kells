# Chapter 0130 — What A District Does With A True Sentence

It was the fourth hour of the hundred and sixteenth day and Tomas Vale went up four steps and through a door propped with a bucket and there were about nine rows on the board on the far wall of that room, and the top one was his, and there was about forty people's handwriting under his.

**He read his own sentence standing up in about as long as it takes to read a thing you know by heart, which is about four seconds, and it is four sentences long and about ninety words, and it is the truest thing he has ever said out loud, and it moves nothing.**

And then he looked at the rest of the board, and he read the other eight rows, and he read them the way he has read about nine hundred lines in a corridor over three days, which is all of them and none of them.

**And at about the fifth hour he asked the man with the lamp a question, and he had decided it on a step the night before, and this one was not cheap.**

“**What happens to it.**”

“**To the row.**”

“**To the row.**”

Hale put his pen down. It is about four years and a lamp and a tray and he had not been asked anything today.

“**It goes on the wall, and a fair copy goes to the district, and the district circulates it.**”

“**Circulates it where.**”

“**That is a district's business and not mine and you have not got a standing in it, and I am not going to pretend I do not know the answer, because I have watched about four hundred of them go out of this room in four years and I have never once seen one come back.**”

“**That is not an answer and I know it is not and I am not going to argue with it.**”

“**No. It is not.**” He put his pen back down. “**And I am going to say one more thing and then I am going to go back to my tray, and I want it understood that I am saying it because you have asked me two questions in three days and both of them were answerable, and not one person in this room in four years has asked me a question I could not answer. **A sworn answer is an answer, and an answer is a thing about a person, and a district is required to send an answer round.** I have never seen the rule. Everybody in this building has worked here longer than I have and not one of them could tell you the rule, and we do not need to know it, because it does not have a face on it and it does not have a day on it either.**”

“**And nobody can stop it.**”

“**Nobody can stop it and nobody is going to try and there is not one person in this kingdom who is wicked. **You have been looking for eleven days for a man in a room and there is not one, and there is not going to be one, and the thing that is going to happen to your four sentences is going to happen in the correct form, out of the correct column, by nobody in particular, and you will not be able to find anybody to be angry with and that is not a comfort and it is not a defeat. It is a rule.**”

---

## And he went down to the arch at the foot of the top terrace and asked a man at a counter, because a counter takes papers and not people and a man behind one is the only kind of man in this city who will tell a stranger the ordinary way of a thing.

Perrin Lowe did not look up.

“**I have not got a paper.**”

“**You have not got a paper and you have had one thing off a wall in this city that you swore to in a room three streets off, and I have known about that since yesterday, because a sheet came past my counter on a cart at about the sixth hour with about nine others on it and I do not open other people's post, and one of them had a district's head on it and a name at the top of it, and I did not stop the cart.**”

Tomas Vale put both hands flat on the counter.

“**Where does it go.**”

“**Round. That is what round means. **A district makes about eleven thousand lines a year and not one of them is a thing a man reads, and the reading of them is nobody's business, and the sending of them is everybody's business.** A copy of an answer goes on a board in the ward. **That is a board on a public wall in a market and about two hundred people a day walk past it, and that is where your four sentences are going, and I have not got a form for stopping it and neither have you and you have not got a reason that I could enter, and I have been here about nine years and I have never once wanted to.**”

“**Which ward.**”

“**That is a district's business and it is the correct arrangement and you are asking me for a thing because you want to be able to do something about it, and you cannot, and I am not going to be the man who gives you the address of a thing I cannot stop.**”

## And so he went and stood in a market of about nine hundred people on the middle terrace at about the sixth hour and there was a board on a public wall with about two hundred people in front of it.

**About two hundred people, and not a crowd. Two hundred at a board is an ordinary morning in a market of about nine hundred, and nobody was shouting, and there was no bell, and a man at a pitch four hundred yards off was selling something and about nine children were in it.**

He stood at the edge and he did not go in.

**The sheet had not been nailed up yet.** It was on the board, loose, at the right-hand end, under about nine others, and the nails were in the board and the sheet was not on them, and a woman with a tray of pins and a hammer was doing three boards along and would come to it in about nine minutes.

**He stood and watched her do it.**

She did two other sheets first. She did them the way a woman does four hundred small jobs, which is without looking at any of them, and then she came to his and she read the top of it to get the place right — **which is a thing a person does with a nail and a hammer and a sheet, and it is not reading it, it is finding the top** — and then she put two nails in at the top corners about four inches apart, and she did not hammer the second one straight and she straightened it with her thumb, and then she went on down the board.

About four hundred people had read it yesterday. He does not know that and is not going to be told. **About two hundred people read it today, in about nine minutes, while a woman with a tray of pins and a hammer worked along a board.**

## And then the man who told him the price was nine feet off, selling something, in a market, on his own pitch, and Tomas Vale stood four feet off him and let him.

**Ord Marren had nine rows in that market and had held them nine years and he had a book of his own and no office, and on the hundred and fifth, eleven days ago, in the ordinary voice, in a market, without pointing, he told a stranger what it costs to be named — about somebody else — and Tomas Vale stood four feet off him and said nothing and did not thank him and did not correct him and was not going to tell him.**

Marren had his own true sentence on the board in the river courts four streets off, third row down, in his own hand, in his own name, put there on the hundred and second, and he had been named on a public board in a market of about two thousand, four days before that, and neither of those two things was the same as this.

He was selling. He had been selling. He did not look up.

**And Tomas Vale stood four feet off him and watched him sell for about nine minutes and did not say one word, and then he went and stood nine feet further off, which is a thing he learned from a woman nine feet off his left shoulder and has never once done on purpose until this minute.**

---

## And here is the whole of what he found out about the price, and he found it out for nothing, in a market, standing up, on the hundred and sixteenth day, and the man who taught it to him does not know he taught it and is not going to be told.

*About two hundred people read it, and the reading is the cheap part, and it costs the district about fourpence.*

*About nine of them will say the name in a different town inside a year, and a man who says a name about four times inside a year in four towns has made it into a thing, and there is no undoing it.*

*Every one of the about nine gets it right the first time and gets it more wrong after that, and in about two years there is a version of it that has a detail in it that was never on the sheet, and by then nobody remembers which of them added it.*

*And there is no form anywhere in this kingdom by which a man who has been named on a board can be unnamed, and there is no clerk who can do it, and one man has looked, and looking is what he does.*

---

**And he did not write the man's name, and he is not going to, and that is the only thing in a hundred and sixteen days he has been given for nothing and has refused to pay for in any way at all, and he has had it for eleven days and has used it twice, and the second time it was about himself and the man was four feet off him selling.**

**And he did not go back to the court and ask for the sheet to be taken down. He knows exactly why he will not, and the reason is four inches long and it is a habit and not a principle and he has had it since the ninety-eighth:**

> *Whenever a building shows me a way of reading a person's own sworn word, I think about hers.*
>
> **And there is no box in this kingdom by which a man who has been named on a board can be unnamed, and the one place in this city where I could go and say the words *take that down* is a room with a rail in it, and the room has no door on it and it does not need one, and if I walk into it and ask, then in about four years there is a man at that rail who will say — a man with a barrow asked me once, on a day, about his own sheet — and I have had that said to me about a sentence I asked a man for, and I wrote it down, and it took a man nine years keeping a gate to find the words and he is right.**

---

**And at about the ninth hour of the evening he was on a step at the foot of the top stair with the tarpaulin over the cradle and the four bolts made fast where they were made fast at the fourth hour, and the sack rolled against the wheel, and the frame had not been in a building since the thirty-third, and he had stood in a market for about an hour this evening and it had not been anything anybody could describe.**

Tamsin Rook was ten feet off his left shoulder. He did not ask her for anything and nobody has asked her for a reason at any hour in a hundred and sixteen days, and he has written that down once in nineteen days and is not going to write it down again.

He wrote the last entry of the hundred and sixteenth, and filled the first, second and third columns and left the fourth as it was.

> *The hundred and sixteenth. **I swore a true thing yesterday for fourpence and today about two hundred people read it in a market while a woman with a tray of pins and a hammer worked along a board, and the reading is the cheap part.**
>
> *Second column, and the whole of the day, and it is the fourth time since the hundred and eighth that a building has shown me the ordinary way of a thing and nobody has ever been cruel in one of them. **A sworn answer is an answer. An answer is a thing about a person. A district is required to send an answer round.** A fair copy goes out of a room with a lamp in it, and it goes on a board on a public wall in a ward, and about two hundred people a day walk past that board, and about eleven thousand lines are made in a district in a year and nobody is unrolling them and there is no form for a list and there never has been. **And there is not one person in this kingdom who is wicked and there is not going to be one, and I have spent a hundred and sixteen days looking for a face and the thing that is going to happen to my four sentences has no face on it and it is not a room and it is not a man.**
>
> *And the finding, and it is the last thing I am going to write this month. **I swore a true thing on purpose, and it was true, and it cost me fourpence, and it has cost me the only thing I had, which was a sentence nobody had said about me.** There is a sheet in a file four streets off with four hundred words in the office's own hand and the last line of it says the person named produced nothing, and that was the only paper in the world anybody could put on a table and say *this man*, **and I have wanted a second one for ten days and yesterday I made one and it is nailed to a board in a market and it is about a column in a store's book and it does not say what I am for and it is not going to.**
>
> *And there is no form anywhere in this kingdom by which a man who has been named on a board can be unnamed. **I said that sentence in a market on the hundred and fifth about somebody else and I stood four feet off the man who said it and let him and did not correct him and was not going to tell him, and today I was the somebody else and there was nobody four feet off me to say it, and that is the whole difference and it is the whole of what a hundred and sixteen days buys.**
>
> *Third column, ruled apart, and the four sentences. **I have written down the price in the words of a man who gave it to me for nothing in a market eleven days ago, and I have not written his name, and I am not going to, and he was four feet off me this evening selling on his own pitch and he did not look up and I stood four feet off him for about nine minutes and did not say one word, and then I stood nine feet further off, which is a thing I learned from a woman and have never done until tonight.** About four hundred read the first one and about nine will say the name in another town inside a year and every one of the nine gets it right the first time and more wrong after that, **and in about two years there is a version of it with a detail in it that was never on the sheet, and by then nobody will remember which of them added it, and I will not be able to go and look, because he has not been told and I am not going to tell him, and that is a thing I have now done to one man and been done to, and neither of us knows about the other one.**
>
> *And I did not go back. **There is a room three streets off with a rail in it and a man with a lamp and about nine people a week who come and go through a door propped with a bucket, and if I walk in there and say the words *take that down* then in about four years there is a man at that rail who will say that a man with a barrow asked him once about his own sheet.** I have heard that said to me about a sentence I asked a man for, on the hundred and sixth, in a yard, and I wrote it down, and the man who said it is right and he keeps a gate and strikes his own bell and he is not going to be thanked by me or by anybody. **And I have had that habit since the ninety-eighth and it is a habit and not a principle: whenever a building shows me a way of reading a person's own sworn word, I think about hers, and today the building showed me the way and I did not take it, and I do not know that I was right and I am not going to be told.**
>
> *And the five dates in a drawer in a county I have left are where they were. **I have not touched them in a hundred and sixteen days and I am not going to touch them this month, and nobody in this city has asked me about them and nobody is going to, and I have not explained them to myself and I am not going to.** Nobody in this kingdom can tell me and I have not told anybody in four counties and a city and not one of them has asked and that is the correct arrangement and I am not going to improve it.
>
> *All five clauses stand. **Nothing rung. I have not rung anything in a hundred and sixteen days, and the plain carrier is in a sack against this step, thirty-nine days untouched, and I put my hand on its crown on the hundred and sixth and put it back, and I do not know where either of my two public ringings went and I am not going to find out.** My own bell is in a sealed yard in Kells and I am not going to it and no bell I have made is unbarred anywhere in this kingdom tonight. Four bolts, and the frame has not been in a building since the thirty-third and I was in a room with a rail in it at the fourth hour and in a market for about an hour and it makes no difference to the frame, **because a cradle is a building-sized thing for a bell and there is no clause in five that will let me put one inside anything and I have been asking four flights up for sixteen days and the answer is the same on every floor.**
>
> *The register is thirteen. **Thirteen documents in a coat, and a court record that is the court's, and a sworn sentence that is the court's, and a fair copy of that sentence on a district's cart and now on a board, and eleven lines in a fair hand in a fold in a bundle on a rail that nobody may copy and that I did not copy, and a sheet on a board on this terrace with my sister's name at the foot of it that I did not take off.** Thirteen and none of them is a fourteenth and all of them are somebody else's, and the one thing in the world that is about me is nailed to a board in a market and is four sentences about a column.
>
> *Which office is it. **It is a rule.** A sworn answer is an answer, an answer is a thing about a person, and a district is required to send an answer round, and the rule was written by somebody who had a reason, and that person is not available, does not need to be, and is not a person I could be angry with if I had the name. **It is a rule and it moves nothing and it has taken the only sentence anybody had ever said about me and put it where two hundred people can read it and about nine of them will carry it to four towns, and it is a rule, and nobody in this kingdom is wicked, and I am not going to be a man who says the second sentence is a victory or the first one is a defeat, because they are both inventory and I have not got anything else.**
>
> *And a notice that has not been nailed up yet. **A sheet went past a counter at the arch at about the sixth hour on a cart with about nine others on it and one of them had my name at the top of it, and the woman with the tray of pins got to it this evening and put two nails in about four inches apart, and there are about four hundred more of them in this district and there is no form for a list and there never has been, and I have found that out four days too late and there is nothing whatever in this kingdom I can do about one sheet of paper.**
>
> ***I have proved an edit. I have a duty my mother accepted and no sentence at all about what became of her, and it is a duty and not a conviction, and the difference between the two is a four-inch box at the end of a form, and a man with a lamp said *it is the same form* in about four seconds and went back to his tray.** I have no stage. I have no sense. I have got a plain bell I have not rung in thirty-nine days and eleven lines nobody may copy and a woman ten feet off my left shoulder and about two hundred people reading a column.*
