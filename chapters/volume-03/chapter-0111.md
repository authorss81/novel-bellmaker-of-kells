# Chapter 0111 — The Flat, And Nine Hundred Bells Waiting

He came down onto the flat below the water at about the fourth hour of the ninety-seventh day of the month, because the flat is where you come down to when there is nothing to go up to, and the barrow came down last and loudly, and a man on a bollard watched it come and did not move.

**The frame went on the flat in its cradle with the four retaining bolts made fast, in the open, under a tarpaulin he had bought for a penny and would lose.** Nothing in this city that costs money is going to cost him money, and the second clause of a receipt he asked a clerk to add to in a county he has left is the reason, and it is the same reason this morning as it was the morning the carrier came to Kells, and it will be the same reason tomorrow.

He had slept two nights in a stair out of the rain at the back of a sail-loft and been moved on from the second night by a man who was not unkind about it and who had a yard, and who wanted the stair for a cart, and who gave him a rope and a place against a wall and about four hours.

**And then the noise of a city at the fourth hour, which is not noise. In a town of two thousand the fourth hour is a sound a man makes. Here it is a sound four thousand people make who are all expecting something from the same hour.**

---

There were bells on the flat. He had seen them on the ninety-fifth and had not been in among them, and in among them the thing is different, because in among them there are bells that are for nothing but being looked at.

**Nine of them in a walk of about a mile.** Four over gates, two on frames, one on a gantry at the head of a slip, one on a granary gable, and one on the frame of a net-loft with a rope hanging off it nine feet long and worn shiny in three places where a hand goes.

**And every one of them was silent, and none of them was broken, and he had been in this city about a day and a half and had still not got over it.**

---

He asked about the top terrace four times before the sixth hour and he got nothing four times, and the four nothings were not the same nothing, and that is worth more than a wall.

A woman at a fish pitch said there was nothing up there but offices and went back to her fish.

A boy of about eleven carrying a hoop asked who Tomas was, and then ran.

A carter with three horses looked up the hill, at about nine hundred feet of it, and said, “**No. Not for nothing.**”

And a man in a doorway with a tally stick in his hand said, at about eleven seconds, which was the whole of his answer and the most useful thing anybody said to him all morning:

“**You could ask. There is nobody up there I would ask.**”

That was true, and it was not the same as a refusal, and Tomas wrote it down in his head and not in the book, because there is a difference between a thing that goes in the book and a thing that goes in the head, and the book is the one that gets read by whoever takes it off him.

**Nobody in this city is going to talk about the top terrace to a man with a barrow. That is not a wall either. A wall is put there on purpose. This is a thing nine thousand people have all decided separately, and the sum of nine thousand separate decisions is a wall, and there is nobody in charge of it, and that is the part he is going to have to get used to.**

---

He worked a wharf.

**A grain wharf, about two hours, and it was the first thing his hands had done for a person since the twenty-eighth, and it earned him nothing at all, and the man who let him do it is called Nevin Holt and he did not thank him and did not remember him and told him so.**

The second clause is the reason. A man with a barrow cannot pay for anything anywhere in this kingdom, because the frame has to stay in the cradle for the carriage, and a cradle is not a thing you can stand at a counter with. So a man with a barrow is a man who can carry, and a man who can carry is free, and free labour on a wharf in a city of nine thousand people is not remarkable and gets no thanks, which is the correct arrangement.

He carried sacks up a plank from a lighter to a floor. Nine trips, and then eleven. His hands knew the weight before he did. He had a cut open on the side of his right hand by about the seventh trip and he did not look at it and it did not slow him down, and there is no better argument in this book for a man being a maker than nine trips up a plank.

And on the tenth trip Holt said, without stopping, “**You have not asked me about that frame. Everybody asks me about that frame.**”

“No.”

“**Why not.**”

“**Because you have not got to and I have not got standing to, and I would rather do the work than spend what I have got asking.**”

Holt looked at him for about two seconds, which on that wharf was a long time, and then went back to the sacks.

---

**And then the man told him about the bells, on his own account, because the tenth trip is the trip on which a man talks.**

There were nine in the walk of a mile. Holt went through them, and he did it the way a man goes through his own household.

The one over the near gate on a frame of two uprights and a crossbar: his own, and he struck it, and the reason he struck it was that the grain went off that wharf at a certain hour and a cart on the road was four hours late and the hour was the point.

The one on the granary gable: **not his.** It belonged to a granary lease and the man who struck it worked in the granary and did not live on this street, and on a day the granary was shut the bell still stood there and said nothing, and Holt said, “**So it is not a bell, on a shut day. It is iron on a frame. Anybody can tell you that and nobody will.**”

The one on the gantry at the head of a slip: a rope, and a boy, and the boy had been doing it since he could walk, and the boy was not here today and the gantry stood there all morning with the rope swinging a foot and a half in the wind from nothing.

The net-loft one, on its frame, with the rope worn shiny in three places: **that one was why Tomas Vale had a place to sleep**, and the man who let him have it did not own the bell and did not strike it, and the bell was struck by whoever was on the loft and could be, and had not been since the rope was new.

“That is four,” said Holt. “**There are about nine you can see from here and I have counted them about nine times in nine years and I have never once written it down, and if you asked me how many bells there are in this city I would tell you I do not know, and I would not be lying, and you would go away thinking I was keeping it from you.**”

“You are not keeping it.”

“**I have never once been asked for it.**”

---

**And here is the sentence of the wharf, and it is four inches long, and Tomas Vale wrote it down at the fourth milestone of his own understanding and has not been able to do one single thing with it since.**

> *A bell is a man standing next to it.*

“**That is what a bell is on a wharf,**” Holt said. “**It is the man and the hour and the reason. Take the man away and it is iron. Take the reason away and it is a noise somebody makes for himself. I have had a bell thirty-one years on this flat and I have never once had a list of mine and I have never wanted one, because a list would be a thing somebody could come and read.**”

**And Tomas Vale wrote it down and did not believe it, and he wrote down that he did not believe it, and he wrote down that he wrote down that he did not believe it, which is the third time in ten days he has done that and which he has not told anybody about.**

He wrote: *I do not believe this. A wire is not a man. There is a bell on a post eleven miles out of Wendle that has rung with nobody within forty yards of it and I stood under it, and I have not got a better answer for that than I have got for anything else in this city.*

**He did not know for about two days that he was wrong, and when he found out it was not in a room and it did not cost anything and it was said by a man who wanted a cart to move.**

---

**What a city of nine thousand bells does at the hour.**

He had been in a yard on the top terrace two days ago and watched about two hundred people come out of doors and stand in the cold and let a bell ring four times and go home again without a word, and he had written it down as though it were about that bell, and on the evening of the ninety-seventh he stood on a wharf on the flat with a cut on his hand and worked out that it was not about that bell.

**It is about the hour. In a town of two thousand a man is a stranger on a market morning. In a city of nine thousand he is a man who came out of his own door at the hour, and there is no third thing to be, and every bell in the place is one of the hour's appointments, and about nine hundred of them are waiting for an hour that has not come yet, and the count of them is a thing nobody keeps, because there is nothing at the other end of it to be counted against.**

There is no roll of the waiting bells in this city. There is no form. **Nobody in Sennet knows how many bells are on the flat, and nobody in the district knows how many are on the middle, and if a man went to a counter at the foot of the top and asked how many bells this city has, the honest answer would be *as many as somebody pays for*, and there would be no column anywhere for what they are for.**

**That is the whole of a city, and it took him two days and a sack of somebody else's grain to see it, and he has been four counties looking for a face.**

---

He asked Holt one thing more, at about the sixth hour, and got an answer in about nine seconds, flatly, from a man who was counting sacks.

“**What is the Office.**”

“**Four buildings, and about nine hundred rooms in them, and a yard, and a bell over a gate, and the bell goes at the sixth hour, and the men in it go home at the sixth hour, and that is all I know and I have not been up there in nineteen years and I am not going.**”

**And then, because Tomas had worked eleven trips and had his hand open, and because a man will say the true thing to a man who has carried for him:**

“**And it is duller than you think it is. That is the thing I would tell you. Everything in it is duller than the telling. A man up there decides a thing and it goes on a board and the board goes on a cart, and about eleven thousand times a year, and not one of the eleven thousand is a man anybody could be angry at.**”

---

He slept that night in a net-loft on the flat with a rope nine feet long hanging beside his head, worn shiny in three places where a hand goes, and he did not touch it, and the frame in its cradle was thirty feet away under a tarpaulin with four bolts made fast and no clapper in it and it was the fourth night he has slept within reach of it and it will be a hundred nights before that changes, and he has written down that he is not going to be told by anybody what to do about it.

He wrote the last entry of the ninety-seventh at about the ninth hour of the evening, sitting on the loft edge with the river going under, and he filled the first, second and third columns and left the fourth as it was.

> *The ninety-seventh. **The flat. Nine bells in a mile of it, and not one of them has an office attached to it that anybody will name.***
>
> *A grain wharf, about two hours, nine trips and then eleven, nothing for it, and my hands knew the weight of a grain sack before I did, and there is no better argument in this book for me being a maker than a plank and a lighter, and I want that written down before the morning turns it into a lesson.*
>
> *And the man is Nevin Holt and he told me about his own four bells in the ordinary way on the tenth trip without being asked, and he told me **a bell is a man standing next to it**, and I have written it down and I do not believe it, and I have written that down too.*
>
> *Second column, and the hard half. **I have been looking for a face for nine days and I have found the opposite of one and it is not a comfort and it is not the good news I have been telling myself it is going to be.** The Office is four buildings and nine hundred rooms and a yard and a bell over a gate, and the men in it go home at the sixth hour, and a man on a wharf who has had a bell on a frame for thirty-one years says in nine seconds that **everything in it is duller than the telling**, and he is right, and I have come four counties and seventy miles to be angry at somebody and there is nobody to be angry at and the best thing anybody has offered me this week is a berth on a net-loft and a man's name.*
>
> *Third column, ruled apart, and I am merging nothing: **nine bells in a mile and not one of them broken and not one of them sounding, and about nine hundred of them in this city, and there is no roll of the waiting bells and no form and no column anywhere for what they are for, because a bell that has not gone yet is a bell somebody intends to use and nobody can be made to account for an intention in a room.** Yesterday I stood in a yard and wrote down that these people came out of doors for the bell, and that is a thing I saw and not the thing that is happening. **They came out for the hour. The bell is what the hour has instead of a man going round with a stick, and I have had that the wrong way round for two days and I am not going to improve it in the morning.***
>
> *And I asked four people on this flat about the top terrace this morning and I got four nothings, and one of them was a carter who said *no, not for nothing*, and one of them was a man in a doorway who said **you could ask, there is nobody up there I would ask**, and he was right, and I did not go up, and I am going up on the hundredth, and I have a printed slip in my coat with a district on it and a store's number and a form I cannot fill in, and the whole of the difference between this morning and the hundredth is four flights of stairs and a man with a book.*
>
> *All five clauses stand. **Nothing was rung, and nothing was rung by me, and I did not touch a bell on this flat although I slept under one and there was a rope within reach of my hand all night and I want that in the book because it is the sort of thing I would not have believed about myself a month ago.** Four bolts, the frame out of doors under a tarpaulin on the flat, and the frame has not been inside anything since the thirty-third and a tarpaulin is not a building. The carrier in the sack untouched, twenty days now, and I do not know where either of my two ringings went and I am not going to be told. My own bell is in a sealed yard in Kells and I am not going to it. **Thirteen documents in a coat and a man's name and a place to sleep, and the name and the place are not a document and I have been careful to write them down in the same breath as the thirteen so that nobody can add them up and get fourteen.**
>
> *And a woman ten feet off my left shoulder who has not asked me one thing in two days, and who has said the whole of it to me exactly once, on the ninety-second, in a market, and who was not thanked and is not going to be, and I have not asked her what the fourth column says and I am not going to, and **the count of the column is still eight, and it was eight on the ninety-second and it is eight tonight, and a thing that is not done is not a thing that has been done, and I have written both halves down because I keep wanting the other half.**
