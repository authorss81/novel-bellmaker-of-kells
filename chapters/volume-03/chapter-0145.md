# Chapter 0145 — Nine Miles Of Copper And A Woman On A Sill

It was the fourth hour of the hundred and thirty-first day and Tomas Vale went down off the top stair to the flat and did not leave the barrow there, because the second clause says the frame stays in the reinforced padded cradle for the carriage and he made the four bolts fast at the fourth hour where they were made fast at the fourth hour, and then he went out on foot.

**He was going to find out what a signal he has rung actually reaches, and who is in it, and he was going to do it on foot, because a man with a barrow in a street stops people and a man on foot does not.**

---

The first thing he found was a road open in it.

A district's trench, dug for a renewal that has been done, left open with the stone stacked at the side and a notice on a stake, and the notice says *wires* and does not say whose wires they are, and it has said that since the hundred and sixth, and he read it on the way in and thought it was a bad notice and has had four days to find out that it is a true one.

**A bell you can blame and a wire you cannot.** A man who mends his own wire in his own loft, on his own account, makes a fault nobody can see, and a fault nobody can see is a fault he is asked about some morning by somebody with a reason, and there is no form in this kingdom on which a man may say *I have mended my own wire and here it is*. So a wharfman will lose nine days, a lighter and a cart on the road rather than own a mend, **and that is the most correct decision anybody has made in four counties and a city, and he is not going to be thanked for it, because he did not do it for me.**

In the bottom of the trench, under about nine feet of dirt and stone, the wire was cut.

**A clean cut from above with a burr underneath it. I have been a maker for thirty-three years and I know what a cut from above leaves and what a cut from the side leaves, and that was a cut from above, and there is a burr on the underneath of it, and the burr is a thing a tool leaves and not a thing a man leaves, and I have not asked anybody about it and I am not going to.**

---

At about the fifth hour he was in a yard with about nine people in it and a man stacking sacks, and he asked the man one question and it was not about the wire.

“**Did the line answer before.**”

“**What line.**”

“**Yours.**”

“**I have never had one.**”

“**I am sorry. Somebody else's, that runs under this road.**”

The man thought about it for longer than the question deserved.

“**My father's did.**” He put a sack down. “**When I was a boy there was a line that came up through the bottom of that wall and it went out to the river and it answered, and there was a day it stopped answering and my father went looking for it and he never told anybody what he found and he never had it again and he did not go and ask why.**”

“**Why not.**”

“**Because the ones above him stopped too.**” He picked the sack up again. “**And when the ones above you stop, the ones above them go quiet, and the last one out is always a building with a bell on it that has to go at the hour, and it goes when it goes because it has always gone at that hour and nobody has got a reason to think of it any other way. I have not asked about that either. I have got nine sacks and I have got to get them in before the wet.**”

**He does not know why a route that answers is a route that does not have to be argued with, and he has not asked, and he has a wall his father put his hand in a wall on. I am not going to write that down in my own book in a way that makes me the man who found it out, because I did not find it out, I asked one question in a yard and a man told me about his father, and neither of us is going to say one word about it again.**

---

At about the sixth hour he was a mile and a half down the road with his hands in his pockets and it came to him that he was walking a line, and he walked the rest of it.

**Nine miles of ground with copper under it and eleven miles of run, and it is not the same number and the two numbers are not about the same thing: the eleven is how far it goes, and the nine is how far a man can walk in a day in this city without a barrow and with nothing in his hands.** A bell on a post, a bracket, a socket, and a wire in the ground under a road, and it goes to about nine hundred things, and a man with a book and no office reads out what came over it in a village of about two hundred people nine days' road up this hill, at about nine shillings a quarter, and nobody has ever asked him how far ahead of the ringing the sheet is, and there is no column for it.

**And every one of the about nine hundred of them goes at an hour appointed by somebody else's paper. That is what a sound is in this city. And the district's business is the sending and the sending is free, and the reading is nobody's business, and that is not a wall and it is not a conspiracy. It is a rate and a column and a hole where a column ought to be.**

At a gate about four streets off the top he passed a frame with a bell on it, and the bell went while he was standing at the gate, and a man inside the yard turned a plate over without looking at it and did not stop, and the bell did not know and has not known since the frame was new.

**A bell you can blame and a wire you cannot, and a bypass is a road and not a claim, and a road does not know who is standing on it.**

---

At about the seventh hour he worked out where she was.

He had known for about four seconds and he had not asked and he had not gone to the road, and she was about two streets off, on the ground floor of a building with a shuttered shop, and she was sitting on a sill with her back to the wall and her feet on the boards and her hands flat on the frame of the opening, and there was a cut in the road outside the shutter about nine feet from her and the trench was open and there were three men in it with a bar and a lamp between them.

**She is holding a sill and a floor and a wall. She is the one who reads vibration and she has been reading it for eleven years and I have known since the ninety-second that she could not hear a note and there has never once been a day I have known that and been able to do one single thing about it.**

He stood at the corner for about four minutes and did not go to the road.

**And the reason is a thing I have not got yet and will get tomorrow, and the reason I know I have not got it is that I have written down every reason since the ninety-first and there is a page of reasons in my own book and there is not one of them about her, and there is not going to be one, because a man who writes a reason for not helping a woman is doing the help for himself.**

He turned round and walked back up the hill to the step at the foot of the top stair and made the four bolts fast where he had made them fast at the fourth hour, and they had not moved and he checked them anyway.

He wrote the last entry of the hundred and thirty-first and filled the first, second and third columns and left the fourth as it was.

> *The hundred and thirty-first, at about the ninth hour of the evening. **Nine miles of ground with copper under it and eleven miles of run, one open trench, one clean cut from above with a burr underneath it, one notice that says *wires* and not *wires belonging to anybody*, one gate with a bell on a frame that went while I was standing at it, and a woman on a sill about two streets off who is holding that building with her hands and does not know I was at the corner.**
>
> *Second column, and what a signal reaches. **A bell is a wire, a bracket, a post and a sheet. A bell on a post is a notice. The sheet under it is not a copy of the sound; the sound is the thing and the sheet is what the sound was for, and the sound arrives before the man with the book, and a man with a book cannot get to about two hundred doors in a day and a bell costs nothing to send, and that is the whole of the coercion and it is arithmetic.** Nine miles of ground and eleven miles of run and about nine hundred things at the end of it, **and not one of them is a keeper's bell and every one of them is the hour.**
>
> *And the cut. **A clean cut from above with a burr underneath, in a district's trench, in a road that is open, under a notice that says *wires*.** A man who mends his own wire makes a fault nobody can see, and a fault nobody can see is a fault he is asked about some morning by somebody with a reason, and there is no form anywhere in this kingdom on which a man may say *I have mended my own wire and here it is*. **So a wharfman will lose nine days, a lighter and a cart rather than own a mend, and that is the most correct decision anybody has made in four counties and a city, and he did not do it for me and I am not going to thank him.** And a service cut and a bypass are a road and not a claim, **and a road does not know who is standing on it.**
>
> *Third column, ruled apart, and the part I am not going to improve. **A forced relay is not mine to stop and I have no standing to stop it, and there is no form in this kingdom on which a man with no standing in any county may lay a hand on a district's line, and the line is not mine and the bell on the gate is not mine and the trench is not mine.** I asked one man one question in a yard about whether a line used to answer, and he told me about his father, and about a day it stopped, and about the ones above, and he has not asked either and I am not going to be the man who asks him. **I did not ask why a route that answers is a route that does not have to be argued with and I have not come away with it and I am not going to go and look for it, and if it is in the hundred and thirty-first it is a thing a man said about his father and not a finding.**
>
> *And the sill. **She is holding a sill and a floor and a wall and she is the one who reads vibration, and I am a man with a bar on his own bell and no authority to be within nine feet of any of it, and I stood at a corner for about four minutes and I did not go to the road and I did not tell her to come off it and I did not ask her for anything.** The instrument that is the only one that would carry a true sentence out of me in one evening and put it in front of every door in this city **is also the only one that runs through a sill she is holding, and those are the same fact, and I have been refusing the instrument for a hundred and thirty-one days and I have never once asked who stands in it.**
>
> *All five clauses stand. **Nothing rung, by me or by her, and nothing bound, and nothing counted into iron, and no stage, and no perception attempted and none had, and no Crown fitting called anything it is not and the word is not used, and no mechanism described and no name transferred and no child found by a document and no sheet taken off a board.** Four bolts made fast, and the frame has not been in a building since the thirty-third and it has been in the open every hour of today and a yard and a road and a gate and a corner are not buildings. **The carrier in the sack, not rung, fifty-four days from the seventy-seventh, and it is not struck.** My own bell is in a sealed yard in Kells and I am not going to it, and no bell I have ever made is unbarred anywhere in this kingdom tonight. **Her bell is in her bag.**
>
> *Nobody asked her for a reason at any hour in a hundred and thirty-one days and I did not ask her about the sill and I did not ask her about the road and I am not going to, **and the slate was not used today and I am not going to write down that it was not.**
>
> *The register is thirteen. **Thirteen documents in a coat, none of them about me, and nine miles of road walked and one cut looked at and one man's father, and none of them is a fourteenth and I have produced nothing and asked for nothing today and I have been given nothing.**
>
> *Which office is it. **It is a trench, a notice that says *wires*, a cut from above with a burr under it, a bypass, and about eleven miles of run to about nine hundred things, and a woman on a sill two streets off who is holding the building she is sitting in and is not going to be asked for a reason by anybody including me.** Nobody in it is wicked. **A bell you can blame and a wire you cannot, and a man who owns a mend owns nine days, and the whole of a hundred and thirty-one days is a line under a road that nobody can be shown and everybody can be asked about.**
