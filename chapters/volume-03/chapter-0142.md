# Chapter 0142 — The Place A Bell Is Not

It was the fourth hour of the hundred and twenty-eighth day and Tomas Vale hung the rope back on the frame of the net-loft where it hangs, and then stood under it and looked at it.

**Nine feet long and worn shiny in three places where a hand goes. It belongs to the loft. It is the fourth thing in this business that a person can be about without saying a word about it, and a woman had it in both hands on the hundred and thirteenth and put it back, and I have not touched it in a hundred and twenty days, and I used nine feet of it yesterday to put a post in a bank and I have given it back before the day was out because it is not mine and there is no form in this kingdom on which a person borrows a rope.**

The bar and the spade were still on the flat and the post was in the ground with an empty bracket on it and the tarpaulin was over the cradle thirty feet off.

He left them where they were and walked.

---

At about the fifth hour he walked the Wharf Road end to end again, from the wharves to the river, and this time he was not asking three questions at bells. He was looking at posts, and at walls, and at the foot of every gate on the flat, and he was looking for a piece of iron that belongs to nobody.

**A gate is a gate's. A wall is a wall's. A post on a bank is the district's, because a district keeps the ground under its lines and puts a sheet on a board at the end of it. I went a mile and I found about nine things fixed to the ground and not one of them had no name on it, and I did not ask a single one of them who they belonged to, because the answer is on the thing and the thing is a document and I have read about nine hundred of those this month.**

He stopped at a yard with a gate in it and a bell on a frame over the gate, and the man who had the yard was not in, and there was a plate on the frame with a ring in it and nothing on the plate.

**A bell over a gate is a gate's bell. It is not a public bell. It is not anybody's warning. It is the sound a gate makes so that a man inside knows that something outside has moved, and that has been the case since the frame was new, and the man inside does not strike it, and the man outside cannot, and there is no form in this kingdom on which either of them may.**

He stood in the road about a quarter of an hour and then went up.

---

Four streets and four flights up there is a room with a counter and a book and a shelf behind it, and the wall behind the shelf is shut.

Osmund Tarrant had a man at the counter with an instruction in his hand, and the instruction had a number on it and a district and a day, and Tomas Vale stood about nine feet off the end of the counter and did not say one word for the whole of it.

Tarrant went along the shelf. He took down one thing and put it on the counter and wrote in the book what went out, the number, the district and the day, and pushed the book round so the man could see the line he had just written, and the man read it and said it was right and went out with the thing.

**Nine hundred of those a year. Every one of them carried out of that door by a different man and about four of them come back broken inside the warranty. I have stood at this counter four times in forty days and I have never once seen anybody look at what went out, and I have worked out why, and it is because the man who could use the book is a man with an instruction in his hand and there has not been one in this room for about four days.**

He looked up.

“**You have not got an instruction.**”

“**No.**”

“**Then I cannot show you the shelf.**”

“**You have not said that until now.**”

“**I have said it twice before, in one sentence each, and you have asked me four questions this month and I have answered none of them, and there is a man going out of that door and I have a book to close.**” He closed the book. It took him about as long as it takes to turn a page. “**The book is open. The wall is shut. Both of those are correct and I am not going to explain them, because if I explain them then in about four years somebody with a column will ask me why I explained them.**”

“**I am not asking you why.**”

“**No. And you have never once asked me anything, and I have said so to myself about four times this month, and I am not going to be the man who says it out loud to a stranger, and I have just said it out loud to a stranger, and I would like you to leave.**”

He left it.

---

At about the sixth hour he was on the middle terrace with about nine hundred people in the market around him and about two hundred of them in front of a wall, and he went up four steps and through a door propped with a bucket and along a corridor and into a second room three doors along, and Corvin Hale had a desk with a lamp on it and a tray of about nine folded forms and four boards screwed to a wall.

He did not look at the boards. He had looked at about nine hundred lines of them standing up on the hundred and ninth and he had got what he came for and he was not going to take a second look at them for a thing he could have asked one question about.

“**Is this the same form.**”

Hale put his pen down.

“**No.**”

“**It is the form the districts send in about a thing in a ward.**” He turned the top one round without asking. “**Four boxes. The ward, the day, the thing, and the keeper. And the fourth one is not the ground, whatever you have been told the fourth one is in this building, because this is not a matter, it is a return, and a return about a bell wants to know who holds it.**”

“**How many come in.**”

“**About nine hundred a year.**” He said it the way a man says a number he has said before. “**It is the same about nine hundred as the matters on those boards, because a ward and a matter are the same number and they arrive together in the same cart, and I have never once seen one without the other and I have never been asked why.**”

“**Is the keeper's box filled in.**”

“**On all of them but one.**”

“**How do you know that.**”

“**Because a box that is simply empty is not argued with and is not forgotten, and a form that has been argued with has a line through it, and a man who forgets a box fills it in later and does it with a different pen.**” He turned the pen over. “**The one that is empty is on a line about a post on a bank at the side of a road, and the word in the fourth box on the line above it is *coupling*, and the word in the fourth box on the one below is *coupling* as well, and I have said the same thing about those three boxes four times this month and you are the only person who has come in here to ask me the same question twice.**”

“**Can I fill it in.**”

Hale looked at him for longer than the question took.

“**You have not got a standing in any ward in this kingdom and I have known that since the hundred and ninth and I have not asked you, and I am not going to ask you now.**” He put the pen down again. “**And if you had one, and if you put a name in that box, then somebody in a ward has a keeper. A keeper is not a man who holds a bell. A keeper is a person with a duty accepted, and a duty accepted is a thing that goes out of that person and into every return that district makes afterwards, and a district makes about eleven thousand lines a year and about nine hundred of them are these.**”

“**So filling it in is a claim on a household.**”

“**Filling it in is a claim on a household that has not been asked whether it wants one, and there is no box on this form anywhere that says the household was asked.**”

---

He stood in a corridor on the middle terrace at about the seventh hour and worked out what he had been carrying for a hundred and twenty-eight days, and it came out in the wrong place, the way it does.

> *A keeper's bell is a bell with a living keeper's hand on it. **Not a bell on a post, and not a bell somebody else decided to ring, and not a bell a district is holding for a ward.** About nine hundred bells in this city and not one of them is a keeper's bell and I have known that since the hundred and twenty-fifth and I had it in my own hand on the hundred and twenty-sixth, and this afternoon I have got the form it would go in and the form has a box for a keeper and the box is empty on one line out of about nine hundred.*
>
> ***And the fifth clause of my own receipt is the reason I cannot fill it in. It says the holder has no standing to speak for any household in Kells or in any ward, and I asked a clerk to write that clause on the thirty-third because he was right, and it has now cost me a form with a box in it that a man could have filled in with one finger, and that is the seventh thing it has cost me and every one of them has cost me something and nothing else has.***
>
> *So: a plain carrier may carry one claim at a time, and a bell that has been answered once and then carried twice is not a bell. And I have a carrier in a sack with nothing in it, and no keeper, and one answer, and the one answer is me, and a bell one man put up and one man struck is a man talking.*
>
> *And the thing underneath all of it, which I did not have until this afternoon. **A claim in metal cannot be shown to anybody on a page. That has been my protection for a hundred and twenty-eight days: no clerk, no district, no man in any building in this kingdom can ask me to see what I have said, because there is nowhere it could be written and I have never wanted it written.** And it is the reason I cannot put one where nine hundred people could be told about it, **and a man who cannot be found cannot hang a bell where a street can hear it, and that is not irony and it is a shape, and the shape is four inches long and it is the fifth clause and I asked for it.**

He wrote the last entry of the hundred and twenty-eighth and filled the first, second and third columns and left the fourth as it was.

> *The hundred and twenty-eight, at about the ninth hour of the evening. **A rope hung back where it hangs, a mile of road walked looking for a piece of iron that belongs to nobody, one thing issued out of a store against an instruction and written in a book, and one form with a box for a keeper on it that is empty on one line out of about nine hundred.**
>
> *Second column, and the finding. **There is nowhere in this city to put a bell that is not somebody's, and there is a form for it, and the form wants a keeper.** A gate, a board, a post, a yard, a shop door: five answers in two days and every one of them has an owner with a paper. **About nine hundred bells in this city and not one of them is a keeper's bell, because a keeper's bell is a bell with a living keeper's hand on it and there is not one of those here.** A carrier may carry one claim at a time and a bell answered once and then carried twice is not a bell.
>
> *Third column, ruled apart, and the part I could not get round. **A return about a thing in a ward has four boxes and the fourth is the keeper, and the keeper's box is filled on all of about nine hundred a year and empty on one, and the one is a post on a bank at the side of a road, and a clerk who has been in that room about four years told me the count himself and he did not have to be asked twice.** I could have filled it in. I had the paper in front of me and there was a pen on the desk and he would have let me, because there is no form in this kingdom that says a man with no standing may not pick up a pen, **and putting a name in that box is not filling in a form, it is a keeper accepting a duty for a household that has not been asked, and there is no box on the whole of that paper anywhere that says the household was asked.**
>
> *And the reason I could not fill it in is a clause I asked a clerk to write for me on the thirty-third, in five clauses of which all five still stand, and the fifth of them says the holder has no standing to speak for any household in Kells or in any ward. **That clause has now done seven jobs in a hundred and twenty-eight days and it has cost me something every single time and nothing else has**, and the seventh of them is the one that matters and it is the one I asked for: it is the reason there is no household in this city I can put a bell in front of.
>
> *And underneath that, and this is the part I am going to carry. **The thing that has protected me for a hundred and twenty-eight days is the thing that stops me.** A claim in metal cannot be shown to anybody on a page, and that is why no room in this kingdom has ever been able to ask me one question, and it is also why I cannot put one up in a street where a few hundred people would hear it and know it had been said. **A man who cannot be found cannot hang a bell where a street can be reached by it.** That is not irony. That is a shape, and the shape is four inches long, and I asked for it.
>
> *All five clauses stand. **Nothing rung, by me or by her, and nothing bound, and nothing counted into iron, and no document produced, given or asked for, and no name transferred, and no child found by a document, and no sheet taken off a board.** Four bolts made fast, and the frame has not been in a building since the thirty-third, and a corridor and a counter and a room with a desk in it are all buildings and the barrow was at the foot of the stair for all of it. **The carrier in the sack, not rung, fifty-one days from the seventy-seventh, and it is not struck.** My own bell is in a sealed yard in Kells and I am not going to it, and no bell I have ever made is unbarred anywhere in this kingdom tonight. **Her bell is in her bag.**
>
> *The register is thirteen. **Thirteen documents in a coat, none of them about me. A fitting went out of a store on somebody else's instruction this morning and it is the store's and it is in the store's book and I did not see what it was and I was not told and I did not ask, and that is not a fourteenth and it is not going to be in my coat, and I have not asked for a copy of anything in a hundred and twenty-eight days.** Nine bells in a mile and four sentences in my own hand in my own book are not documents and are not anybody's.
>
> *Which office is it. **It is a return with four boxes and one of them wanting a keeper, and about nine hundred bells that are the hour, and a gate and a board and a post and a yard, and a book in a store that records what went out and a wall behind it that is shut, and a clerk who says no in about four seconds and is not sorry, and a storekeeper who has now told me he would like me to leave and I have gone and he was right.** And I asked the cheapest question in this kingdom for the fifth time in a hundred and twenty-eight days and I got a form back with a keeper's box on it, **and the standing reason I could not fill it in is four inches long and it is a clause of my own receipt and it is the only thing in a hundred and twenty-eight days that has never once been wrong.***
