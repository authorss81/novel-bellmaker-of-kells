# Chapter 0101 — A Bell On A Post

The rain had stopped somewhere in the night without Tomas Vale noticing it stop, which was a thing that had not happened in eleven years, and the road up off the water was a made road for the first two miles and then it was not, and a barrow will go at the speed of a barrow whichever of those it is on.

He was four miles up it at about the fourth hour of the eighty-seventh day of the month.

**He had a barrow, a frame in a reinforced padded cradle in the barrow with the four retaining bolts made fast at every place the frame had been set down since the thirty-third, a sack against the cradle with a bell of fourteen pounds two ounces in it, thirteen documents in a coat under a book, five receipts, a fragment of nine lines out of a book of numbers, a slate and four columns and a road.**

And no room. There had been a yard behind a draper's shop at about the fourth hour yesterday and a man with a book of his own who had put two loaves and a piece of cheese on the barrow and said out loud, on purpose, that it was on the pitch.

That is the whole of what he had and he was going to write it out flat on the ninety-fifth day or whenever he stopped, and not before.

---

The difficulty of the day arrived at about the sixth hour and it arrived on its own, and it was a bell.

It was on a post about eleven feet above a notice-board at a place where the road crosses a stream, and the bell was about as big as a milk pan and it was hung with its mouth down and there was nothing under it and nobody near it, and Tomas Vale had the barrow stopped in the middle of the road looking at it before he had decided to look at it.

**It rang.**

Not close. Not the way a bell rings when somebody is standing under it swinging a rope. It came up out of the ground the way a voice comes up out of a crowd, and it arrived, and it was the same note for about four seconds and then it was gone.

There was nobody within about forty yards of that post. He looked for about nine seconds, which is a long time on a road, and there was a woman with a barrow going away up the hill and a dog and a hedge and the stream and no rope and no hand on anything.

Nobody had struck it.

---

He went and stood under it, which is a thing a bellmaker does and cannot be talked out of.

The bell was bronze. It was not one of his and it was not a Crown carrier, and he knows the difference the way a man knows the difference between a kettle and a pan. It had a plain crown, a sound bow, and about four inches of space under the mouth, and the mouth was clean, and there was wax in it — old wax, grey, three winters at least, in a ring round the inner lip, and **that is what a bell looks like when a person has stood under it and looked at it and touched it, and not recently.**

And there was a board under it about four feet by three, and there were nine sheets on the board, and eight of them were Crown sheets with a district heading and a number at the top of them, and the ninth was a handbill about a horse.

The bell had a bracket on the back of it that a bell does not have if it was made to be struck. **A bracket, and a socket, and a wire going down the inside of the post and into the ground, and that is not a striker's gear. That is gear for something that comes from a distance.**

---

He did not ring it. He did not touch it beyond the wax, and he wrote that down afterwards and it is a rule he set for himself on the road and not a law.

What he did instead was sit down on the low wall at the crossing with the day-book out and write three things, in the order he actually thought of them, which is not the order he would have chosen.

> *The eighty-seventh. A bell on a post above a notice-board at a crossing, eleven miles off nothing I have written down yet, and it rang at about the sixth hour and there was nobody within about forty yards of it and nothing to strike it with.*
>
> *I have been writing in this book for six weeks that a document is what a person is read by. I have it in the second column of the fortieth and a clerk in Miremarket said it to me in nine seconds and I carried it eleven days and made it into a key and it was not a key.*
>
> *Here is the other half of it and I have not got it before this morning. **A sound is what a person is read by, and a sound is cheaper than a sheet, and it goes further, and the sheet is only there to say afterwards what the sound was.** And I have been reading the sheet.*

He read the sheet afterwards. That is what the board is for.

The top sheet on the board said, in the flat hand of a printed form with a district heading:

> *COURT DATE. Public. To be held at the hall in Wendle on the day named below. All persons whose names are entered in the district's list for this hundred are summoned to attend at the second hour of the day and to remain until the officer is done.*
>
> *By order. District of Wendle and Ashmoor. [number]*

The date at the bottom was the eleventh day of the month.

**The date at the bottom was not the day the bell rang.** It was four days off. He stood under the bell and worked that out twice, because it is the sort of thing a man wants to be wrong about.

The bell had rung today. The sheet was for the eleventh. The bell had not come to tell anybody what day it was. **It had come to tell them there was going to be a day, so that a household could not say on the ninth that it did not know, and a household that did not know would have to come and find out, and a household that comes and finds out has been in the room.**

He wrote that down and then he took a second look at it, because he did not trust it, and the second look took about two minutes and the second look is what he put in the book.

> *Or I have this the wrong way round, and the bell rings the day before because a man with a book cannot get to about two hundred doors in a day and cannot be in two villages in a day either, and a bell costs nothing to send. I do not know which of those two it is and both of them are the Office and not the bell, and I have got two explanations and one bell and I am not going to write down the one I like best.*

---

Tamsin Rook had been standing about ten feet off his left shoulder since the bell rang and she had said nothing at all about it, which was her habit with a thing she had not worked out, and he had not asked her, and it went in the book that way because it went in the book every way.

She said, at about the seventh hour, walking:

“**That was not a keeper's bell.**”

“No.”

“**And it is not a plain one either, and I know it is not a plain one because I have carried a plain one for eleven years and a plain one is a thing you decide to hit. That thing was hit by somebody about eleven miles off and neither of us was told when, and I have not been on a road in eleven years where I could not tell you who struck a bell.**”

“No,” said Tomas Vale. “And there is no box for it on any form I have ever seen. **It is not a keeper's bell and it is not a plain bell and it is not unbound, because an unbound bell is the first of those two and this is a third thing, and I do not know what to call it and I am not going to coin a word for it in a book on a road.**”

“**Do not.**”

“Why.”

“**Because a word is a thing you can carry and you have got about nine of them already that turned out to be shelves.**”

---

He wrote the last entry of the eighty-seventh at about the ninth hour of the evening, sitting on a wall at a place where the road crosses a stream, with the frame in its cradle four feet off and the bolts made fast, and he filled the first and third columns and left the second and the fourth as they were.

> *The eighty-seventh, and here is the whole of the day and I am not going to improve it:*
>
> *A bell on a post above a notice-board, rung with nobody near it, and the sheet under it was dated four days forward, and it was a court date, and about two hundred people in a village four miles up this road have to be in a room on a day that a sound in the air has already told them is coming.*
>
> ***And the Office has not sent a man to do that. The Office has sent a wire, and a bracket, and a sheet, and a date, and the wire is in the ground and nobody has to be standing anywhere for it to work, and that is the whole of the thing and it took me about nine seconds under that bell and I have been carrying two questions for fifty-five days and I have got nearer this one than I have got nearer anything.***
>
> *Which office is it. **I still do not have the name of it. I have a district and a number and a bell on a post and a sheet with a court date on it, and a name is not a district, and a district is not an office, and there is a woman at a water bell in a village two counties back whose name is on a schedule and nobody has told her, and a number is a filing reference and not a man, and I am not going to write a paragraph about any of it because a paragraph is a thing I would be able to carry.***
>
> *Third column, ruled apart, and I am merging nothing: **the sound came first and the sheet is what the sound was for.** A man with a book cannot get to about two hundred doors in a day. A bell can. And I have been reading the sheet for six weeks and I have never once asked what carried it.*
>
> *All five clauses stand. **Nothing was rung.** The frame is four feet off me with four bolts made fast in it and no clapper and I have not been inside the cradle. The bell in the sack has not been touched since I put it in and it is not certified for anything. The bar on my own bell is in another county and my own bell is in a sealed yard in Kells and I am not going to it. My thirteen documents are thirteen.*
