# Chapter 0120 — If There Were Three Of Me

It was the fourth hour of the hundred and sixth day of the month and Tomas Vale was sitting on a step at the foot of a stair on the top terrace of Sennet with a barrow against the rail and a tarpaulin over a cradle on the barrow, and he was eleven days into a city of about nine thousand people, and he had a hearing behind him, and he was doing the one thing he had come here to do, which was to find out what a city of about nine thousand bells sounds like when it is asked a question instead of given a notice.

**He wrote the question down at the fourth hour in about nine seconds because it was the only question he had, and then he sat with it and found out in about two hours that he already knew the answer and did not like it.**

---

There is a rule about this and he had it by heart before he came to a capital, and it was written down for him in an arcade in a town he has left by a man who tapped a box with a fingernail and told him it would unsettle him to pay attention.

**Four parts.** Something a person can walk to and touch. A living keeper with a specific accepted duty, named, and not the whole town. The three answers. And a carrier who strikes it, how many times, and what it is not permitted to add.

**And the three answers are the hard part and they are not the metal.** A private handbell may carry one answer. A public signal in a place needs three, and the three have to be three people who each understand the claim and each may say yes, no, later, or this much and not that, and each is recorded in their own words.

**And the rule that does the work is four inches long and it is this: you set the claim down once, and you read the same words to each of them in the same order, and if you improve the words for the second one because the first one frightened you, you are not collecting answers, you are arranging agreement.**

He had not counted anything into iron since the fortieth day of this month. Seventy-three days. A number he could recite and could not use.

---

**And here is what he had on a step in Sennet at about the fifth hour, and it took him about nine minutes to write it out flat and about nine seconds to understand it.**

**One.** A plain carrier in a sack against the cradle, fourteen pounds two ounces, no wax, no dressed groove, a caster's mark and a day, bought at Bramden for one pound fourteen shillings and rung four times in its life and not once by him today. **It is a real bell and he could put it on a leather at about the sixth hour in that yard and strike it and about nine hundred people in this city would hear it.**

**Two.** A frame in a reinforced padded cradle with four retaining bolts made fast, a coupling cut nearly through and a thin copper jumper soldered across it, a child-sized wax handprint in the crown and a warped lip, **and no clapper, and a receipt in five clauses that says in the first of them that no person shall furnish one. It cannot be rung. It is the only bell in this kingdom he cannot have and it is in the open thirty feet off him and has not been in a building since the thirty-third.**

**Three.** A keeper. He had none. He had no standing in any county, and the fifth clause of his own receipt says in terms that the holder has no standing to speak for any household in Kells or in any ward, and he asked a clerk to write that clause in a watch house on the thirty-third because he was right.

**Four.** A carrier. He was the carrier, and a carrier who is also the only person in the yard who has a reason to be in the yard is a carrier who cannot be counted, because **a bell that one man put there and one man struck is a man talking, and the whole of the difference between that and a warning is that anybody can walk away from it.**

**And three answers.**

He had one.

---

**And at about the fifth hour a carter came up the Wharf Road with three horses and stopped at the bottom of the stair because a barrow is in the way of a cart, and Tomas Vale asked him to carry a thing up the four flights.**

He had it worked out in his head before he opened his mouth and it was a frame, and it did not have to be this frame, and he knew the objection to every word of it before he asked.

The carter looked up the hill, at about seven hundred feet of it, the same way he had looked up it on the morning of the ninety-seventh when a stranger with a barrow asked him something he had not explained yet.

“**No. Not for nothing.**”

“**You have not asked what the thing is.**”

“**No. And I am not going to, and you have not told me and I have not asked, and that is how a thing like this goes wrong and not the other way round.**”

He put the reins over the near horse's neck and did not get down.

“**I have nine loads a day and a boy on the second cart and I have carried other men's things up those steps for about nine years and I have never once carried a thing up them that nobody could account for afterwards.**”

“**There is nothing on it.**”

“**You do not know that.**”

He looked at the barrow, and at the tarpaulin, and he did not look under it and he was not going to.

“**And here is the whole of it, and it is the same as a man who mends his own wire and I have had that argument with a foreman twice this month. If I put a thing down in a yard in this city that I was not told to put down, and there is no form on which anybody wrote down that I was told, then I have made an entry in somebody's day that only I know about, and the only way that ends is somebody stands in front of me with a reason and asks me about it. **And I have three horses and a boy on the second cart and I have not got a son to put in that gap, and I would like you to go and find a man with a form and I will carry it up four flights this afternoon.**”

**And Tomas Vale did not argue, and did not thank him, and did not say that he understood, and stood there while three horses went up the Wharf Road, and wrote down at about the fifth hour that he had asked a man who was right to carry a thing and been told no in the time it took him to look at the barrow and then up at the four flights and then at him, and that the man had not asked what the thing was, and that the reason was the reason of a man who has nine loads and a boy on a cart and no form.**

**That clause has now done four jobs and it is going to do a fifth one about four days off this city and it will not be mine.**

---

**And at about the fifth hour he went into a yard at the foot of the stair and asked a man for a true sentence, and he asked him on purpose, and he knew what he was doing when he did it.**

Ormond Bly had a gate and a bell on it about the size of a soup plate and a yard of packing cases and nine years. **And he had told a stranger on the hundred and third, unasked, that he had watched him standing at a door, and that a woman four hundred yards off had watched it too, and neither of them had asked him for anything since, and a man who keeps a gate for nine years is not going to be the one who offers twice.**

“**I am not going to give you a sentence for nothing.**”

“**I am not asking you for nothing.**”

“**Then you are asking me for something and it is the same, and I do not know yet what it is, and you are asking me anyway, so you may as well say the whole of it.**”

Tomas said the whole of it in about nine seconds. He said it flatly, standing at the rail with his hands empty, and he did not make a speech about it and he did not say what he wanted the sentence for, because he did not know.

And Bly gave him one, and it took him about as long as it takes a man to look at a gate he has looked at every day for nine years, and it was true.

“**I have a gate and a bell on it and I strike it myself and I have done it about four thousand times in nine years and about nine hundred of them were for a cart, and I have never once struck it for a thing I had not seen with my own eyes. That is the whole of what a bell is and I did not learn it in a book. I learned it in a yard.**”

“**That is good.**”

“**It is no use to you at all.**”

“**I know.**”

“**Then I will tell you why, and it is because you asked me.**”

He put a case down.

“**I have said that sentence about four hundred times in nine years, at this rail, to about nine hundred men, and it has never once been in a book. The day a man comes to this rail and asks me for a sentence, that sentence has a man in it, and the man is you, and the next person who asks me whether I have ever had a belief put in my mouth is going to be somebody with a column, and I am not going to be the first entry in it.**”

He picked the case up again.

“**I have said the true thing. It is mine. It is going in a yard and not in a book. If you carry it up a hill it is a thing I said to a stranger, and in about four years there will be a man who says I said it because a bellmaker with a barrow asked me, and that man will be right, and I will not be able to say one word against it.**”

---

**And he wrote it down in his own book that evening, flat, in four inches: he asked, and he got a sentence, and the sentence was true, and the asking was the whole of the damage.**

**And then he did the other half of it, which is the half he has been getting right for about eleven weeks, and it is four inches long, and it took him about nine minutes on a step. He went back over every true sentence anybody has said to him in this city in eleven days and put three of them under each other.**

**A bell is a man and an hour and a reason.** A wharf on the flat, the ninety-seventh, out of a man's mouth on the tenth trip, about his own four bells, asked by nobody.

**I have a district's business in this room and no column anywhere in this kingdom for the rest.** A counter four flights up, the hundredth, out of a man behind a counter about his own book, asked by nobody.

**A bell is what I have learned in a yard and it is not in a book.** A yard at the foot of this stair, this morning, at the fifth hour, out of a man with a gate and a bell on it, asked by me.

**Three men, three rooms, three days, three different things, and not one of them is about the other two, and about nine hundred people in this city would each have believed the one that suited them, and between them they say nothing at all.**

---

**And at about the sixth hour he went up the four flights and stood in a yard on the top terrace and about two hundred people came out of doors and stood about and did not make a noise, and he stood in the middle of them and was part of it, and a man with a book turned a plate over on a board at the rail and struck a bell about as big as a cartwheel four times with a rope.**

**There was nothing on the plate.** He could see that from nine feet. The district had not sent a sheet and there was nothing new to read and about forty people who came for the reading stood about for about a minute and went in again, and the man with the book struck it four times anyway.

The man was called **Marek Douse** and he had stood at that rail for six years and he had a book of his own with no office and a page for every day of the six, and he was not a servant of anything and he was paid by the quarter and he was not doing it because he was told to.

He put the rope down and said, to nobody, in the ordinary voice, in the open, in a yard with about two hundred people in it:

“**Six years on that frame and four strokes and it has meant one thing since the frame was new: there is a sheet on the board. **I have never known it to mean anything else. I do not know whether I am to strike it today. Nobody has told me not to.**”

Somebody at the back said, “**There was nothing on it.**”

“**There was nothing on it,**” Douse agreed. “**And I struck it four times, and I have done that on about nine days out of a hundred in six years, and about two hundred of you came out of doors for the four, and I do not know what to tell you, because I have never asked anybody and there is nobody above me to ask and if there were I should not know what to ask him.**”

He picked the rope up.

“**That is the whole of what I know about that bell and I have known it six years and I have never said it out loud in this yard, and I have said it now because there is a man standing in the middle of the yard with a barrow who has not asked me a single question, and that is the only reason in this kingdom there is going to be.**”

---

**And at about the seventh hour a man sweeping the yard came across it with a broom and stopped about nine feet off him, and Tomas Vale did not say a word, and neither did the man with the broom for about nine seconds, and then the man said it, and he did not say it to Tomas either, he said it to the boards.**

His name was **Piet Sarn** and he had swept that yard for eleven years and had never been in the upper room in his life and had never wanted to and did not think about it.

“**I sweep this yard and I have swept it eleven years and I could not tell you what is in this building. I know the light goes out of one window at about the sixth hour. That is nine hundred rooms and I know the light going out of one of them, and that is all I know, and I have never said it out loud and I have never had the occasion, and I have just had the occasion, because you have been standing in this yard for an hour and you have not asked me one thing and neither has anybody else, and I want that in a place where somebody has written it down.**”

“**There is a man on a step who writes things down.**”

“**Then he can have this and he can do what he likes with it and it is not about him.**”

He swept on.

---

**And Tomas Vale stood in that yard and worked out the whole of it in about four seconds, and it is the finding of the hundred and sixth day, and he has not been able to put it anywhere since and he is not going to try.**

> ***Three people said one true thing each today, in the open, in their own names, in a yard, and two of them said it because I asked and one of them said it because nobody did, and I was nine feet off all three of them.***
>
> ***And they were about three different things. And if they had been about the same thing, in three places, on the same day, that would have been a warning, and about nine hundred people in this city would have heard one thing three times instead of one man telling them what to think about it. That is the whole of what a bell is for and I have known it since the eleventh day of this month and I have not used it since the fortieth.***
>
> ***And I have got one man. That is the arithmetic and it took a hundred and six days and a carter with three horses and a yard and a gate. A man can be one of the three answers. A man cannot be two of them, and the second one he is, the first one stops being an answer and becomes a man saying the same thing twice, and a thing said twice by one man is a thing said once louder.***
>
> ***And there is no form in this kingdom on which a man may go to three men and say the same words to each of them and have it be three answers, and if there were, the words would be mine and all three of them would be one, and I should be doing the thing I have refused to do in four counties and a city, which is arranging agreement and calling it a count.***

---

**And he took the sack out from against the cradle at about the eighth hour and unrolled it on the step and put his hand flat on the crown of it and left it there.**

It was about four feet high and it had a caster's mark on it and a day, and no wax and no dressed groove, and it has been rung four times in its life and the last of the four was the seventy-seventh, twenty-nine days, and he does not know where that one went and he is not going to be told and he is not going to look.

**It is the most ordinary object in this kingdom and it is the only thing he has that could put a sound into about nine hundred people at once, and it has nothing in it at all, and the reason he could not do it with it is a craft rule and not a feeling.**

> *A warning is a thing a person says about a thing they have looked at, with a keeper, and a bar, and their own fault in it, and it names what it does not know, and it tells nobody what to decide. **I have made that shape four times in my life and I have rung it twice in a market and both times a stranger rang it and not me, and a bell that a man rings for himself is a man in a yard with a bell.**
>
> *And if I strike this one tonight in that yard at the sixth hour, about two hundred people will hear four strokes and come out of their doors out of nine years of habit, and they will be out of doors in about nine minutes, because a bell they have heard every day of their lives is not a signal to them, **it is the hour.** And I have no keeper and I have no bar and I have no third and fourth answer and the two people in that yard who said a true thing today were not saying it about my business and were not asked to and would not have said it if they had been. **A thing said three times that is not the same thing is three things, and I cannot make them the same thing without lying to two of them, and I have spent seventy-three days learning to ask a person to refuse me and I am not going to go back and arrange a consent in a yard and call it iron.***

---

**And at about the ninth hour of the evening he was still on that step at the foot of the top stair with the tarpaulin over the cradle four feet off him, the four bolts made fast where they were made fast at the fourth hour, and the sack rolled and against the barrow again, and Tamsin Rook was ten feet off his left shoulder with a bell in her bag that she had rung once since Kells, in a market of about nine hundred people, on her own account, for a reason that had not been him. She had been four hundred yards off on the middle terrace on the hundred and third, watching a man not go through a door, and she had come up off it on the fourth of this month and not said why, and he had not asked, and it was the fourth day of not asking.**

**He did not put a question on the slate. He wrote that down once, and then he wrote under it that he was not going to write it down again, because she had said in her own book on the hundred and second that the third time she wrote it she would be counting, and a woman who counts is a woman waiting to be owed, and he is not owed and neither is she.**

**The count is eight. A thing that is not done is not a thing that has been done.**

She had said about nine sentences to him in eleven days and had not been asked for anything by anybody in a city of nine thousand people, and tonight she said one to him, and she said it four inches long at ten feet in the ordinary voice, and it was his own sentence and she had it out of his own mouth about four hours before and she gave it back to him in the words she had heard.

He did not improve it.

**“**You have got a barrow and a bell and no keeper and no second person, and you have known that since you were nine feet off a door, and you have spent a hundred and six days finding out that the reason you cannot do it is arithmetic and not the Office.**”

“**Yes.**”

“**Then you are going to have to be three men,**” she said, “**and there is one of you, and I am not going to be the second, and if anybody in this city asks me which one I am I am going to tell them nothing at all, and you knew every word of that before you asked, because you did not ask.**”

“**I did not ask.**”

“**You did not ask,**” she said, “**and you are not going to.**”

---

**And he has not been able to put the rest of it anywhere, and here it is flat, and the two halves of it do not meet and he is not going to pretend that they do.**

**On the hundred and third of this month a man in a room with a lamp in it entered one person's answer into a column in about four seconds, in the correct form, out of the correct column, and that answer came out of the column as the answer of the whole matter, and there is a file in a room four streets below this step that says in the office's own hand that the person named produced nothing, and it is four hundred words long and it is the only document about Tomas Vale that exists in the world.**

**And on the same city, three days later, three people said three true things in three yards, and about two hundred people stood in one of them and let a bell ring four times for a sheet that was not on the board, and none of the three was asked to be about his business, and none of the three is an answer to anything, and none of the three can be joined to the other two, and he is not going to join them.**

> ***Those are the two halves and they do not meet and I have been looking for a place where they do for a hundred and six days.***
>
> ***The Office can make an answer for a person. It cannot stop three people answering for themselves. **And neither of those is a victory, and I am not going to be the man who says the second one is the answer, because I have already said once this month that a thing I liked was the answer and I was wrong about it in a loft on the ninety-ninth with nine tons of grain on the floor, and I am not doing it twice.***
>
> ***What I have got is three people and a yard and about nine hundred bells in this city, and not one of them has been rung tonight for anything anybody in it chose. **If there were three of me this city could be told something today.** And there is one of me, and one of me is what a hundred and six days buys, and it is the best thing I have ever had in my hands and I have written it down and I am not going to do a single thing with it tonight.***

He wrote the last entry of the hundred and sixth at about the ninth hour of the evening on a step at the foot of the top terrace, with the frame in its cradle four feet off him and the bolts made fast, and he filled the first, second and third columns and left the fourth as it was.

> *The hundred and sixth. **I have found out what a city of about nine hundred bells sounds like when it is asked a question, and the answer is that it does not, and the reason is arithmetic, and I have got to the bottom of the road in both directions inside four days of each other and neither one has a door at the end of it.***
>
> *Second column. **Three people said one true thing each in the open in their own names in a yard, and two of them because I asked and one of them because I did not, and about two hundred people stood in a yard and let a bell ring four times for a sheet that was not on the board and went back in, and a man with a book and no office and six years on that frame said out loud that he does not know whether he is to strike it and that there is nobody above him to ask. **He is right and he has been right for six years and nobody has ever asked him and I have not either, and I did not arrange one word of it and I have written down that I asked two of the three and that the asking is the whole of the damage.***
>
> *And a man sweeping a yard told me that he knows nine hundred rooms by the light going out of one window at the sixth hour, and said it to the boards, and asked me for it to go in a place where somebody has written it down. **He is not a hero and he is not owed anything and he is not going to know by tomorrow that I wrote it, and the place is a book in a coat that nobody has read in a hundred and six days, and that is the correct arrangement and I have now got four of them in a city of nine thousand people in about six days and I have not asked one of them for anything and one of them has asked me for a piece of paper and I have given him my own book and not a copy.***
>
> *Third column, ruled apart, and the thing I cannot get out of my head: **Three sentences about one matter, in three yards, on one day, would have been a warning, and this city would have had it whether anybody rang anything or not, and I would have been the man who put it there.** That is the whole of what a bell is for and I have had the rule since the eleventh of this month and I have not used it since the fortieth and I counted nothing into iron for seventy-three days. **And I have got one man. A man can be one of the three answers and cannot be two of them, and the second one he is, the first one stops being an answer.** And there is no form in this kingdom on which a man may take the same words to three men and call the result a count, and if there were, all three sentences would be mine and none of them would be anybody's answer, and that is the thing I have refused in four counties and a city and I am not going to do it in a yard. **If there were three of me.***
>
> *And a carter with three horses and a boy on the second cart said no to me at the bottom of this stair, and the length of it was however long it took a man with nine loads a day to look at a barrow and then up at four flights and then back at the man asking, at the fifth hour, and he did not ask what the thing was and was right, and the reason he gave is the reason of a man who mends his own wire, and a mend that is in nobody's book is a fault nobody can see and a fault nobody can see is a fault he is asked about some morning by somebody with a reason. **I asked a man who was right to carry a thing and he told me no and I did not thank him and I am not going to be told it was worth it.** That clause of my own receipt has now done four jobs and it will do a fifth one inside a month and it will not be mine.*
>
> *All five clauses stand. **Nothing rung, by me, in this city, in eleven days. **Four bolts, the frame out of doors under a tarpaulin at the foot of this stair, not in a building since the thirty-third, and the cradle is a building-sized thing for a bell and there is no clause that will let me put it in a room. I had the plain carrier out of the sack and my hand on its crown at about the eighth hour and I put it back, and I do not know where either of my two public ringings went and I am not going to find out. My own bell is in a sealed yard in Kells and I am not going to it. **Thirteen documents in a coat, and a record of a hearing that is the public's and not mine and that I stood at a rail and read in about eleven minutes and took nothing from, and those two figures are correct and they are two different objects, and a letter from Kells is not a fourteenth because it is a letter to a man.***
>
> *And I read the store list a leaf at a time on two boards said I should, and I did not take either leaf, and the list said nothing about the third question and nothing about what ninety words are for, and I am not going to write to a woman about it, because she did not ask me and because a leaf pinned at an angle under a drawing-pin is a thing I have decided not to take and it is the same decision twice, and I wrote that down on the ninety-first and it has not changed.*
>
> *And the five dates in a drawer in a county I have left are where they were on the ninety-second and where they are now, and I have not touched them in a hundred and six days and I am not going to touch them this month, and nobody in this city has asked me about them and nobody is going to, and I have not explained them to myself and I am not going to.*
>
> *Which office is it. **It is a room with a lamp in it and a column, and a yard with a bell on a frame and about two hundred people in it who come out for the hour, and a sweep-man who knows the light going out of one window, and three yards and three true things that are about three different things, and a barrow and a plain bell and twenty-nine days and a route map I have not got yet and have not seen.** It can make an answer for a person in about four seconds out of a column. **It cannot stop three people answering for themselves and I am not going to call that a victory and I am not going to call the other half a wall.** A format has no door and a city has about nine hundred doors and every one of them is a man.*
