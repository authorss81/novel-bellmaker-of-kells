# Chapter 0002 — The West Road Warning

By late morning, the rain had lifted enough for the west road to open, but the mud remained deep enough to swallow the wheels of the first grain cart. Tomas walked down from the Bellhouse with a leather satchel, a length of red thread, and the paper of measurements. He had left the fixed Crown survey carrier locked in the casting room. The door was iron-bound and the key was in his pocket. That did not make the carrier safe, but it made the next question easier to ask: what had the people near the route actually heard?

The Low Ward river landing was crowded despite the weather. Barges had tied up beneath the stone quay, and the fish sellers had covered their baskets with oiled canvas. A crowd had gathered near the public notice board, not because a notice had been posted there, but because someone in the north-gate watch had said that the Bellhouse had found a dangerous bell.

Tomas saw Halden Crake before Halden saw him.

The deputy reeve stood beneath the awning of the Anchor and Sickle with his cloak folded over one arm. He was broad through the shoulders and careful with his feet, a man who understood that crowds had weight. His clerk stood beside him with a rolled notice under one arm. When Halden saw Tomas, his expression closed.

“Vale,” he said. “Keep that carrier away from the public.”

“I have.”

“Your route is three streets away.”

“Bellwire runs under the road. I’m following the route, not showing the bell.”

“Your route is not a warrant.”

“It is a repair concern.”

“It is a Crown concern now.”

The clerk lifted the rolled notice but did not unroll it. Tomas caught a glimpse of red wax and the broad black letters of the Crown seal. His chest tightened. He kept his attention on Halden.

“Where did the sound go?” he asked.

“Where did you hear it?”

“At the Bellhouse.”

“That is not a place, Vale. It is a building you control.”

“Where did your watch hear it?”

The clerk looked at the ground. One of the fish sellers answered for him.

“At the gate,” she said. “One long note after the hour, with a rough pulse beneath it. It came through the wall.”

Another voice called from the quay. “I heard a bell under the water.”

A boy near the landing steps said, “It said the children.”

The crowd shifted. Tomas felt the sentence move through it before he could stop it. He raised his hand, palm outward, but did not touch anyone.

“Who else heard it?” he asked.

The fish seller pointed to a gray-haired man with a ferry pole. “He was on the west bank. He says it was the flood bell.”

The ferryman shook his head. “I heard iron. Like a chain pulled under the road.”

A woman holding a bucket said, “I heard the market bell. The one that closes the lane when the carts are late.”

Their answers did not agree. Tomas felt a quick, unreasonable annoyance. If they had all heard the same sentence, he could have written a warning with a clean edge. Instead he had a collection of tones, fragments, and fears.

Halden stepped onto the quay. “Enough. The sound is being treated as a public notice without authority.”

“It was not a notice,” Tomas said. “It was a damaged carrier that may be sounding through a live route.”

“You are the only person who says that.”

“Because you are the only person refusing to ask how the route works.”

A murmur moved through the crowd. The clerk shifted his roll. Halden’s hand went to it, then stopped.

“Come with me to the landing office,” Halden said. “I will give you a route map. You will tell me what you know.”

“And if I do not come?”

“I will have the watch secure the carrier and close this road until the Crown surveyor arrives.”

The threat was ordinary. That did not make it weak. Tomas looked at the water, the carts, and the people who had already begun to gather their children. He could refuse, but refusal would turn his uncertainty into a public act without giving anyone new information.

“I will come,” he said. “But I will ask my questions where the witnesses can hear them.”

Halden’s expression hardened. “You are not conducting a hearing.”

“I am asking what they heard.”

“At the watch house. With the clerk present.”

“Fine.”

The landing office was a damp room above the ferry sheds. Its window faced the river, and its shelves held ledgers swollen with damp. Halden placed a map on the table. The west road was marked in brown ink, with the old route shown as a dotted line. A newer Crown line ran beneath it in black.

Tomas noticed where the two lines met. The junction sat just beyond the river landing, below the road’s western rise. A small square marked the north gate. Another mark, newer, sat beside the Low Ward.

“Show me the carrier’s line,” he said.

Halden pointed with a wooden ruler. “Crown maintenance line. It runs under the north-gate wall, along the old foundation, then branches toward the market. Your Bellhouse sits over an old service cut.”

“It is connected to the west road.”

“It is connected to a route that passes the west road.”

“That is not the same.”

“It is the same to anyone who must cross it safely.”

Tomas leaned over the map. The route had been drawn in a straight segment beneath the Bellhouse, then stopped at a blank patch near the landing. A repair mark had been scratched out. The red thread he had brought lay across the paper. He placed one end at the Bellhouse and one at the junction, measuring the line of the old cut by sight.

“The carrier’s line is not shown here,” he said.

“No.”

“Who removed it?”

“The Crown.”

“Which Crown officer?”

“The office that maintains the route.”

“That is not a person.”

“It is the department that maintains the route.”

Tomas looked at him. Halden’s anger was not foolish. A damaged route could take down a market day. A false warning could empty the river landing. A true warning could do the same faster. Halden wanted a simple answer before the town invented its own.

Tomas took out his paper. “I need three witnesses separately. No leading questions.”

Halden’s eyes narrowed. “You intend to make a public signal.”

“No. I intend to find out whether I am hearing a route, a rumor, or a bell that has been driven by someone else.”

“Your carrier has no clapper.”

“Then the sound is not ordinary.”

“Exactly.”

“Which means someone connected a physical line to it.”

“Or someone removed a safeguard.”

The two words hung between them. Halden picked up the rolled notice and broke its seal. He did not read the whole document. He slid it across the table.

“Look at the date,” he said. “The inspection was already filed before your bell made its noise.”

Tomas read the heading and the route number. The rest of the order remained folded beneath Halden’s thumb, a neat black line visible where the paper creased. He could not make out the paragraphs that mattered without being made to receive them as an official explanation.

Crown Renewal Inspection — Low Ward — dawn, fifth day from posting. Route L-4.

“Where is the surveyor?” he asked. “And what does the order permit?”

“I knew an inspection was scheduled. I did not know a carrier had been stripped.”

“What does it permit?”

“The clerk can answer that at the public court. I will not turn a sealed order into a summary that becomes your accusation.”

Tomas looked at the folded lower half of the notice. “It says dawn.”

“It does.”

“And the carrier rang before the inspection.”

“Apparently.”

“That does not make the order safe.”

“No.” Halden’s voice softened, not with agreement but with fatigue. “It makes it explainable. There is a difference.”

Tomas copied the date and route number onto his own paper. He did not take the notice. “Where is the surveyor?”

“Delayed on the north road.”

“Delayed by what?”

“Crown business.”

“That is not an answer.”

“I have men at the gate. I will find out.”

Halden’s clerk looked toward the door as if expecting someone to enter. Tomas folded his paper and tucked it into his coat.

“Three witnesses,” he said. “Tonight or tomorrow morning, before the market fills. I will ask what they heard, not what they think it means.”

“I will not let you turn a damaged bell into a public order.”

“I will not use the carrier to order anyone.”

“You can say that after you ring it.”

“I will not ring it.”

“Then what do you call the warning?”

Tomas looked toward the window. A woman in the Low Ward had lifted a child onto her hip and was watching the office door. Another family was moving down the quay with a blanket wrapped around a small bundle. The danger, if it was danger, had already entered ordinary life.

“I call it a warning until the people it reaches can decide what it is,” he said.

Halden studied him. “You think that is enough.”

“No. I know what it costs to say it.”

Tomas left the office before Halden could answer. He found the three witnesses in different places: the fish seller beside her covered baskets, the gray-haired ferryman at the west bank, and a boy who repaired straps near the market lane. He asked each the same question—what did you hear?—and did not mention the inspection until the question was answered.

The fish seller had heard a low note and a pause. The ferryman had heard a pulse under the stones. The boy had heard a child’s voice say, “Before dawn.”

The boy’s answer made Tomas’s stomach turn, but he wrote it down without interpretation. The child had also said the name “Vale” as if addressing him. Tomas asked whether that had been clear. The boy shrugged and said the wind had taken most of it.

Before leaving the landing, Tomas stood beneath the notice board where the fish sellers and carters could see him. He did not touch the paper or the bellwire. He said in a plain voice that a damaged Crown carrier had sounded, that a dawn inspection had been filed for the Low Ward, and that he had heard people describe a warning about children. He named the route, the uncertainty, and the fact that he could not identify the person who had accepted responsibility for the order. Then he stepped down from the stones.

No one cheered. One woman asked whether she should move her children. Tomas said he could not answer that for her. Another man said the Crown had probably made the notice to frighten them. Tomas said that was possible. The crowd remained uncertain, but the uncertainty no longer belonged to one frightened witness.

By evening, the west road was quiet. The market carts had gone, and the notice about the inspection remained pinned to the public board. Some people had read it. More had heard about the bell.

Tomas returned to the Bellhouse with three accounts that agreed on little. He laid them beside the route measurements and the notice date. The carrier had sounded. A dawn inspection existed. The road connected. None of those facts, by themselves, named who intended harm or what the danger would become.

He copied each account onto a separate slip and wrote the time beside it. The slips were not answers. They were proof that three people had been asked what they heard without being handed Tomas’s conclusion. He put them in the ledger where Oren could witness them.

A runner from the north gate met him at the door. The boy was out of breath and held a scrap of paper.

“A courier,” he said. “Says there’s a handbell missing from the relay lockbox. A small one. The watch is searching houses for it.”

Tomas took the scrap. The courier’s name was written across it in a sharp, impatient hand.

Tamsin Rook.

He looked toward the locked casting room, then at the paper in his hand. The carrier was not the only signal in Kells that had been altered by someone who believed a route could be made to speak without asking.
