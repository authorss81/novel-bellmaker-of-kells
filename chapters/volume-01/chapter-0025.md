# Chapter 0025 — The Order Was Entered

The market court board had a crowd around it by noon, and Tamsin could not follow a word of it.

That was the part nobody outside her own head understood. She was not deaf in the way the word means. She could hear a bell, a man shouting, a cart on stone, rain coming. What she could not do was follow four people talking in an open street, and a ward board with thirty people round it on a bright day was four people talking in an open street with the whole of the argument happening below the level where anybody who talks like that says the words properly.

So she stood where she could see the faces and she did what she had been doing for four days, which was to read the paper.

The pinned copy of the schedule was sun-bleached and curling at the corners, and it had forty-one household lines on it in a watch-house copyist’s fair hand, and it had been pinned through a drawing pin by somebody who had wanted it to survive a night of rain.

The market court sat at the noon hour with the two Crown notices up from the night before and about forty people in it, and the first thing on the floor was not the schedule at all.

It was Deputy Crake reading aloud a refusal, because Captain Pike had insisted for two days that a refusal like his be said in the open rather than in a lane, and the deputy had said *all right* in a tone that meant he had thought about it and decided it was the cheaper of two expensive things.

*I decline the district appointment offered to me on the eleventh day, a watching at a river crossing with a stone bridge, four days’ ride. I decline it because I do not want it and because I want it. I want it, and that is the reason a captain of this watch should have no part in deciding it, and it is in my own hand and it is signed. — Oren Pike, Kells watch.*

Then Crake read his own answer in full, which was two sentences, of which the second was: *The district office will hear about Captain Pike by the end of the week, and I am sorry about it, and I am going to do it anyway.*

Tamsin could not follow a word of it in that room, and she did not have to. She could read Crake’s face, and Crake’s face was a man reading his own handwriting out loud and not liking the shape of any of it, and the watch captain’s was a man who had known every word in advance and had asked for them.

She read the heading of the pinned schedule twice, and the first field of each line, and the response column, and the office mark, and the dates.

Then she read the first field of the last line, and read it again, and went and found Ferrow.

“You read this out on the ninth evening,” she said. “In the watch house, at the long table, to the captain and to him. You read the heading and the count and the office number and the date, and you read the Fenn line twice, because I asked you to, and I read it a third time myself upside down and made you turn it round.”

“I read forty-one households.”

“You read forty-one households and then you stopped. There is a ruling at the foot of it. I do not think you could have read under that ruling if you had tried. Nobody reads under a ruling. I have been a reader of route lists for eleven years and I did not read under it either, and I did not know that was what I had been doing until this morning.”

Ferrow looked at the paper and not at her. “There is a ruling.”

“I can see a ruling. What is under the ruling?”

“Nothing,” Ferrow said. “There is nothing under the ruling. I read to the ruling because it is a table and a reader stops at the ruling, and if I had read under the ruling I would have been reading past the table, and a man in an office would have said that I had read something that was not on the sheet.”

“You have not looked under the ruling since.”

“I have looked under the ruling twice,” Ferrow said, “and it has not changed, and I am not going to put my hand on it in daylight, and I would be obliged if you would not ask me to, because the Crown man who wants the L-4 papers spoke to me yesterday for four minutes about nothing at all, and a man who talks to you for four minutes about nothing is a man who has written down what time you came into the room.”

Tamsin thanked him and left him alone, on purpose, because Ferrow had been helpful in public twice in eleven days and could not afford a third.

---

She went up to the watch house at the second hour with a slate and a stub of charcoal and no particular plan beyond the fact that she could not follow a conversation and could read a document perfectly, and that the last eight months of her working life had taught her that those two facts were most of a trade.

Oren had the file open on the long table. Jerm was at the end of it with a book. Tomas Vale was standing at the side of the table with his hands behind his back, in the attitude of a man who has been told he may read the six sheets and not take them, and is behaving exactly as he was told, and does it so carefully that it is possible to see the effort.

“Surveyor,” Oren said.

“Not today. I cannot hear you and I am not going to pretend.” Tamsin put the slate down flat. “I want to read four papers and I want to say one thing out loud at the end, and I am going to be slow. Captain, will you let me do it here rather than in the court where I cannot work.”

“Sit down, then,” Oren said, “and Jerm will read anything you want read twice.”

There were six sheets. Tamsin had seen three of them before, upside down, across a table, in a lamplit room, and she had asked for them to be turned round. She started with the fourth, because it was the one nobody had looked at since it was read out on the ninth evening.

The schedule.

She read the office mark in the response column of the first line, and then of the second, and then of the eleventh, and then of the fortieth, because she was not looking for a document, she was looking for a habit.

*OFF. 411.*

She wrote it on the slate, and put a tick beside it, and did not look up. Tomas, on the other side of the table, wrote four words in the back of his own day-book, which was the first thing he had written since the last of the previous evening and it was this: *fine pen. That is a summary page. A summary is made for somebody who was not in the room.*

“Second sheet,” she said. “The works order for the culvert.”

Oren gave it to her. The Crown road gang’s works order, signed by a foreman, dated the day after the ward was warned at the court, and Bevin Sarr’s eleven witnesses still able to say that a Crown officer had used the word *Crown* twice at the low turn.

She found the office mark in the corner where the routing goes, because Crown work orders always carry the office that paid for them, and it is the one thing on the page nobody reads.

*OFF. 411.*

She wrote it under the first one.

“Third sheet. The service ticket. The whole of it, including the line underneath the copy.”

Jerm read the ticket and then read the line, and the line was still there, in the smaller hand: *answered in office, L-4 ward roll, per local practice.*

The routing line at the foot of the ticket said OFF. 411.

Three ticks and three figures on a slate, and it was the fourth hour of the eleventh day by then, and Tomas Vale had not said a word and had begun, very slightly, to breathe in the way he did when he was frightened of what was on the bench.

“That is an office number,” Tamsin said, turning the slate round so Oren could see it. “On a ward response schedule entered before the ward was asked. On a Crown works order dated before any of this. On a maintenance ticket for a carrier that was rung on the first day. Written by three different hands, at three different times, in three different rooms, and it is the same four hundred and eleven in all three.”

“That is a district renewal desk,” Oren said slowly. “That is not a man. That is a desk with a docket number.”

“Then it is a desk, and a desk is made of people, and people do the work.” She put her thumb on the schedule. “I am not finished. Fourth sheet. The order.”

The L-4 inspection order was two pages and the town had seen the plainest parts of it for ten days. It was dated the day the notice went up on the board, three days before the carrier was delivered to the Vale Bellhouse. It was signed at the foot under a Crown seal and carried no name at all, which is the only part of it that has ever been read out in a public room, and that was the seal, on the ninth evening, in the watch house.

“Routing line,” Tamsin said.

Jerm turned the sheet. *OFF. 411.*

“Date?”

“Three days before the carrier rang,” Oren said.

“Date of the trailing line?”

Tamsin had not looked at it yet. She had been working down the first field of every line for a quarter of an hour, a house number at a time, and she had stopped at the fortieth without noticing that she had stopped, because a reader reads a table to the ruling and she had been doing the same thing the whole time and had been furious with herself for the last two hundredths of an hour.

“Look at the row under the ruling,” she said.

---

The row under the ruling was not a house.

There was a ruling at the foot of the forty-one lines, and under it there was one more row, and the first field of that row said **L-4** where every other line said a number.

The second field, which on every one of the forty-one held a count of persons, was empty. Not a zero. Empty. The ink never touched it.

The response column, which on every one of the forty-one held the office mark and, on some of them, a word, held the office mark.

And in the date column there was a date, and Tamsin read it twice and then made Jerm read it twice and then made him read the date at the foot of the inspection order, and then she did the subtraction on the slate in a hand that got worse as it went, which is how anybody who has spent a life reading marks knows that a person has found something they have not expected.

“Nine days before the carrier rang,” she said.

Nobody said anything.

“Read it out. Both dates, and then the difference, and say the difference out loud because I want a witness to the arithmetic and not to me.”

“The trailing line is dated nine days before the carrier was delivered,” Jerm said, reading it as he read everything, flat and in order. “The inspection order in this file is dated three days before the carrier was delivered.”

“Six days,” Tamsin said. “The answer to the order was entered six days before the order was written, and six days before there was an order in this town to answer.”

She put the charcoal down and sat back and did not say the next part for a while, because the next part was the part that had been sitting underneath the arithmetic the whole time, waiting for the arithmetic to be finished, and she wanted to be certain of it before she said it in a room with a watch captain and a bellmaker in it.

“Captain,” she said. “On the first day, in the hour before light, a bell in that workshop sounded about an inspection that had not happened. Everybody in this town has been carrying a piece of that around for ten days. The boy on the landing said it said *before dawn*. A man in the market crowd repeated it. And I have watched your ward say, over and over, that a bell is a thing that knows things before they happen.”

“Yes,” Oren said.

“Well. It does not.” She put her finger on the empty field. “It cannot, and it does not have to. A bell carries a claim that somebody has taken responsibility for making. That is the whole of the craft and Sabin Fell has been telling the same story to both of you for eleven days. It does not need to know what is going to happen. It needs a person to have taken on the duty of warning about it, and it will carry that duty for as long as the duty stands.”

“That is what Tomas has been saying since the second day,” Oren said.

“Then he has been saying it and neither of you has followed it, because you have all been waiting for a better reason.” Tamsin looked at the row with nothing in the second field. “There is no house on that line. There is no household, no count of persons, no person in a room, no answer given to anybody. What there is, is the route itself, written in the column that only a responding household is written in, with the office mark in it, and dated six days before there was an order to respond to.”

She tapped the date.

“So somebody in office four hundred and eleven accepted a duty, on the twenty-second day before, about a thing that had not been written down. They wrote it in the place where duties go, in the ordinary form, in the ordinary hand, in the ordinary week, the way forty-one other people in this ward were entered in the same column in the same week without being asked.” She looked up. “And the bell rang on the first day, and it carried that, because a duty accepted is a duty accepted. It was not prophecy. It was filing.”

The long table was extremely quiet. Jerm’s pen was on the paper and had not moved.

“Filing,” Tomas said.

“You have said the word to me before and I have watched you not hear it.” Tamsin picked up her slate. “There is a duty in a form. The form is in an office. Somebody in that office is answerable for the form. That is not a mystery, that is a *post*. And the bell did what it does with any accepted duty, which is carry it, without knowing and without asking, out to a place where it might be useful. It carried a Crown office’s acceptance and it sounded in a ward like a thing the ward had said.”

“It did not sound like an office,” Oren said. “It sounded like a child being taken.”

“Yes,” Tamsin said. “And that is the part I have been trying not to say since the sixth hour this morning, and I am going to say it now, because you have both been treating this as though a horror in a district town is smaller than three children in a wagon.

“Somebody laid wax over an older groove in the crown of my bell, in this town, on a night I did not see, so that one note of mine would travel further than the rule I set for it. I have known about it since the fourth day. I have called it an accident for eight months, and I have blamed myself for holding a bell I did not know the inside of, and I have been ashamed of a thief for half a year because I was easier to find than it was.”

She stopped, and Jerm wrote all of it down, and she made him read it back before she would go on.

“That is a technique,” she said. “Somebody wanted one note to go further than the rule allows, and did it to the metal, and the metal did exactly what it was told. And that technique — one thing made to carry further than the route it is allowed to travel on, so that a thing looks bigger than the thing that agreed to it — is in a filing office in the district town, in ink, on a form, with a column and a return cycle, and it has been there long enough that an office number is on three papers in three hands.” She set the slate down. “I have been carrying a Crown practice round my neck for eight months and calling it a crime. It is not a crime. It is *procedure*.”

---

Tomas had been standing in exactly the same place for eleven minutes, and now he moved, and what he did was the thing she had told him he would do and could not stop doing.

“There is a person,” he said. “In four hundred and eleven. There has to be. A desk is a place where a person sits. And that person sat down six days before they wrote that order and entered a duty about it in a form, and I want—”

“No.”

“—a name, and I know that is the sentence you are going to make me take back, and I am going to make the sentence anyway, so you can have the satisfaction of refusing it, and then I am going to want it again tomorrow—”

“No, you are not.” Tamsin stood up. She had to raise her voice, which she had not expected to do, and she noticed the smallness of the room and did not apologise for it. “I have got the arithmetic and I have got the fact and the fact is enough to be worth a year. I have not got a name and there is no name on that page, and if you go and invent one tonight you will have a name to be angry at, and you will be angry at it for a month, and it will be the wrong man, and I will have spent a good day on a bad answer.”

“I did that on the ninth day with Iven Sore’s name—”

“Yes. You did, and you wrote it down, and then you sat here for ten days and it turned out to be a question you could not answer, and it will be worse with a man in an office, because a man in an office has a reason to have not written his name.” She picked up the slate and the charcoal. “Here is what I am giving you. The sum. Nine days before the bell. Six days before the order. One row under a rule with nothing in the persons field and the office mark in the response column. I will swear to that in any room, to any clerk, under my own name, and I do not care if the Crown has a file on me, and it will.”

“And you will not tell me what it means.”

“I have told you what it means. What I will not tell you is *who*, and I will not let you guess, and I am not doing it to protect anybody. I am doing it because the moment we have a name, this stops being a practice and becomes a grievance, and a grievance is a thing two men can argue about instead of a thing forty households can look at.” She set the charcoal down on the table. “A name is a small thing. What is on that page is not small. It is that the Crown can enter an answer for a ward, before it asks, and the whole apparatus of this country reads that page as the ward speaking. Every man in a district office is doing what I did for eight months with my own bell. That is what we publish. That is the whole of it and it is enough to be worth the trouble it will cause.”

“You have not answered the question of how a date six days early becomes a sound,” Oren said.

“No,” Tamsin said. “I have answered the question you asked. I have not answered the question you want, and I am not going to try to, because I have spent eight months believing my bell had been tampered with by a person and it turned out that a person had tampered with a *form*, and the difference between the two is exactly the difference between a thief and a department, and I do not know how a department gets into a bell and I have thought about it for six hours and the honest answer is that I do not know.”

Tomas laughed, or made a sound in the vicinity of a laugh.

Then he stopped, and put both hands flat on the table, and looked at them, and said, in a completely different voice:

“There is nothing in this yard I can ring it on.”

“No,” Tamsin said. “There is not. And I would think twice before you went looking for something to ring it on, because the day this gets published it will want a bell, and the first bell anybody puts on it will be chosen by whoever is most frightened.”

---

They took the finding to the market court at the fifth hour, because a fact of this kind is worth nothing in a watch house, and Jerm read it out twice at the foot of the board, under the pinned copy, to about sixty people, and Tamsin read the date twice herself with her face to the people at the back so they could watch her mouth.

She had four things written on the slate. Four.

*ONE. A Crown page headed ROUTE L-4 — RESPONSES ENTERED BEFORE SERVICE has one row under its last rule which is not a household. It names the route. It has nothing at all in the column where the other forty-one rows have a count of persons. It carries the office mark OFF. 411, the same office that appears on the Crown’s own works order for the culvert and on the maintenance ticket for the carrier.*

*TWO. The order it answers is dated three days before the bell was delivered. The row is dated nine days before the bell rang.*

*THREE. A bell does not know the future. A bell carries a claim that a living person has taken responsibility for. Somebody in that office took a duty about that inspection six days before the inspection was written, entered it in an ordinary form in the ordinary week, and the bell carried it to a ward.*

*FOUR. I do not know who. I am not going to guess. A name would be smaller than the practice and easier to argue about.*

The crowd was very quiet in the way a crowd is quiet when everybody has understood the same sentence at the same moment and is waiting to find out whether they are allowed to say so.

Then a man at the front said, “So it’s not a haunted bell.”

“No,” Tamsin said. “It is a bell that does the only thing it was ever able to do, and an office that put a duty in a form before anybody had to be asked.”

And then the crowd began to argue, and there were three voices in the first thirty seconds, and one of them was saying *we are not on that list* and one of them was saying *my house is on that list* and one of them was saying *who is four hundred and eleven*, and Oren’s hand came up once, and did not go down, and stayed up for a while, and Tamsin found that she was watching it with something that was not hope.

“Well,” she said, to Tomas, quietly, under the noise.

He shook his head. “It has to be published in the open to be worth anything. It cannot be a finding in a watch house.”

“It is in the open. I have just read it out under a board in front of sixty people.” She looked at the north road through the gaps in the crowd. “And by the morning there will be two hundred, and then it will be in four other towns, because that is what paper does, and then somebody in the district office will come to this ward to look at it, and he will come to a ward where three children went out of that gate yesterday, and one of them went on a wagon with a woman on the tailboard who was never on the warrant.”

“Then we should be careful what we ask for.”

“We should be *accurate*,” Tamsin said. “Careful is for people who can still change their minds. We are going to say a true thing in a public place and we are going to say it in the plainest words we have, and if the Crown has to walk into this ward to answer for it, then the Crown will have to walk into it, and every door in this lane will know the date it walked in on.”
