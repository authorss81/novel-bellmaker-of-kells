# Chapter 0042 — The Name in the Column

Nell Fenn had a practice, a slate, and two days, and on the morning of the twenty-third she took both of them to a kitchen at the top of the ward and put them on the table and said, “I want to bring Ellen Wilde’s household to the board.”

Mercy Slee had the boy on the stair again and did not open the door any further than a hand’s width, which she had stopped doing about a week ago, and which Nell had privately decided was not about the boy.

“Her name is Mrs Wilde,” Mercy Slee said. “Ellen Wilde has been dead two years.”

“Yes. I have that on a Crown form.”

“You have it on a Crown form because her mother-in-law was standing in that doorway in the autumn before the child was born and wrote her own name in her own hand, and nobody has ever struck it out, and that is the most frightening document any of us has seen this month and you want to bring it to a board.” Mercy Slee let the door a little wider. “And I have watched you for nine days, so I am going to say the rest of it before you say it, and then you can be angry with me and go and do it anyway. Say the rest.”

“You are not a keeper of this ward.”

“No. Say it.”

“I am going to bring a household to a board because its door has a dead woman’s name on it, and if I do that in daylight, then Mrs Wilde is a house on a Crown page and in four books in this town before the day is out, and every Crown office in the district will have a woman called Wilde at one hundred and eight with a girl of ten in a wagon and a dead grandmother in a column.”

Mercy Slee shut the door.

“That is the correct argument and you have known it since you were fifteen, and you have said it to me as a formality, because you have already decided to do it and you have come here to be told you may.” She sat down with the cup she had not made. “Say the other half, then, because the half you have just said to me is the half you have said to yourself every morning for three days and the half that keeps you at this table.”

“The other half is that nobody in this ward knows that house exists.”

“Yes,” Mercy Slee said. “And now say the other other half, and then we can have this out properly, because I am not going to be had at by a girl who has brought me half a thing.”

Nell put her hands flat on the table.

“The other other half is that I have got something and I am not going to use it.”

---

That was the whole of the first hour and it took them both about four minutes to get there once it was out in the open.

“You have got the count,” Mercy Slee said. “Nine *not returned* and six *accompanying*, and every house a child was taken from is on one of the fifteen, and no other marked door is. You made it on the twelfth in a lane with a charcoal and you have told one person and you told me in this kitchen on the eighteenth and you have not spent it in nine days.”

“It is the strongest thing in this town.”

“It is.” Mercy Slee said it without hedging, which she did not often do. “It is also a page that starts with *Fenn*, and if it is ever read out in a room, then for the rest of your life the thing this ward knows about the Fenn house is that your brother and your father were in an argument with a piece of paper. You have known that since the twelfth. So say why you are not using it, and say it as a thing about today and not as a virtue.”

“Because it is about a response column and not about a witness column, and if I spend it in a room tomorrow, then by the twenty-seventh the Crown puts a witness column in front of the ward and every person in this room hears the two of them in the same breath and remembers one answer and not two, and the thing I have spent nine days building will be gone by the twenty-eighth.” Nell turned the slate over and did not put anything on it. “And because I am sixteen and I have got one piece of evidence and I am not spending it on a speech. I am spending it on whatever it turns out to be for, and I do not know yet, and that is the honest state of it.”

“Good,” Mercy Slee said. “Now the part I am actually annoyed about, and then we will go to her.”

“Tell me.”

“You are not bringing that house to the board because I might get a file on it, and you have not asked me, and you have not asked Mrs Wilde, and you have come to this kitchen to tell a woman who has a boy of nine on no roll at all that you are going to do a thing that will put a file on a house that has nothing else on it.” She put her cup down. “That is my argument from the eighteenth and I have not withdrawn it and I am not withdrawing it now. I have thought about it all morning while you were on the lane. It is a good argument. I do not have a better one. And I want you to notice that the reason it is a good argument is that it is about **me**, and I have been letting it be about me for nine days because it is the safest thing I own.”

Nell looked at the table for a while.

“Then I should go and ask her.”

“You should go and ask her, and you should not take a slate, and you should not take me, and you should not tell her what I said about files unless she asks, and if she asks then you tell her the truth including the part where I did not want to be asked.” Mercy Slee got up. “And I am coming anyway, because I am not going to let a girl of sixteen go into that lane by herself, and because there is a Crown officer at my door twice in three weeks and I have got a right to be tired.”

---

The Wilde household was one hundred and eight and it was at the top of the ward past the washhouse turn, and the front room was empty and there were seven people in it who were not of the house.

They had been there since the previous evening. There was a fire in the grate that had gone out and been relit twice, and there was a woman of about thirty sitting on the floor against the wall with her boots still on, and a boy of about fourteen on the boards with a bowl in his hands, and four neighbours, and Mrs Wilde sitting in the good chair with her hands in her lap doing nothing at all with an expression that Nell recognised, because she had worn one herself at the washhouse on the twelfth and had not known at the time what it was.

She recognised it now. It is not grief. Grief in this town is loud and goes to the washhouse. This is a person waiting to find out whether she is going to be told.

“You are the girl with the slate,” Mrs Wilde said.

“I am Nell Fenn. I keep the ward’s book. This is Mercy Slee of a hundred and nineteen and she is not here about anything and she has not come with a slate.”

“I have come with my ears,” Mercy Slee said, and shut the door behind her, and the boy of fourteen looked up and then went back to his bowl.

Mrs Wilde did not ask them to sit. She said:

“You are going to want me to come to your board.”

“We are,” Nell said. “I want you to come to the board on the twenty-fifth and read something out in a market court with about two hundred and thirty people in it, and I want to tell you the whole of what it is before you decide, and I am not going to pretend that is a small thing, and I am not going to pretend I have worked out a way of doing it that is not a small thing, because I have not.”

“Then tell me what it is and then I will tell you what I think about it, in that order, and I have been waiting two years for somebody to come and ask me anything at all.”

---

So Nell told her. All of it, in order, with no argument in it, in a front room with a fire that had gone out, and it took about twenty minutes, and she did not once say *we*.

She told her about the column. She told her that it is printed on the full return and not on the summary form a house is handed, which is on the record because a Crown clerk stood up in a market court on the twentieth and said so. She told her that a name may be put in it by a person of the house, in the house’s own hand, and that the office proposes no name. She told her that the column is not cleared between rounds and that nobody on this earth can now strike a name out of a witnessed return, which a Crown officer said in a lane at four in the morning on the twenty-first while about sixty people were standing in it. And she told her that the Crown had answered Sena Dunn’s question about the column in writing, in a clerk’s own name, on the eighteenth day, and that therefore the shape of a thing that can be checked and a thing that cannot is now known on both sides.

And then she stopped, and said:

“That is the practice. What I want to add to it is a question, and the question is yours to hear first and to refuse, and I am going to say it badly because I have only had it since last night.”

---

The question, when she said it, was this:

*On a Crown return for route L-4 there is a column headed* Witnessed by. *The Crown has told this ward that a name may be put in it by a person of the house, in the house’s own hand, and that the office proposes no name, and that the field is not cleared between rounds. A person who wishes to may go to an officer of the Crown and ask about their own household’s own line: is there a name in the* Witnessed by *column on my line; if there is one, is that person living; and whose hand is it in. They may take a witness. The answer must be written down in the officer’s own name where the person who asked can see it. Nobody has to ask. Nobody has to say why they did not. A household that has asked is a household that has asked and not one that is in dispute.*

Mrs Wilde read it twice off the slate without taking it, and then she said:

“Read that last part again. *A household that has asked is a household that has asked and not one that is in dispute.*”

“That is Mrs Slee’s and it is the only part I argued for and lost.”

“Who wrote it?”

“She did. I want you to know that, because in nine days every notice on that board has had my name on it and the person who actually decided the sentence has been standing next to me and nobody ever reads her name.”

Mrs Wilde looked at the slate for a while and then she said the thing that made the morning work.

“Say the rest of it,” she said. “The part where it is about me.”

---

“The part where it is about you,” Nell said, “is that if you stand up in a market court and read that, then there is a household at one hundred and eight in four books in this town and in a district file, and the file will say that a household called Wilde at one hundred and eight knows about a witness column, and the file will have the girl in it too, and it will not have my name on the question and it will not have Mrs Slee’s on it, because the rule is that the hand is the house’s, and the file will have a house and no person, and that is the best kind of file there is and I cannot promise you it is a kind that does not exist.

“And that is the small half. The large half is that every Crown officer in this district is going to know within a month that this ward has a practice about the *Witnessed by* column, and that practice is going to be used at the other doors on that schedule, and one day somebody is going to come to this lane with a *reason stated by* field and a report and find out which household started it, because that is exactly what they did to me over a mark in a trench, in under two hours, and I have still not got over it.”

“All right,” Mrs Wilde said. “Now the part I have to say, and then you can decide whether I am a woman who wants this.”

“Yes.”

“My name in that column is my mother-in-law’s hand and my mother-in-law has been dead two years, and I have been writing to Fourmile for two years to get one line struck out of one page, and I have written eleven letters, and I have had four answers, and three of them said that the return stands and one of them said that a return is not amended except at a renewal, and I have been told to wait every single time, and I have waited, and I have got a daughter of ten in a Crown wagon because of a name my mother-in-law wrote in my doorway before my daughter was born.”

“And nobody in this ward has ever heard any of that.”

“Nobody in this ward has ever heard any of that because I have never been anywhere.” Mrs Wilde’s hands had not moved. “I have not been to the board. I have not been to the court. I have been at this door for two years waiting for a Crown thing that does not come, and there is no Crown thing coming, and I am not going to pretend to you that I have been brave about it, because I have not. I have been angry, and I have been angry at my own daughter for being born, and there is no version of me that is a good example of anything.”

Nobody in the front room said anything.

“Here is my price, and I am naming it and you are not, and I want that entered somewhere in your book with the hour on it.

“I will read it. I will stand at the side of that board, not the front, and I will read it and I will not be a thing that people came to look at, and if a man in that room says one word about my girl I will stop in the middle of the sentence and go home, and you will not stop me and you will not argue with me. And afterwards there is going to be a file on this house and I want to know that before I say yes and not after.

“And the other half, and it is the half I have been sitting on since last night, and I am going to say it because I heard a woman say it in this lane on the twenty-first and I have not been able to put it down since. She said that a thing said once by the person it belongs to is a record, and a thing found out about in a lane is a rumour. And she said it about herself, and she had made herself smaller on purpose so that nobody could make a version of her that was better than what she said. And I have had a version of me for two years and it is the version where a sensible woman waits, and I do not want to wait any more, and I am aware that what I am actually doing is choosing which version of me gets written down, and I have thought about that all night and I have decided that I would rather be a woman who read a question out loud in a market court than a sensible woman who waited.”

She looked at the slate again.

“Sit me at the side,” she said. “And write down that I asked you what the cost was before I said yes, because in two years nobody has ever asked me what anything cost.”

---

They walked back down the lane with an empty slate.

And on the way, at the third of the four gratings, where a length of chain had been put on the mend in place of a bar, Mercy Slee said, “You are not using it.”

“No.”

“You are going to have to bring it up again inside a week, you know. It is the best thing you have and it is about a response column and it is yours, and one day it is going to be the only argument that works, and if you hold it a week longer than you can then somebody else will hold it instead and it will not be your way of holding it.”

“I know. I have written the date I intend to use it on and I have written it in my own book and it is after the twenty-eighth.”

“After the twenty-eighth,” Mercy Slee said, and stopped, and then said, “Good. That is a real thing and not a trick, and I am glad, and I am not going to ask you what happens after the twenty-eighth, because you do not know either, and if you knew I would be frightened of you.”

---

They did the knocking at the second hour of the afternoon, and they did it on their own and not at the board, and it took them until the light went.

Nell had one question and she asked it at eleven doors and it was the column question in the household’s own words, and she asked it at a door and not in a court and she wrote down nothing at any door, because a slate is a notice and she had learned that on the twentieth day at the cost of a woman of about forty and a satchel and a lamp.

The answers came in ones and twos, and most of them were nothing.

At one hundred and nineteen Mercy Slee put her own head out of the door and said no, and there is nothing on our line, and I have never asked and I am not going to.

At one hundred and thirty-three Mrs Corris said: “Mine. Twenty-day. I put it in myself and I am alive, and you may put that in your book, and I would like it in your book.”

At one hundred and twenty-one, a house with a boy of eleven gone on the tenth, there was a woman of about fifty who let them in and then shut the door behind them and stood with her back against it, and who said, after a while, “My husband’s name. Two years ago. He was in the room when they came the first time. He is not dead, he is at Fourmile, he is a wright, and he does not know that his name is in a column and I am not going to be the one who tells him in a market court.”

“He is living,” Nell said.

“He is living and he was not in this house on the day they came and he has not been in this house for two years, and that is a name in a column that says a person was in the room.”

Then, at a house on the far side of the ward that Nell had never had any reason to knock at, there was an old man of about eighty who had been in his doorway, and who said:

“I do not have a child and I have never had one of your Crown slates on my door and I have never been marked by anybody in my life.”

“Will you ask the question?”

“I will ask the question,” he said, “and I will tell you now that I expect nothing, and I expect the answer to be nothing, and I will want it written down that I asked.”

And at the fourth hour of the afternoon, at a house with a door number on a list that Oren Pike’s gate book held, the answer was this: a woman of about sixty read the question, went and found her husband, and came back and said that there was a name in the column, and that it was her elder sister’s, in her elder sister’s own hand, and that her elder sister had died in the spring, and that the household had never been marked and never had a child taken from it and does not appear on any of the forty-one lines that anybody has ever read out in a room.

“Do you know why your sister’s name is on a line for a house that was never visited?”

“No,” she said. “Nobody has ever come to this door. That is what I am telling you. Nobody has ever come to this door, and my sister wrote her name in my mother’s hand-book in this room in the spring, and it is on a Crown page, and it has been on it since before the girl was taken, and I do not know who put it there or how, and I would like somebody to come to this door and ask me about it in a form.”

Nell wrote that down in her own book, in her own hand, with the hour on it, and read it back to the woman, and the woman made her read the last four words again.

Then she shut the door.

And the girl stood on the step in the lane with a slate under her arm and a thing in it, and did not go to the board with it, because it was half past four and there was nothing anybody could do with it before dark, and because she had learned the hardest thing in this month, which is that a fact with no procedure attached to it is a fact in a girl’s coat.

---

The Crown officer was at the door of the ward board when they got back to it, and he was a young man with a satchel and a slate and he was not the same one as any of the last four, and he had been standing there some time, and the board was shut behind him.

He said, before either of them said anything:

“I am not here about the round. I am here about the second supplementary return, which goes to every household before the close of the twenty-sixth, and I have a list of the doors it has gone to and I am going round them again with a man because two of them have not been taken, and I have been asked to say at the board, in a room, in daylight, before I go —”

He stopped and looked at the two of them and at the shut board.

“Am I in the way?”

“Yes,” Mercy Slee said pleasantly. “Say it to the shut door and then come in and say it again.”

---

Nell Fenn opened the ward board and stood aside and the officer came in and said it a second time, in a room, in daylight, to about nine people, and the market court clerk wrote it down, because that is what the clerk does.

“I am directed to state at this board that the district is preparing a return of the households in this ward which intend to remove from the ward, before the coming renewal, any person under the age of seventeen resident in it. I am not asking you for a list. I have been told there is no list, because a list does not exist. I have been told to ask the board whether such a list exists, and to ask it before I go to the doors, and I would like it said in this room that I asked.”

Nell looked at him for a moment and then said:

“There is no list in this town,” she said, “and I keep the ward’s book, and that is me saying it in a room, and I would like the word *none* entered.”

He said, “I will read that back if you say it slower.”

“There is no list of households that intend to remove a child from this ward,” Nell Fenn said, “because no household in this ward has said out loud in a room where a Crown man was writing that it intends to, and the reason is that the district put the question in a notice on the twenty-first and the board has been asked it at its own step and has answered *none*, and the answer is in the ward’s book in three hands with the hour against it, and there is no list, and I have asked that *none* be entered.”

The clerk entered it, in three hands, with the hour, and the officer read it back and initialled it, and then he stood in the room for a moment longer than he needed to with his hand on the strap of his satchel.

“For what it is worth,” he said, “I did not want to be the one asking it. I am sorry about your form. I mean that as a man and not as an officer, and I am not going to write that down either.”

“Everybody in this town keeps saying that,” Mercy Slee said. “Some of you mean it.”
