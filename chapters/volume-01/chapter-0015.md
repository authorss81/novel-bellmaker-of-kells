# Chapter 0015 — The Sealed Workshop

Deputy Reeve Crake had the Crown inventory order folded inside his coat when he came up the Bellhouse lane in the second hour of the morning, and he had come alone, which Oren found more interesting than the order.

“You have forty bells on those shelves,” Halden said.

“Twenty-six things on those shelves,” Tomas said. “Six of them are not bells. Two are anvils that came with the building.”

“Then you will not object to an inventory that counts twenty-six and corrects the figure.” Halden did not raise his voice. He never did. It was the first thing Oren had learned about him, three years ago, at a market day when a girl had fainted in the arcade and Halden had spoken to her at the same volume he used for a receipt. “The Measure filed a challenge against your licence yesterday. The commission's position is that a workshop which has bound a claim of its own, on a line the Crown maintains, with a carrier that is a Crown object, is a workshop whose bells cannot be left in private hands while a hearing is pending.”

“The carrier is not a Crown object in my hands. The carrier is in your watch store.”

“The carrier is Crown property in the watch store, on a joint receipt that expires at the rescheduled dawn, four days from today.” Halden looked at the yard. “The bells on your shelves are a different question and you know it.”

Oren put himself between the door and the lane, which was not a lawful thing and was a watch captain's thing, and which Halden permitted.

“Read me the operative words,” Oren said.

Halden read them. The order permitted the Deputy Reeve to inventory, seal, and remove from private keeping any signal apparatus in a workshop holding a disputed claim, upon the ground of craft hazard and public safety, pending a public hearing.

“It says *seal*,” Halden said. “It says *inventory*. It says *remove from private keeping*. You may tell me which of those three words you intend to read narrowly, Captain, and I will tell you that the commission has read none of them narrowly since before the Hush.”

“Then I will read them and put my reading in the record, and you will put yours in beside it, and the hearing will read both.” Oren took the order. “I am not refusing you. I am refusing to do it in a corridor.”

They did it in the yard, in the rain, in front of eleven people who had gathered because the Crown officers were at the Bellhouse and the ward had learned that this was where things were decided.

The first thing Oren did was separate the objects, out loud, in front of everybody.

“Four categories, and I am going to say them once. One: the fixed Crown survey carrier, waist-high, mounted, no clapper, wax handprint on the lip, route damage beneath the workshop. It is not in this yard. It is in the watch store, in its frame, in a padded cradle, under a joint receipt signed by me and by the Deputy Reeve, and it stays there until the hearing and not one hour longer.”

Nobody looked at the closed casting-room door, which Oren noted, and wished somebody had, because the empty room was the most eloquent object in the yard.

“Two: a small trial handbell, newly cast, bound to one narrow claim. It has one answer. It cannot carry a second claim until that duty is answered or deliberately unmade. It is named in the order. It is not going anywhere, and I will say more about it in a moment.”

“Three: ordinary working bells on the shelves, which is twenty-six objects of which six are a figure of speech, including two anvils. Four: a courier handbell in somebody else's keeping, which is not Crown inventory and is not on these shelves, and which I will come to as well.”

He set the four headings on a fresh sheet on the bench, where the crowd could read them upside down.

“Now, Deputy. Which of these three categories may be sealed.”

“The working bells.”

“All twenty-six?”

“The connected ones.”

“Which are the connected ones?”

That was where Halden had to do work, and Oren watched him do it without any satisfaction, because the man had a legitimate grievance and knew how to state it. There were nine working bells on the shelves that had bellwire on them: old service cut under the workshop floor, some Crown-maintained, some the Bellhouse's own from before his father's time. Eight of the nine were loose stock that had never been hung. One was a bridge bell for the millrace that had hung on the north wall of the yard since the year the Bellhouse had been built, and which Tomas had shimmed and tuned in an afternoon four years ago and which was the only bell on the list that a working household relied on.

“Eight, then,” Halden said. “Those eight may be sealed, inventoried, and held in the survey store for the term of the hearing. The bridge bell stays, and I will not argue about the bridge bell, because the millrace has no other warning and this town has had enough of those.”

Oren entered it, and then set his own line under Halden's, which was the part that mattered.

“Twenty-six objects, of which eight are sealed by Crown wax for the term of the hearing. The eighteen remaining, including the millrace bridge bell, are recorded, not removed, and remain under the standing no-public-ringing order. Nothing in this inventory moves ownership. Nothing in this inventory makes a private answer into a public one, and I will read that sentence into the record at the hearing if the Crown asks me to.”

“I will ask him,” Halden said.

“Then I will have written it already.”

They sealed the eight. Oren put his own watch mark on the same wax, on the same side, over the Crown seal, so that the wax on each of those eight bells had two hands on it and would come off in two pieces or none. The clerk entered the inventory and read it back twice. It took an hour and a half, and Tomas stood through all of it with nothing to do, which Oren suspected was a deliberate part of the Crown's method and would have done it the same way.

Somebody laughed once, near the end. It was not a good laugh. Oren let it stand, because a laugh was better than a stone, and because the man who laughed had a child on his hip and had clearly not slept.

At the fourth hour the Crown officer tried the trial handbell, and Tomas did it himself first.

He lifted the cloth, took the bell by the frame, and set it out on the middle of the bench where eleven people could see it, with the paper sliver wedged against the crown and the crown's own metal showing on all four sides of it.

“The sliver is the market court's L-4 posting,” he said. “It is not wrapped around the bell. It does not touch the rim. It comes out with two fingers, and it goes back in with two fingers, and I have done it this morning in front of a Crown officer and it did not mark the paper.” He lifted it out, held it up, and put it back. “The bell carries one claim, one keeper's duty, and one answer. It cannot be made to carry a second, and I will not attempt it, and if anybody in this yard believes I have been smuggling a second claim into a private bell, they should look at the frame now and then look at it again at the hearing.”

Nobody touched it. Oren entered what Tomas had said and made Tomas sign beside it as maker and witness, which was a thing Tomas would be held to and a thing Oren had insisted on, because a claim without a signature on it is a rumour with a bell attached.

“And the courier's bell,” Oren said. “It is here, and its keeper is here, and Captain Pike arranged a comparison in daylight under watch supervision on the day the bell was recovered, and that comparison was never made. I am making it now, and it is going on the record, and no one is taking the bell off the person who holds it.”

Tamsin came out from under the stair where she had been standing out of the rain, and unwrapped it herself, on the bench, in front of everybody, and set it down.

She was very precise about this. The cloth went on the bench and the bell went on the cloth, and she did not let Tomas within an arm's length of it, and she said so before she started.

“You read it,” she said. “You do not touch it. You read the wear and the clerk writes the wear and I say whether the reading is fair.”

Oren had Jerm hold the book. The crowd had gone quiet in the particular way a crowd is quiet when it thinks it is about to be shown a thing that will settle an argument.

“Flattened crown,” Tomas said, from where he stood. “Handle worn pale on the courier's side, which is normal for a working handbell. And here, in the groove under the crown, a dark line of wax laid over a paler groove underneath it. The wax is on top. It was not cut into the metal. Whoever did it wanted the same note to travel further, not a different note to travel.”

“And how far is further?” Halden asked.

“That is not a thing I can see. It is a thing that has to be heard.” Tomas looked at Tamsin. “It is your bell. You measured it, in March, with a watchman at the landing and a boy on the stage. What did you find?”

Tamsin said, “The authorized route for this bell is the north gate to the river landing, and no further, because past the landing the flood bell takes up the local signal. In March I rang it once at the gate with the watchman's knowledge. A boy on the landing stage heard it. That is the authorized limit, and the boy was standing at it. In June I rang it once at the north gate with a watchman at the landing stage, and a man on the ferry road, a quarter mile past the low gate, heard it and walked down to ask who was calling the market. I did not authorize that. I did not know it before I tested it, and I have not been able to make it stop.”

“Is that a fault or a design?” Oren asked.

“It is a fault I did not make, in a bell I am responsible for, and both of those are true and I would like one of you to find a place to put them that is not a drawer.”

Nobody spoke for a moment. The wax in the groove sat in the rain and did what wax does, which was nothing.

Oren entered it. He set down that the courier retained possession throughout, that the comparison had been conducted under watch supervision, that no custody was transferred, that the route was not disclosed and had not been asked for, and that the watch had not required the route and did not hold it.

Then he set down the sentence that mattered, and read it aloud before he signed it.

“The wear in this bell widens a signal beyond its authorized route. That is a fact about metal. It is not evidence that the Low Ward agreed to anything, and it is not a public answer, and the person holding this bell has not given the watch her route and is not required to.”

Tamsin looked at the sheet, and then at him, and had nothing to say, which Oren understood was the highest compliment she had available.

“You may record that I object to that last clause being written at all,” Halden said.

“You may. I will write that the Deputy Reeve objects, and put it under my line, and the hearing can read the two together.”

After that the yard emptied by degrees. The eight sealed bells had their Crown wax and Oren's mark, and were carried down the lane in a handcart to the survey store, and the loss of them went through the Bellhouse like a change in the weather. The millrace bell stayed on the north wall under a written condition that it might be rung only for its own millrace and only by Tomas or a person he had named in writing, which Oren insisted on putting in the record, because a bell with a condition and no name on it becomes a bell anybody can blame.

The chandler came at the fifth hour, and then the miller.

The chandler wanted to know whether Nell Fenn's work would keep coming to the Bellhouse, and Nell herself said no from the step, without waiting to be asked, which Oren entered as well.

The miller took the bell for his daughter's feast.

“I have the name of a man in Miremarket,” he said, not unkindly. “He is not you. He charges more. I would rather he had it than that it sat in your window with a Crown mark on the rim, and I would rather tell you that than have you find out at the feast.”

“I understand,” Tomas said.

“Send me a note when the mark is off. I may come back. I am not saying that to be kind, I am saying it because you did the girl's clapper properly four years ago and I have never forgotten the price you did not charge me for the rush job.”

He went. Tomas entered the miller in the lost column and left the paid column alone, which was becoming a habit.

Then the lane was empty, and the yard door had a Crown seal on it as well as the no-ringing notice, and Halden's seal was over the no-ringing notice, and Oren's mark was beside Halden's, and none of the three would come off without all three coming off.

Tamsin was still there. She had not gone up the lane with the rest of them, and she was standing by the bench with her hand flat on the cloth her bell had been lying on.

“You did not take it out of the room,” she said.

“What?”

“The trial handbell. You had it under that cloth and the Crown was at the gate and there is a cellar stair behind the casting room that goes out under the wall. You thought about it. I watched you think about it twice, and I have seen you do that before, and both times it was about keeping a thing safe from a man who wanted it.”

Tomas had, in fact, thought about it. He had thought about it for most of that second hour while eight bells were being waxed in his rain.

“If I had moved it,” he said, “then in nine days somebody would ask why the private answer was not in the place where the record says it was. And the answer would be that the maker hid it, and the answer would be true, and it would be the only true thing anybody could say about it.”

“Yes.”

“I wanted to.”

“I know. That is why I am still standing here.” She folded the cloth over her bell and put it away. “If you had done it, I would not have helped you. I want you to understand that this is not a compliment. It is a condition. A route that is manufactured cannot be repaired by anybody, including the man who made it, and I have spent my life on routes that were manufactured by men who were frightened in exactly the way you are frightened at this moment.”

“I understand.”

“Do you?”

“Not entirely,” Tomas said. “But I know the shape of it now, and I set it down this morning in a paper I have not finished, and one of the lines in that paper is that a keeper may take himself off a route without giving a reason and without a cost, and I set that line down before you said it, which I would like noted, and which I do not expect you to believe.”

The rain came harder.

Oren came out of the casting room and stood under the eave with his ledger shut.

“Vale,” he said. “I am going to ask you to do something tomorrow that you will not want to do, and I am going to ask you now so that you have a night to think about how you will refuse, because I would rather you refused in the morning than fumbled at noon.”

“Say it.”

“In two days' time there is a public test at the market court. Three answers, kept separate, on a claim with your name as keeper of the words.” Oren turned the ledger over and did not open it. “Before a town answers you, the town is going to hear what you did on the night of the Hush, in the open, from your own mouth, and the witnesses are going to be there, and I am going to sit in the front and take the account. Not a trial. I cannot hold a trial and I will not pretend to. A record.”

Tomas looked at the yard, at the sealed door, at the millrace bell on the wall that was the only one left in the yard he could ring for anything.

“Why now?” he said. “Why not at the hearing?”

“Because at the hearing you will be a man with a claim, and a crowd will hear a man with a claim explain a warning, and your word for your word will be worth about as much as anybody else's.” Oren's voice did not change at all, which was how Tomas knew what it cost. “Because I have been carrying a version of that night in my own chest for twelve years where it is the part where the warning stopped, and I would like it to be on paper, in my hand, in a room where your sister can hear it if she ever comes, and I would like it there before you ask anybody else to trust you with their children.”

He said the last part as a fact and not as an accusation, and it was the first time in twelve years that Oren Pike had put anything about Ewan into a sentence aimed at Tomas's benefit.

“Your brother is not the only person who has been carrying it,” Tomas said.

“No,” Oren said. “But I am the only one left who has to decide whether to use it.”

He went down the lane with the ledger shut under his arm, and Jerm followed with the book of the comparison, and the two Crown guards turned their chairs to face the wall, which was the closest thing to privacy that yard would ever have.

Tomas took the no-ringing notice off the door so that the Crown seal underneath could be read, and put it back crooked, which was the only opinion the Bellhouse had left to offer.

In the casting room, the pale rectangle of unfaded floor where the carrier's frame had stood for four days was still there. He had swept around it twice now and would, he understood, go on sweeping around it. Beside the bench lay a wrapping sheet with four unfinished lines of a withdrawal procedure, an inspection record with a limitation in it, and a column in the day-book in which the numbers had begun to run in a single direction, all of them the same, none of them recoverable.
