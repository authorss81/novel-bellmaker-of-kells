# Chapter 0041 — What a Page Is For

The morning of the twenty-second day in Kells was a morning with no bell in it, and there was an enormous amount of talking.

Tomas came down the hill at about the seventh hour because he could not sleep past six, and the lane told him the state of the town before anybody did. Shutters up on the ward side. Nobody at the mouths of the low lane. A woman washing at a tub she had not put out in the sun, which is a thing you do when you do not know what today is. And at the low end of the turn, where the lane turns for the landing, Gib Hask’s six men were standing in the same place they had stood on the fifteenth day with a crowbar across a knee, and the crowbar was not across a knee now, it was in the ground.

Not deep. Four inches, which is what a bar goes in when a man is saying he is not going away and has better things to be doing at four in the morning.

Renn’s gate had not come up. It had not come up since the seventh day of this month and it was the twenty-second, and Tomas stood at the low gate in the cold and looked at the sill and the gravel packed behind it and the Crown cart still across the culvert two hundred yards round, under a works order that the Crown’s own papers still carried as current, and there was nothing he could do about any of the three things he could see.

---

The ward board had forty people at it by the ninth hour, which was more than had been there for the first reading of the pinned schedule and about a third of what had been in the lane on the twenty-first.

And there was a sheet of Crown paper on the board that had come up from Fourmile on the morning post, which had been unrolled and weighted with two stones, and which Nell Fenn was reading out loud because it was the ward’s practice and she was the ward’s book.

It was an extract. It had a district stamp on it and a line at the top in a small neat hand that was not Ansel Stroud’s and was not Ferrow’s, and the line said that it was made out for the information of the ward board and the local court, and that the ward’s practice was a matter for the district and no longer for the local court, and that therefore the information was being supplied to the ward in the ordinary way.

Then the figures.

*Renewal upon route L-4, conducted at first light on the twenty-first day of this month at thirty-seven doors, the form being exhibited entire and any person admitted as a witness.*

*Twenty-two of the thirty-seven doors: objection entered by a person of the household, in the margin of the household’s own line, in the clerk’s own name and initialled. Of those twenty-two, nine: the answer given was that the officer was not permitted to say, and the objection and the refusal to answer were both written down.*

*Witnessed by: the column was filled in two lines. The office proposed neither name. The field is not cleared between rounds.*

*Persons removed: one. Removed at the fifth hour and thirty minutes.*

And then, at the foot of it, in the small hand again, four sentences that had been copied out of somebody else’s return and not altered in the copying:

*I certify this round as a round. I do not certify any household’s answer, and I decline to countersign any record which asserts that any household on route L-4 assented to or received an answer. A form that is exhibited entire, and a procedure that is published, and a clerk with a conscience, together make a round that is conducted properly and conducted entirely, and that is not the same thing as a round that is lawful, and I have been a surveyor for eleven years and I am obliged to write down that I no longer know what a form is for.*

Nobody in the lane said anything for a while. The stones that were weighting the paper had to be moved by somebody and Mercy Slee moved them, because she was nearest and because she was the sort of person who moved a stone.

Then a man at the back said, “Twenty-two.”

“Twenty-two,” Nell Fenn said. “Out of thirty-seven. That is a margin in a clerk’s own name at twenty-two doors, and at nine of them the clerk wrote down that he was not allowed to answer, and both of those things went up to Fourmile on a Crown horse in a Crown hand.”

“And one,” Mrs Corris said.

She was at the front, in the same shawl, and she was sixty-five and she had put her own name in a Crown column on the twentieth day, and she said the second word the way you put a knife down.

“One,” Nell Fenn agreed. “Out of thirty-seven doors and forty-one on the schedule and four hundred and eleven persons in this ward, and it was taken at the fifth hour and thirty minutes off a line nobody in that house had ever been marked on, and the objection was written down in the margin in the clerk’s own name and initialled, and the objection did not stop it. Both numbers are on the same page and I am not going to let anybody in this lane have one of them without the other.”

---

Tomas had his own page on the bench in his own yard and it was fifty-one lines long and it was the only document about the twenty-first that a person in this ward could walk to their own door and check.

He had written it in three columns, and the headings of the three columns were not *position* and not *answer* but **first thing, second thing, third thing**, and that was on the nineteenth day and it was the only idea he had had in a month and it was the only one he had had in a month that had turned out to be exactly right for reasons he had not intended.

He read it through once in the yard with the door open. It was accurate. Every line of it was something he had seen with his own eyes, and he had put nothing in it that he could not stand behind at a door, and at nine o’clock in the morning he came out of the yard with it under his arm and went down the lane to the board, and he gave it to Mercy Slee.

He did not give it to Nell. He gave it to Mercy Slee because Mercy Slee already held the co-held copy of the schedule and a boy of nine and no roll anywhere, and because Mercy Slee had been entered in a Crown return on the seventeenth day by a watch captain who had not asked her, and because he had learned one thing in twenty-two days and it was that a page he gives to a person is a thing he has done, and a page he keeps is a thing he has not.

“I am not keeping this,” he said. “I have written it and I am handing it over and I am going to be asked about it and I am not going to be able to say what it means.”

“I know what it means,” Mercy Slee said, and folded it into the inside of her shawl, against her arm, where a person cannot take it off you without taking your shawl.

“Mercy—”

“You have written down that a child went off a witnessed return in a margin that did not stop it,” she said, “and that is a page, and a page in a shawl is a page that cannot be taken off a board in an hour with a slate. Say nothing else about it and go and stand somewhere else for a while.”

---

He was asked about the market lane four times before the middle of the day.

The first was a boy of about eleven at the mouth of the low lane, who wanted to know whether the bell on the nineteenth had been about the wagon. The second was a carter who wanted to know, in front of nine people, whether thirty-six pounds was going to be made up to him. The third was a man Tomas had never seen who had come from Fourmile on the morning post to find out whether a bell in Kells could empty a market lane. The fourth was at the ward board, in daylight, with about sixty people in it, and it was the only one that mattered.

It was Absalom Teage who asked it, and he asked it standing up, in the flat voice of a man laying bricks, and he had a slate under his arm and he had been out since the seventh hour.

“You said on the twentieth morning that you were going to say nothing for four days,” Teage said. “I have counted them and there are two of them left. I want it said in this lane and not in a room, because I have a boy of nine and I stood in this lane on the twenty-first for two hours and watched a girl of ten go up two steps into a wagon, and I have not been able to work out what I am supposed to do with my face when a bell of yours goes.”

“I said four days and I meant four days, and the fourth day is the twenty-fourth,” Tomas said.

“Then say it on the twenty-fourth.” Teage did not put the slate down. “You rang a bell four times in this market lane on the Friday night at about twenty past eight and a hundred and eighty people went out of it in the dark, and a carrier lost a cart and thirty-six pounds, and a Crown girl of nineteen has a wrist broken who was standing where you asked her to stand. Have you got anything for me? Not an apology. I have had nine of those from nine men this week. Have you got a thing I can use.”

“Yes,” Tomas said. “But not today. I will say it on the twenty-fourth, in this lane, as many times as anybody wants to hear it, and I will not improve it the second time. And what I am not going to do is tell you here what it was, because a thing I say in a lane at noon on the twenty-second is a version, and this ward has three weeks of evidence about what happens when a sentence leaves the room it was made in.”

“And on the twenty-first,” Teage said, “there was a Crown wagon go up that lane at the fifth hour and thirty minutes and a bell of yours did not stop it and there was nothing in Kells that could have. Have you got anything for that?”

“No,” Tomas said. “And I have been walking about with that for three days.”

The lane did what a lane does. It went quiet, and then it made the noise a lane makes when a lot of people are thinking at once, and Absalom Teage put the slate under his other arm and said, “The twenty-fourth, then,” and went off up the hill with about four people following him at a distance, which is what happens in this town now.

Tomas wrote the whole of it in his day-book afterwards, with the hour on it, in the flat way he writes things he is ashamed of, and added one line that is not a fact and that he has not struck out:

*Asked four times about the nineteenth, in one day, and refused four times, in one day, and did not say the word broadcast, because I do not think that is a word anybody in this lane will use in my lifetime and I am not going to be the man who brings a Crown word into a lane in the last week of the month that he spent trying to keep them out of one. The reason is owed and is not given. It is owed on the twenty-fourth and it will be given in a lane and not in a room, and I have written the day on this page so that a man cannot come at me in a week and say that the day came and went.*

---

By the second hour of the afternoon there was a thing happening in the ward that Tomas had nothing to do with and did not like, and it was the best thing he had seen in three weeks, and he had to stand in his own yard and watch it not be his.

Absalom Teage had a slate and a piece of chalk and a list of house numbers on the back of a Crown works order, and he was going down the lane asking **one** question at each door, and the question was not one of the three.

He said it out loud at the top of the lane for anybody who wanted to know, which was about a dozen people, and then he said it twenty-odd more times in twenty-odd doorways.

“Two things. What did you do on the twenty-first, and who saw you do it.”

And he wrote the answer down in his own hand with the house number, and he did not ask what was in a margin, and he did not ask a Crown officer anything, and he did not ask a single question that a clerk could answer.

Forty-one doors on the schedule. He got to nineteen of them by the second hour of the afternoon and he had been turned out of four and let in by nine and shouted at by one, and at house 114 he wrote his own answer in his own hand, because he had been standing in the lane, and it said: *Nothing. Stood. Two hours. I have a boy of nine.*

Tomas came out of his gate and stood and watched the whole of it for about twenty minutes, and did not go over, and did not help.

And the thing he noticed — the thing that went into the day-book that night, in four words, because it needed four — was that in nine days of teaching this town to ask a Crown officer a precise question in a public room, he had not once produced a question that a person could answer at their own door about their own morning, and that a carter with a slate and a piece of chalk had produced one in about a minute without being told anything by anybody, and that the difference between the two was that his version needed an officer in the room.

---

The Crown’s answer came at half past the eleventh hour, nailed to the board beside the extract, and it was not a notice, and it had no objection in it, and it took the clerk who nailed it less than a minute to get it level.

It was a form.

*SUPPLEMENTARY RETURN, ROUTE L-4, SECOND SERIES. TO BE DELIVERED TO EVERY HOUSEHOLD IN THE WARD BEFORE THE CLOSE OF THE TWENTY-SIXTH DAY.*

*Fields, in this order: the household’s intention, in respect of any person under the age of seventeen resident in it, as at the coming renewal; the name of any person to whom such person would be delivered; the reason, in the household’s own words, for the intention; and the name of any person in whose presence the intention was stated.*

*No signature is required. A household may return nothing. A household may return a statement in place of a return, and any such statement returned before the close of the twenty-sixth day will be certified by the district and entered on the return for the renewal of the twenty-eighth day.*

It is a good form. It has the reason in it, which is the field Absalom Teage had demanded in open court on the twentieth day, and it has the witness, which is a name in a household’s own hand, which is what the *Witnessed by* column has always been, and it asks for a household’s intention in the household’s own words, and it explicitly permits a household to return nothing, and it says that a statement will be certified.

Nell read it out twice, which is what she does, and the second reading was slower than the first and there were about fifty people at the board and eleven of them left while she was doing it.

“It is the best thing they have put in front of this ward,” Mercy Slee said, to nobody, in the voice she used for a thing she had been arguing against for nine days.

“It is,” said a man at the back.

“It is exactly the three questions with the hand taken off them,” Mercy Slee said. “That is all it is. It is us, in a Crown hand, with our own words in the reason field, and a box for the name of whoever was standing there. The only difference is that the Crown gets to send it round instead of us going round, and there is no one in a lane arguing that somebody should not answer.”

Nobody answered her, and she was not wrong, and by the following morning there were three households in this ward who had sent something back.

And Tomas Vale, standing at the bottom of his own lane on the twenty-second day of the month, with the extract of the surveyor’s return above him on a board and a form below it and a carter’s slate going up and down his street, understood the whole of the twenty-two days he had spent in a single sentence, and it is the sentence that is the title of the page he wrote that night, and he did not improve it in the morning, and Oren Pike entered it in the gate book in his own hand, which is the only place in this business where a sentence has ever been entered twice:

*That a procedure can make an office legible and cannot make an office stop. Deputy Reeve Crake said that on the twenty-first day in a lane and it is the truest sentence anybody in this commission has said this month, and I have nothing to add to it, and I have had a day to try, and I am a maker of bells and I have been trying to make a page into a thing that stops people for twenty-two days.*

*And the part I can add, and I add it because it is mine and not his: the page is not the town’s instrument. The page is what the town has instead of a reason. On the twenty-first there were twenty-two margins and one wagon, and both of those are on the same sheet in the same hand, and I have carried that sheet about all day in a shawl, and it is the best thing anybody has given me this month, and it did not stop a child going up two steps, and it is not going to, and there is no sentence anybody in this ward can write that will make it stop, and that is not a failure of the sentence.*

*Cost of the day, in figures, because the figures are for them. One Crown extract received and read. One page of my own handed away and not kept. Fifty-one lines checkable at any door. One carter’s question asked at nineteen doors in one afternoon without being told it by me, which is the only thing I did today that was worth anything, and I did not do it. Four times asked about the nineteenth in one day and four times refused, and two days of the four still to run, and on the twenty-fourth I will say it in a lane and I will not improve it.*

And one other thing, which is not on the page, because it is not a fact about the Crown and not a fact about the ward and is only a fact about a man, and it is in his own day-book where nobody looks:

*And I find that on the twenty-second day, with my page gone into a shawl and my bell on its frame in a yard with three seals on the door, I am not lighter. I thought I would be. I have carried that page for two days as though it were the thing that was doing the work, and it was never the thing that was doing the work. The thing that was doing the work was a carter with a slate and a woman of sixty-five who put her own name in a Crown column because she was in the room, and a woman of about forty who had a Crown officer at her door twice and did not want to be invisible on the twenty-first. Nobody in this ward has been saved by a procedure this month. Every single thing that has happened in this ward that mattered was done by a person who could have done nothing, on their own account, in their own words, and there is not one of them I would have chosen.*

*And if I had been a bell instead of a man I would have rung about it, and it would have gone further than I want it to, and the ward would have filled in the rest of the sentence for me by the second strike.*
