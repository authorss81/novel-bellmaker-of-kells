# Chapter 0008 — The Watchman’s Account

Oren Pike had heard bells in three forms during his years with the Kells watch.

The first was a sound someone had chosen. A market keeper rang the opening bell after the sellers had agreed on the hour. A flood keeper rang when the river rose. A courier’s handbell repeated a signal only as far as the route allowed. Those sounds had edges. They could be argued with, delayed, or refused.

The second was a sound carried by machinery. A relay bell repeated what it received, sometimes cleanly and sometimes with a burr that told the listener the line had been stretched or poorly joined. Oren had learned to trust the burr before the note.

The third was the sound that remained after a man had made a choice and tried to make it disappear. His brother’s warning had been one of those. The bell had not been silent when Ewan died. Tomas had cut the rope that carried it, and the sound had ended before it reached the people who needed it. The rest had been aftermath.

Oren stood at the north gate and listened to the two notes repeat.

The low note came through the stone beneath the watchman’s boots. The second came from the bell above the gate, clear and high enough to make the mortar tremble. The fixed watch bell had a proper clapper, and its mouth held the ordinary mechanism that made it work. It was doing exactly what it should. That made the repetition worse.

“Write it down,” Oren said.

The watchman on duty obeyed. His name was Jerm, and he had held the north gate for nine years. He wrote the hour, the weather, and the two notes in a square hand.

“Did anyone touch the rope?” Oren asked.

“No.”

“Did a courier use the relay line?”

“No courier came through.”

“Did you hear a bell in the Bellhouse first?”

Jerm hesitated. “Not before the first note.”

Oren turned toward the road. The Vale Bellhouse sat above the gate, its sign faded and its casting-room shutters closed. A Crown notice hung on the front door. The fixed carrier was inside, he had been told, and Tomas had been told not to ring it.

“Send a runner to the watch house,” Oren said. “And find the maintenance crew that closed the river route. Tell no one to move the gate bell.”

He went up the hill himself.

The Bellhouse answered his knock with silence. Tomas opened the door after a long pause, as if he had expected every visitor to be an officer. His face was drawn, and there was a bright line of redness around his ears.

“The gate bell repeated the pattern,” Oren said.

“I heard.”

“Did you strike your trial handbell again?”

“No.”

“Did anyone in the yard?”

“Nell answered the private claim. Nothing was connected to the gate.”

Oren looked past him into the casting room. The small trial handbell sat on a covered bench. The fixed Crown carrier remained upright in its mounting frame, straps taut, empty crown exposed. Oren had seen enough Crown equipment to recognize the frame’s retaining bolts. The carrier could not be lifted casually. It had been designed to stay where a route placed it.

“Show me the carrier,” he said.

Tomas unlocked the casting-room door and stepped aside.

Oren entered. The room smelled of cold metal, oil, and the sharp sweetness of Mara’s copying wax. The fixed carrier’s lip was marked with the child-sized handprint. Beneath the floor, he could hear the uneven tick of a cut coupling.

“Who delivered it?” he asked.

“Two porters at first light, and the surveyor Pever an hour later with a clerk. He was late.”

“Who saw the first ringing?”

“I did. Pever came after. The porters may have heard the later one.”

“Write that down.”

Tomas took a fresh sheet from the bench. Oren did not dictate quickly. He made Tomas state each fact in the order it had happened: the carrier arrived before first light; its lip was warped; the clapper was absent; it rang without being struck; the route beneath the workshop had a cut coupling; Mara’s note was found behind the frame. When Tomas reached the note, Oren stopped him.

“What does it say?”

“Do not let them call it an answer.”

“Who wrote it?”

“Mara.”

“Can you prove that?”

“No.”

Oren’s jaw tightened. “Then you cannot put Mara’s name in the watch record as a confirmed author.”

“I can put ‘a note in handwriting attributed to Mara Vale.’”

“That is not the same.”

“It is the truth available.”

Oren looked at the carrier. The child’s wax handprint was clear in the gray light. “The old tone?”

He meant the Hush. The word belonged to a rope cut years ago and to every argument that had followed. Tomas’s shoulders tightened.

“The old route carried a similar pulse,” Oren said. “Not the same bell. The same kind of line.”

“I know.”

“Ewan heard it.”

Tomas looked at him. “You never told me that.”

“You never asked.”

“I was told the warning failed.”

“It failed. That does not mean it was empty.”

Oren walked to the window and looked over the north road. From there he could see the low roofs of the Low Ward and the pale strip of the river beyond. “During the Hush, the gate route began to carry a warning from the upper line. A child was trapped under the bell frame. The frame was coming down. You cut the rope.”

“I cut the rope to get the child out.”

“The rope held the frame’s counterweight. When you cut it, the counterweight fell. Ewan was beneath the line.”

“I know where he was.”

“You know where you left him.”

The words were not new. They had been said in the market court, at the watch house, and in the hours after the burial. Tomas had never found an answer that made them smaller.

“The tone went out afterward,” Oren continued. “Ewan heard it before the frame fell. He said it was warning us to clear the lower road. By the time the rest of us understood, the line had stopped.”

“Someone could have heard it.”

“Someone did. But Ewan was the one I needed to reach.”

Tomas’s hand found the edge of the workbench. “I did not cut it to silence anyone.”

“I know what you meant.”

“That is not what happened.”

“No.” Oren turned back to him. “That is what you have been telling yourself.”

The silence between them had weight, but it was not the old silence of the first days after the Hush. Oren had come with a route docket and a public duty. Tomas had made a private warning. The two facts were touching at a point neither had chosen.

“Your carrier sounds,” Oren said. “Your trial bell sounds. The gate repeats. You want me to put a watch seal on the carrier.”

“I want the evidence protected from anyone who can remove it.”

“That is not the same as asking me to trust you.”

“I know.”

Oren took a small brass seal from his belt. The Kells watch mark was worn almost smooth. He placed it on the mounting frame beside the carrier, not on the bell itself. A strip of red cloth ran beneath the frame, and Oren tied it around the retaining bolt.

“Dated watch seal,” he said. “The carrier stays in this frame and in this room until the Crown and the watch agree on a custody order. No one moves it without a record. No one rings it.”

Tomas looked at the seal. “The route under the floor?”

“Your witness observes it. I will send a watchman to the yard. You do not open the channel alone.”

“I can inspect it.”

“In front of a witness.”

The terms were harder than a command and safer than trust. Tomas accepted them.

Oren wrote a receipt for the seal: time, room, condition of the carrier, absent clapper, child-sized wax handprint, damaged route beneath the workshop, and the route fragment Tomas had carried from the Bellhouse. He read it aloud before signing. Tomas added his own name as maker and witness, not as keeper of the public warning.

“Where is the route docket?” Oren asked.

Tomas gave him the slip he had received at the market court. Oren unfolded it beside the ledger. It named the route, the north gate, the survey store, and a local surveyor whose service entry had been withdrawn.

“His name is Iven Sore,” Oren said.

Tomas looked up. “The Bellwrights’ Measure master?”

“The name on the service ticket is Iven Sore. The roll has no current entry for him. There is a Crown seal, but no living witness to the withdrawal.”

“Could be a clerk using his name.”

“Could be.”

“Then the route could be a test.”

“Or a maintenance error. I will not call either one a crime until I have a person who can answer.”

Oren folded the docket. “The Crown wants the carrier secured before the original dawn. Halden will use the repeated tone as proof that the Bellhouse is unsafe. If you hide the trial bell, he will call that concealment. If you ring it, he will call it a public command.”

“Then I will not ring it.”

“Not in public. Not anywhere until we know what the route is doing.”

They stood in the casting room while the light thinned. Oren could hear the ordinary market beyond the walls, the creak of carts, and the ordinary human complaints of people who did not want to be searched. The warning had entered the town’s day, but the town had not become quiet. That was not proof. It was only a fact worth recording.

Oren escorted Tomas out of the casting room and across the yard. At the gate, the fixed watch bell gave a single note as the watchman changed shift. It was an ordinary sound. Tomas still flinched.

Oren noticed. “You do not have to trust the bell.”

“I know.”

“You do have to stop treating every sound as a verdict.”

“Your brother was warned by one.”

“Ewan was not killed by a verdict. He was killed by a falling frame and a man’s choice.”

The words were harsher than Oren intended. He looked away. “Record that I said both.”

“I will.”

“Do not soften it.”

“I will not.”

Oren returned to the watch house. The seal on the fixed carrier was bright red against old wood, and the trial handbell remained in the casting room under a temporary no-public-ringing order. Tomas had evidence protection, not safety. Oren had a public duty, not trust.

When the night watch reported, the north-gate bell rang twice again.

The docket in Oren’s pocket named a surveyor who had disappeared from the roll. The tone named no one at all. Oren wrote both facts down and sent a copy to the watch house archive before he allowed himself to think of Ewan.
