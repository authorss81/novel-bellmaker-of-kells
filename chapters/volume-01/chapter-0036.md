# Chapter 0036 — The Column Nobody Has Seen

The problem of publishing a thing you have destroyed is that you have to be able to swear to it, and Nell Fenn could swear to it, and could not show anybody.

That was the whole of the eighteenth of this month, and it took her until the fourth hour to say it in a sentence she could act on.

She could not show anybody. She had read a column heading off a Crown form to a Crown clerk in a filing room and had written it on a slate and had let him correct one word and had burned the slate in the washhouse fire on the twelfth day, and what was left was a girl of sixteen saying *there is a column and it is headed Witnessed by and it is empty on every return we file*, which is exactly the shape of a thing a Crown officer writes down and takes to a district office.

And there was a second thing under the first, and it was the thing that had kept her awake for six days.

If she said *a Crown clerk told me*, she had named a clerk who had said a true thing in a room with the door shut, and a named clerk is a man in a file being described, and a man in a file being described does not last a week.

If she said *I read it off a form*, then somebody would ask her which form, and which return, and in which room, and she would have to produce the form, and the form was in an office in Fourmile and would come back to her by a road she could not control.

If she said *the ward’s practice was published on the thirteenth and the Crown published a counter and a clerk wrote in a margin*, then it was a scandal, and a scandal has a villain, and a villain is a man or a woman with a name who can be argued about for a month, and in a month it will be *the ward had a grievance about a page*, which is precisely what Officer Rudd said would happen in open court on the thirteenth, and Oren had read that out twice because it was true.

So she sat on the washhouse step at the eighth hour with a fresh slate bought that morning for sixpence and nothing on it, and she worked out, over about two hours, the only shape she had ever seen work in this town, and the shape was Tomas Vale’s, and she had got it off him on the thirteenth by refusing to let him say it.

The three questions were not an accusation. They were questions anybody could ask about their own line on their own paper, and they worked, and Absalom Teage had got two sentences written in a margin in a Crown clerk’s own name by standing at a table in daylight, and it had cost nothing to publish because there was nothing in it to refuse.

What Nell had was a column, and a column is not a question, and a column has to become a question before it can be put into anybody’s mouth.

---

She went up to 119 at the tenth hour and knocked twice and Mercy Slee opened the door a hand’s width, which she had been doing for a week, and behind the door there was a boy of nine on the stairs who was at no school in Kells and had a right to be at a school in Fourmile, and was at neither.

“I have something and it is not on the board and I am telling you before anybody, including him, and I want it in your kitchen and not on your step, because I have noticed that things said on a step are heard by whoever is passing and I do not want this to be a thing people have heard by supper.”

Mercy Slee let her in and shut the door.

“Say it.”

“There is a column on the Crown form behind the schedule we pinned up. It is headed *Witnessed by*. It is the name of a person who was in the room when a household gave its answer. It is empty on every return any of us has ever filed, and the clerk said he has filed four hundred and six of ours in eleven years, and it is empty on all of them. And a summary form drops the reason an answer was entered in office instead of a room.”

Mercy Slee did not move and did not sit down.

“Why are you telling me this on the eighteenth day and not on the twelfth, in a washhouse, with a slate?”

“Because on the twelfth I had just burned it and because I have spent six days working out how to give it away and I have not worked it out yet, and if I come to you with a thing I have not worked out, then you will say *that girl has an idea*, and you will be right, and that is a thing I cannot afford this week.”

“Go on.”

“If I say it the way it is, then it is a scandal.” Nell put the clean slate on the table. “If I say it as a question, then it is the thing you did on the thirteenth. And I have got it written and I want you to read it before I say any more of it, because you are the only person in this ward whose house is on no roll and whose opinion I have never once been able to guess at, and if it is wrong I would rather you told me now than found out in a week.”

She turned the slate round.

*Any person in this ward may go to any Crown clerk, in daylight, and ask this question about a paper they have been given:*
*On a Crown form, there is a column headed* Witnessed by. *What goes in it, and is there one on my form?*
*And they may take a witness, and they may write down what is said to them, and the answer must be written down where the person who asked can see it.*

Mercy Slee read it twice.

“That is the three questions with a different hat on,” she said.

“It is.”

“It is also a question that a person asks *about a paper they have been given*, and the Crown hands the paper out, so the Crown puts the question in the Crown’s own mouth.” She looked up. “That is clever. Is it yours?”

“It is mine, and I have been sitting on it for two days, and I want to say where I got the shape of it, because you will find out and I would rather you heard it from me. On the thirteenth, four of us in the watch house argued about who should say it, and I said a claim voiced by its maker is the thing we have spent this month learning not to do, and I was pleased with myself about it, and I have thought about it every day since, and you are about to say a thing to a room and I am going to say it first and then you are going to say it, and that is not the same argument twice. It is the same argument with me standing behind you instead of in front.”

“Where is your name?”

“Underneath, in my own hand, and yours beside it, and Oren’s if he will put his on it, and I am not going to read either of your names out first. That is the other half of it and it is the part I want you to argue with me about.”

---

They argued about it for two hours and the argument was real and Mercy Slee won two thirds of it.

She won on the word *any*. “You have written *any person may go*. That includes the Cafferys’ neighbours and it includes a household that wants to be left alone, and you know what a person in this ward does with a permission. Rudd’s objection is on the record and it is true. The Crown can only note a house by knowing the house. You have not written a permission. You have written an invitation, and you are putting it in the hands of four hundred and eleven people and every one of them is going to decide on their own what to do with it, and the ones who do nothing are the ones who are safe, and the ones who do something are the ones who are on a page, and you cannot write *any person* and mean to protect the ones who do nothing.”

“Then I take it out and I write *a person who wishes to*.”

“Better. And you write that nobody has to, and nobody has to say why, and that a household that asks is a household that has asked and not one that is in a dispute, because *dispute* is a word they use and *asked* is a word we use, and words are the whole of what is being taken off us.”

Nell wrote it and Mercy read it back and struck one word herself, which was *clerk*, and put in *officer of the Crown*, because, as she said, *clerk* has begun to mean one particular young man with ink on his fingers in this ward, and if that is the word we publish, then the first person to be frightened of is the only person we have got.

They lost on nothing. They compromised on one thing, which was the price, and the price was this.

“The first household to put it in a Crown room becomes a household the Crown has a file on,” Mercy Slee said. “Not a bad file. A file. A line in a book with a name against it, in an office, that says this house asked about its own answer. And that file will sit there. And when the twenty-first comes there will be a woman in a carriage who can read it.”

“I know.”

“You know it the way you know the weather. I have a boy of nine who is on no roll and who is going to stay on no roll for as long as I can manage it, and you are asking me to be the one who puts my door number next to that word first, in public, with my name on it, in front of a clerk, so that four hundred and ten other people can feel safer about something I do not have to be.”

“I am not asking you to do that.”

“You are exactly asking me to do that,” Mercy Slee said, “and you have come to my kitchen to ask me to do it, and you have not asked me yet, because you are sixteen and you have a rule about asking people first, and I want you to know that the rule does not make it not happening.”

Nell sat on the stool with her hands flat on the slate for a while.

“Who is it then?” Mercy Slee said. “Somebody has to be first. If you do it, your house is the first and you are a child with a brother and a father that the Crown has already got a line for, and you will be the girl who asked, and that is your whole life. If I do it, my house is the first, and my house is on nothing.”

“And that is why it has to be you and not me,” Nell said. “That is the whole of the answer and I worked it out at about the eighth hour and I have been sitting on it since. You are on no list. Not the ward’s list, not a roll, not the notice, not my slate. So if the first one to ask is you, then the first household the Crown has a file on is a household it has never had anything about, and there is nothing on them to connect it to, and it starts clean. If the first one is me, then the first file has *Fenn* on it and line twenty-two on it, and the connection is free, and they will have that connection for a year and I will have handed it to them on the fourteenth of this month instead of the twelfth.”

Mercy Slee looked at the door, and at the boy on the stairs, who had come down without a sound and was standing in the kitchen with his thumb in his mouth.

“Nell Fenn,” she said, “I have known you six days and I have watched you do four things this week that I did not know children could do, and I want to say one thing about how you work, and then I will say my piece.

“You ask people first. That is a real thing and it is rare and I have not seen it anywhere else in eleven years, and it is why you are alive and why I am sitting in this kitchen. And I want you to notice what you have just done, because I do not think you have noticed. You did not come to me and say *will you be first*. You came to me and worked out, out loud, in front of me, why it could only be me, and then you handed me the reason as though it were mine. And if I had not been here, you would have worked it out and you would have done it yourself and you would have been quite right to, and that is a worse thing and not a better one.”

“Then do not do it,” Nell said.

“No,” Mercy Slee said. “I am going to do it. Not because you worked it out and handed me a reason. Because there is a thing I have not said in this kitchen and I am going to say it now, and then you can put it on your slate and I will not stop you.”

And she said it.

She said: I have had a Crown officer at this door twice since the tenth with a slate, and both times the slate was for somebody else, and both times I have stood in this doorway and said *that is not my boy, he is not on your paper, there is no paper of yours with this house on it*, and both times he has gone away, and both times I have lain awake afterwards working out what the third time is going to be. And I have worked it out, and the third time is the twenty-first, and the only thing I have that might be worth anything is a question that makes them write down what they did, and if asking it puts a file on this house then the file is on a house that has nothing else on it, and I would rather have a file on this house than be invisible on the twenty-first.

Nell wrote it down. Not on the public slate, on the other one, the small one, and read it back, and Mercy Slee said *that is fair* and *do not put that on the board, girl, that one is mine*.

---

They read it at the ward board at the fourth hour, in daylight, with about forty people, and Nell had chosen the hour and the place for the first time in nine days and she had chosen them because the board is where this ward does things where somebody’s name is on the paper.

Mercy Slee read it. She read it standing up, in a clear voice, in the ordinary voice of a woman of about forty reading a notice in a lane, and when she came to the part about a witness and writing the answer down where the person who asked could see it, she stopped for a second, and said:

“And I am the first. My house is the first, and I have asked to be, and the address is one hundred and nineteen, and you are all going to know that on the twenty-first, and I have said it here first so that nobody has to be brave later than I was.”

Then Nell Fenn read the price underneath it, which she had written out in advance and had asked Mercy Slee to look at before the reading and which is the reason this is a notice and not a grievance, and a notice can be put back up.

*This is a question and not an accusation. It names no officer and no office and no person. It says what a paper has on it and asks a clerk to answer in writing. Any person in this ward who wishes to put it may put it, and no person has to, and nobody has to say why they did not. The first person to put it was Mercy Slee of house one hundred and nineteen, who was on no roll of this Crown office, and who has said so here before anybody else could say it.*

Nell Fenn signed it in her own hand, and Mercy Slee beside her, and then there was a pause of the length of two breaths, during which Oren Pike read the whole thing twice at the back with his hat in his hand and said nothing, and then put his name on the bottom line, which was the first thing he had put his name under in this town that was not a matter of order.

---

The Crown counter was sitting under the arcade because the round was on the twenty-first and they would sit on the twenty-first, and they had come on the eighteenth anyway, because the watch had put a table and a chair in that arcade on the ninth day and nobody had ever withdrawn the compulsion.

There was a clerk behind it and a lamp and a book, and at about the sixth hour of the eighteenth a woman came up the arcade from the market lane, and she was about forty-five, and she was wearing a good coat that had been mended twice, and she stood at the front of the table and she did not look at the book first.

Sena Dunn of house one hundred and twenty-one, who had asked *which child* four times at a door on the tenth day, and who had agreed to a route in open court on the eighth and withdrawn in open court on the eighth, and whose name was in no book in this town, and who had not been at the ward board in nine days and had not been asked to be.

She asked it.

She asked it in the words, and the words were on a slate and she had a copy of the slate, and she had a woman with her who could say what the words meant, because Mercy Slee had insisted on that and had not insisted on anything else.

“Ask the question,” Mercy Slee said. “Say it as it is written.”

Sena Dunn said it as it was written. And the clerk behind the table was Ansel Stroud, and he had a book, and he wrote the house number without being asked, and then he answered, and his answer took four minutes and ten seconds, and it was much longer than a clerk’s answer should have been.

He answered the first part correctly and quickly: the column is for the person present at the answering; the name goes in a hand from the house, not a clerk’s; on a Kells return it is empty.

Then he said: “It is empty on returns from this district. It is not empty on returns from every district, and I am not permitted to tell you which, and that is not evasion, that is a prohibition, and I will tell you that it is a prohibition because you would otherwise think I had made it up.”

He put his hand flat on the book.

“Ma’am — may I say one thing that is a lawyer’s thing and not a clerk’s. On a Kells return it is empty. On a Kells *summary*, which is the paper in your hand, it is not printed at all. Those are two papers and I have just answered you about the one you have not got, and I would like you to be careful in the room with what you do about that, because if the ward publishes this as a question and the Crown produces the summary form, then the Crown will look like a liar and it will not be, and a Crown office that gets away with looking like a liar twice does not get away with it a third time.”

Then he said: “May I say a thing that is not an answer, and that I will not enter, and that I would like the person who asked to hear once from the man behind the table.”

And then he said it.

“In this ward the rounds are conducted at four in the morning, and every Kells return in eleven years that I have filed is empty in that column, and the reason that column is empty in this ward is not that nobody in this ward is ever in a room when an answer is given. It is that there is no room at four in the morning.”

Nobody in the arcade said anything. There were about thirty people under the tiles.

Stroud shut the book.

“I am not going to write that down,” he said, “because if I write it down it is a Crown officer’s opinion about the conduct of a Crown round, and there is a form for that and it is not this form, and I have a family. I have said it once, out loud, in a room, in daylight, and about thirty people have heard it, and I would ask you to decide what to do with it, because I have decided what to do with mine and I cannot do yours.”

Sena Dunn stood at the table for some time.

“Put my number down,” she said at last. “You have put it down already. I saw you do it. I want to leave it there.”

“It is there.”

“Good,” she said. “Then I have been written down, and I have asked, and both of those are true, and my boy is not coming back and I would like a page that says I came to a table on my own feet and asked a question about a form, and if that page is ever read, that is on it.”

She walked back up the lane. There were about thirty people under the tiles and not one of them said anything, because the clerk had put a thing into the air that could not be put back into a box, and everybody in that arcade understood it, and everybody in that arcade also understood that a young man with ink to the second knuckle had just thrown away the only thing he had that nobody could take off him.

Nell Fenn wrote it up that night in her own book, in her own hand, with the hour on it, and she wrote four lines and then stopped and came back and wrote a fifth, and the fifth was this:

*I did not choose the first household and I want that written because I have been telling people for nine days that choosing is the whole of the craft. I chose a reading. Mercy Slee chose to stand up. A woman I have never spoken to walked up to a table on her own feet and said a question she had copied off a slate, and she is in a Crown book now, and she is not on the ward’s list and was not on the Crown’s, and there is nothing about her anywhere except that she asked. I have been the girl with the slate for nine days and this evening the slate was hers and I was the one holding it, and that is the best thing that has happened to me in a month and I do not know yet whether it is a good thing.*

The amendment went up on the board at the eighth hour that evening, in daylight, with about twenty people at the step, and Nell read it out and then read the whole of what had happened in the arcade, which was that the first household in the ward to put the question to a Crown clerk was not one anybody had chosen, and that the clerk had told thirty people in public that the column is not printed on the summary form at all, and had asked the ward to be careful with what it did about that. And under the amendment, in Mercy Slee’s hand, there is a household number, an hour, and four words of answer.
