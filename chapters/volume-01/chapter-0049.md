# Chapter 0049 — What Burns

At about the tenth hour Crown Officer Rudd produced a fifth thing.

He had said at the ninth hour that he had produced four things and that the third of them was spent, and he said that in public and at his own cost, and then at about the tenth hour he came back into the market court with a schedule in a different hand from the other four, and read it out entire, standing up, with about two hundred and forty people in the room and the rain coming in under the arcade tiles where the fish market’s gutter has been wrong for eleven years.

*FIFTH SCHEDULE, L-4 matter, district of the northern sub-district. To the officer of the commission attending at the ward of Kells on the twenty-eighth day. The records of the workshop of the Vale Bellhouse, being the working books, the cut book, the bench-books, the postings, the witness-matter register and the record of bells declared, are Crown material within the L-4 matter, the same being the record of a Crown instrument received into that workshop on the first day of the month, and are to be taken, copied, and such copies retained; and the originals of the said books are to be destroyed in the yard of the workshop, in daylight, in the presence of the ward, in accordance with the practice of this office, the hour of the destruction to be entered by a copyist.*

There is a clause in that. It is not a small one and nobody in that room drew attention to it, and it is the clause this whole month has been about, and it is the reason the sentence at the end of this day is the sentence at the end of this day.

It says the originals are to be destroyed. It does not say they are to be removed. It says the hour is to be entered by a copyist.

And Officer Rudd read that schedule out in a full market court and then stood aside, and the district’s copyist took out a book, and Rudd did not say one word about it either way, and afterwards, in the lane, when a carter’s wife asked him whether it was right, he said four words which are on the page because Tomas Vale had them down within an hour of hearing them: *It is a form. It is a schedule. It is right.*

---

The removal of the persons named on the return was done by four Crown officers between the tenth hour and the second hour of the afternoon, and the watch took part in it in exactly one way, and Captain Pike has entered that way in the gate book and would not have it described any other way in any room.

“Captain,” Rudd said to him at the tenth hour, in the arcade, with the rain coming in, “I am taking six people out of this ward on the return of this morning, and the six are named, and the grounds are on the round’s book, and I am going to read you the grounds. And I am going to ask you for the copy of the arrest for each of them, because a Crown office gives one and it is the only paper any of them will have, and I would rather it were a watch captain’s hand than mine.”

“I will give you a copy.”

“Not the arrest. The record of it. In your hand. With the hour and the ground and the place.” Rudd had the round’s book open. “I am asking you for the thing you have spent this month asking other people for, and I am asking you for it on a morning when it is going to cost you something, and I would like it noticed that I am asking, and that I could produce six arrests without asking a single question of the watch of this town, and that a return of this morning says the ward’s bells are unaccounted for and that the return does not say the watch is on good terms with the Crown.”

“Why are you asking.”

“Because at the seventh hour yesterday evening you wrote down that the bells of this ward are not accounted for by any person, and because you were talking about a register of yours, and because a person who keeps a book ought to know what is done with the book,” Rudd said. “And because I would like one document in this ward on the twenty-eighth of this month that is not a schedule.”

Oren Pike wrote six records. He wrote them himself, and he wrote the ground, the hour, the place, the officer’s name, the name of the person, and — because that is what he has been entering into books for nineteen days — the fact that the person was told, and the fact that a reason was or was not given, and the fact that the person was not given an opportunity to speak, where that was the case, which it was in four of the six.

He gave a copy to each of the six as they went, and the copy said *taken on a witnessed return* or *taken under a Crown works order* or *taken as the household of record upon a line in the* Witnessed by *column*, and the last of those had a dead woman’s name in it and a living woman’s daughter in a wagon on the north road, and Mrs Wilde read her own record on the step of her own house with a lamp, and said one sentence about it, which was: *You have written down that I was told. That is the first time in two years that anybody has written down that I was told.* And she was taken down the lane to the north gate, on foot, with about thirty people walking beside her and not saying anything, and she went like that because she had been made the only household in Kells in two years that had a line in a column and a margin and a record of being told, and she was not going to be the only household in Kells in two years that got taken quietly.

The six, with the grounds, as they are on the page:

**Gib Hask**, gate-porter and rope-maker, of the low turn, under a Crown works order of the second day of this month, for taking out ninety feet of backfill above the ward’s drain between the second hour and the fourth hour of the morning, with a bar and a spade, and the Crown’s own record of the works order says the cart across the culvert was to be removed on the eighth day and was not.

**Mrs Wilde**, of one hundred and eight, as the household of record upon a line in the *Witnessed by* column, the line being one which the district office had entered in a return and could not produce a living witness to.

**Wenna Cray**, keeper of the ward board, entered by the watch captain of this ward on the seventeenth day of this month as having public standing in matters of signalling, and by the watch captain on the twenty-eighth as having withdrawn a duty in public and by her own hand, on the ground stated in the withdrawal.

**Ivo Prentice**, of the ferry road, entered on the same list of the seventeenth for a petition made to the commission, and for having stated in a public room that the Crown’s cart remained across the culvert at the low turn seventeen days after the works order for its removal.

**Louisa Tace**, of forty-four, a house that has never been marked and that has never had a Crown slate on its door, entered upon a return of this morning in consequence of having put the *Witnessed by* question of the twenty-third day to an officer of the Crown at her own door, which question is in the district’s file, and her answer to it, which is: *There is a name in that column on my line. It is my elder sister’s. She died in the spring. No person has ever come to this door and I would like somebody to.*

And **Absalom Teage**, of one hundred and fourteen, entered upon the same list of the seventeenth for having said a thing in a market court on the twentieth day of this month, and for having returned the second supplementary return in his own hand on the twenty-second, and the record of his arrest, made by the watch captain of Kells, in his own hand, with the hour, says this: *That at the eighth hour and twenty minutes of this morning this household produced to an officer of the Crown its own return of the twenty-second day, in which the household stated the removal of the person named therein, and that no removal of any person from this household was made. And that the person named in that return is this household’s child of the age of nine years.*

There is a Crown document about Absalom Teage that says his son has already left the ward, and there is a Crown document about Absalom Teage that says he was taken out of it at the second hour of the afternoon, and both of them are true, and the first of them is in a Crown’s own hand in a round’s own book, and there is no field anywhere on either sheet that can hold both facts at once.

Nobody in Kells made those two facts into a sentence. They tried. Nell Fenn tried for two days and set it down and read it back to herself and struck it out, and what she has in the ward’s book is four lines, and the fourth line says: *And I have tried for two days to make this into one sentence and I cannot, and I am not going to pretend to anybody that I can, and I am going to leave it as two facts with a date against each of them, which is the whole of what this ward has learned to do in a month and is not enough and is the best thing anybody here has.*

---

And at about the second hour of the afternoon, in the market court, a Crown officer read out the list of the six persons taken, in a Crown hand, out of a Crown list, in a full room.

Every one of the six had been entered in a district file in the last three weeks, and Nell Fenn, sitting at the clerk’s table with a slate, counted them as they were read, and put her hand over her mouth, and did not say a word in a public room for about a minute, because all six of those names had been on the district’s own amended return of the seventeenth day, in Oren Pike’s flat hand, with a date against each one, under a heading the Crown had written for itself in its own margin on the evening of the seventeenth.

It was for that. The whole of the seventeen days of that document was for that, so that on the twenty-eighth a Crown officer could read six names out in a market court and every person in the room would know where they had got them from and would not be able to say.

Oren Pike was the watch captain who had supplied them, in twenty minutes, on the seventeenth day, because they were the true ones and he would not lie in a return. He was standing at the side of that court with his cap under his arm, and Tomas Vale came and stood beside him for about a minute, and neither of them said anything, and then Oren said, to nobody:

“That is the whole of it. I did that on the seventeenth because it was true and because the alternative was a return that said *refused* and a refused return is read as a ward with nothing to declare, and I taught this town to declare things, and I have not once in nineteen days asked myself what declaring things looks like from the other end of a satchel.”

And he took out the book and wrote into the gate book, in his own hand, in about nine minutes, and it is in three books by the evening, and it is the paragraph he has never taken out and has never improved:

*That I sent eleven names of this ward to a district office on the seventeenth day with the dates, and that the district retained the list and amended the heading in its own hand, and that on the twenty-eighth day six persons were taken out of this ward whose names are on that list, and that no other person was taken, and that the six are the six, and that I am the man who put them in a column with a date against it, and that a list of persons who have spoken in public in a ward in the month of a round is a list of persons who can be removed from a ward, and that I said that sentence to Captain Pike in this court in words on the seventeenth day and I said it in the wrong tense.*

*And the second is that I would not lie in a return, and I did not, and that is correct, and it is the whole of the trouble with being correct, and there is no version of the seventeenth day in which Captain Pike refused and no harm came to anybody, and I have had nineteen days to find one and there is not one, and I am not going to be a man who explains it away, because the district did not forge anything and did not want the six. It asked for a list of persons having public standing in matters of signalling, with dates, and I gave it a list of persons having public standing in matters of signalling, with dates, and it went where a list of persons having public standing in matters of signalling, with dates, goes.*

---

**House one hundred and eight was marked again at about the second hour and a half and no child was taken from it, and the reason is the fourth thing this district has done to that door in a fortnight and the only one of the four that was not a Crown clerk’s.**

Mrs Wilde read the form aloud in the lane, in daylight, with about thirty people in it, for the second time in a week, and the line said five entered and five returned and the *Witnessed by* column held her mother-in-law’s name in her mother-in-law’s hand, given at that door two autumns ago and carried forward through every round since, and the column was not cleared between rounds, and the name could not be struck out and had not been struck out.

The officer entered her objection in the margin in his own name and initialled it, in front of Surveyor Collis, and then — and this is on the page and it is in Collis’s return — **asked her to countersign the objection and not the answer.** And she countersigned the objection. In a margin. In a lane. In daylight. A Crown surveyor of the northern district with a seal put her name on the margin of a household’s objection and did not put it anywhere near the answer, and wrote in her own return that she had done so and why, and the why is one sentence and the sentence is: *because an objection is a fact about a door and an answer is a claim about a person, and I will sign the first and not the second, and the district can have whichever it can get.*

Mrs Wilde said one thing about that, in the lane, and it was: “Then the column is not empty. I have been telling people for two years that the column is empty and it is not empty, and nobody has ever cared whether it was empty, and now there is a surveyor’s name on my line in a hand I have never seen in my life, in a margin, in a lane, and there is my mother-in-law’s hand in the column, and the surveyor is alive and my mother-in-law is not, and I do not know which of the two of them is going to matter in a year, and both of them are going to be in two books in this town and one in Fourmile, and my daughter is ten years old and on a wagon somewhere north of here, and I would like somebody to tell me what I am supposed to do with that.”

Nobody could, and about nine people tried, and the answer she got was from Mrs Corris of one hundred and thirty-three, who said, in a shawl, that she had been in a full market court two days ago and had put her own name in the same column on purpose and it had not saved anything, and the two women stood in the lane together and did not agree about a single thing and neither of them was wrong.

---

And in the market court at about the third hour of the afternoon, on the way up the lane with the crowd, before a bellmaker’s bench-book was being torn from, a Crown officer produced an object in oiled linen and set it on the clerk’s table in a full market court and unwrapped it, and it was the trial handbell, in its ash frame, and it had not been rung once in eighteen days and it was not going to be rung, and about two hundred people in that room looked at a bell that had been taken out of a workshop on a handcart between two watchmen at twenty-five minutes to four in the morning on the tenth day of this month.

“Do you identify this object.”

“Yes,” Tomas Vale said, from the side of the room, some hours before he was arrested. “It is my trial handbell. It is Crown property in a district building since the tenth. It was bound when it was taken and it has not been rung and it has not been unspent. I have a receipt for it and it is in my coat and it has a correction of mine in the margin and the Crown copyist’s initial under the correction, and the initial is not agreement.”

The Crown officer looked at him.

“Your receipt is in your coat, Mr Vale.”

“It is.”

“Then I am not going to take it off you in a full court. I am entering the object as produced, and I am entering your statement, and it will be read in a district office, and there is a surveyor in this room who will want to see it.”

And Surveyor Rhea Collis looked at the bell on the table for about a minute, and then said, in a full market court, to about two hundred people, that she was an accredited surveyor of the northern district with a seal, and that she had examined the object, and that she would record in her own return, unasked, that a bell of which the maker of the Vale Bellhouse has produced a custody receipt with a correction in the margin in his own hand is in this court, exhibited, on the twenty-eighth day of this month, by a Crown officer, as a document, and that it was not rung, and that no person in that room heard it, and that she could not tell from where she was standing whether the paper on the table or the argument about the paper was the more frightening, and that she is a surveyor and it is not her trade and she is obliged to write it down.

The bell went back into the oiled linen. It did not go back into the Bellhouse, and it was never returned to the Bellhouse, and the last time anybody in the county of Mereward saw it inside a workshop it was in a lane on a handcart in the rain with two watchmen holding the shafts.


---

The Crown’s party went up the Bellhouse lane at about the half past three of the afternoon, four men and a copyist, and there were about a hundred and forty people at the top of that lane by the half past three, in the rain, and they stood in the yard and in the lane and nobody was asked to move and nobody was moved.

They took the books off the shelf first. The three quarto volumes and the ledger that had been on a table in daylight on the afternoon of the twenty-seventh day, in front of forty people and a copyist who had written *taken from a shelf in the yard at the fifth hour* and had not opened one of them.

Then they took the cut book, and the witness-matter register, and the postings, and the record of bells declared, and the whole of the L-4 working papers, and the copy of the notice of the second day with the words *answered in office, L-4 ward roll, per local practice* on it, and Tomas gave every one of those up himself, off the shelf, in order, with a hand that did not shake, and put them in the copyist’s satchel as they were called for, and he said the name of each one as it went in, out loud, so that about a hundred and forty people in a yard in the rain could hear what was being taken out of his father’s shop, and the copyist wrote the names and Tomas did not ask to see the list afterwards and was right not to.

And then the copyist came to the bench-books.

There were four of them. They are the working books of a shop that has been in the same three rooms since before the Crown laid a mile of copper, and they are not Crown material in anybody’s ordinary sense, and the schedule said *bench-books*, and there was a year in the second of them, and a year in the third.

“Those are the shop’s,” Tomas said.

“They are named in the schedule,” the copyist said, and he did not sound pleased about it and he did not sound sorry either, and he said it the way a man says a thing he has been told to say, and then he added, out loud, in a yard with a hundred and forty people in it, the only sentence of his own he has ever said: “I have never had a schedule with a year in it in it before. I am writing the year down because it is in the schedule, and I want it understood that I am writing it down because it is in the schedule.”

The year was Reckoning 619.

And it is about three hundred and eleven pages, and it is the only book in this county that anybody can now produce which was written in the year of the Hush by the hands that worked through it, and it is being destroyed in a yard in daylight because a district in a real building in a real town has put the word *bench-books* in the fifth line of a fifth schedule, and neither of those two things was the district’s purpose and one of them is the reason for it.

---

And the copyist did not take the second of the four out of the satchel. He took it out, and read the head of it, which is a page of accounts, in a yard, in daylight, in front of a hundred and forty people, and then he said, to the Crown officer, not to the crowd:

“I am not a man who reads books. I am a copyist and I copy what I am given. There is no form for this and I am not going to make one up in a yard.”

And Officer Rudd, who had come up the lane with the party and had been standing at the yard gate since the half past three because he did not come further in, said:

“Captain Pike. Fetch a butt.”

And while the butt was being fetched, a thing happened in that yard that took about nine seconds and is in three books and is the reason this town does not end this month with a Crown office owning every word anybody in Kells wrote this month.

Watchman Jerm had the watch’s duplicate of the seven-day bell register open on the bench, in his own hand, because the Crown party had come up the lane and he had brought the book to the yard to show the hour and the place of every bell struck in this ward between the seventeenth and the twenty-fourth days of this month, as a watch keeper may and is instructed to, and it was open at the nineteenth, which is the page with twelve entries on it, and in the column for the person who sounded it, twelve times, the single word *none*, each of them in a clerk’s hand and each of them initialled.

A Crown officer came in with an armful of the L-4 working papers, which is what he had been told to collect, and he put the armful down on the bench, on top of the open register, because his hands were full and there was nowhere else in a bellmaker’s yard to put four inches of paper.

Bevin Sarr was standing four feet inside the yard gate, which he had come past about nine inches in an afternoon and where he had been told by a Crown officer that he would be asked to move on, and which he had not done. He reached over the bench, took the corner of a watch page between two fingers, and lifted it clear of the Crown’s armful, and handed it to the girl standing on his other side, who was Nell Fenn and who took it because her hands were empty.

That is the whole of it. It took nine seconds and it was not theft, because a man taking a sheet of a watch’s own duplicate register off a bench in a yard in daylight, in front of the district’s copyist, is not stealing Crown material, and it was not conspiracy, because nobody said a word to anybody and there was nothing to say to, and it was not planned by anybody at all, including the man who did it, who has said since that he did it because his hands were not idle and because he has not been still in a doorway in nineteen days.

The district’s copyist wrote down that four inches of Crown working papers had been set down on a bench, and did not write down anything else about it, because the armful stayed on the bench and was not taken and the page went out of the yard in a different hand between four different people and the copyist had ninety-one other things to write down in the rain that afternoon, and he was about forty and he had a cough.

Nell Fenn had the nineteenth of the watch’s duplicate register in her coat for about six hours and did not know that she had it, and at about the tenth hour of the evening she found it in the pocket of a coat on a chair in the front room of a house in the market lane, and read the twelve entries, and read the word *none* twelve times, and took it down to a net-maker’s shop at about the eleventh hour and gave it to Bevin Sarr, and said, to his wife, standing in a doorway, four words which are on the page because he made her write them down:

“He is not on the list. Nobody will ask him where he got it.”

And the butt was the ash-pit in the corner of the yard, which is the pit his father and he have used for twenty-two years to cool a founder in and to burn a pattern off a failed mould in, and it is a stone-lined pit four feet across and it is dry, and it is the correct place to burn a paper, and that is the whole of the cruelty of the afternoon: there was a correct place to burn it and it was in the yard already.

They burned it in three goes, in the open, at about the fourth hour, and the copyist wrote the hour against each go and the number of pages, and about a hundred and forty people stood in the rain and watched, and nobody sang anything and nobody cheered and nobody booed, and the rain made the fire very hard to see and then very easy.

Tomas Vale stood in his own yard, four feet off the ash-pit, with his hands at his sides, and did not ring anything, and did not ask anybody to stop it, and did not go up the hill to Sabin Fell or down to the watch house or into the market court, and he set down the hour, and the three goes, and the page numbers, and the rain, in his day-book, in the flat hand, at the time.

He was asked about it four times by people who were not the Crown.

Mercy Slee asked him at about the fourth hour, in the lane, in the rain, with a lamp in her hand and her shawl over her head, whether he was going to do anything.

“No.”

“You cast a bell on the nineteenth that emptied a market lane.”

“I did.”

“You have a bell in that yard and a keeper at the low gate and the district has a page about it in Fourmile and it is twenty-five minutes past the fourth hour and the Crown is in your yard.”

“Yes,” Tomas said.

“That is not an answer to a question.”

“It is the answer to that question,” he said. “If I ring it now — and I want you to hear this properly and then I am going to go and stand in the rain again — it will go to a district office in Fourmile and a man there will read it, and what he will read is a bellmaker standing in a yard while a Crown burns his father’s books, and that man will be right about the bell and right about the fire, and he will write a page that says the bells of this ward are unaccounted for, and it will be true, and it will be the best evidence in the whole file, and every person in this ward who put anything down this month will be read by somebody who has been handed a reason to think the whole of it was a bell.”

He put the day-book down against the wall so it would not get wet.

“**A bell cannot stop a fire. And a bellmaker who thinks it can has stopped being a maker.** I have had a month to work that out and I worked it out in about nine seconds in a lane about twelve hours ago, standing next to a man who took ninety feet of backfill out of a Crown trench with a bar at four in the morning, and he did not stop a flood either, and the gate came up, and it is also what the Crown will put in a return tomorrow.”

“You have let them burn it.”

“Yes,” Tomas said. “I have stood in my own yard and let them burn the year six hundred and nineteen, and there is nothing in this town that will stop them, and the last two people I have ever been able to lean on told me in the last fortnight that they would not, and they were both right, and if I stand in this yard and do something about it then I am doing it for me, and every person at the top of this lane is standing here for free, and I am the only one in the yard with a bell.”

And he wrote the day’s record in the same three columns he had used on the twenty-first day — *the first thing done, the second thing, the third thing* — and it is thirty-one lines, and it is checkable at any door, and he did not keep it.

He wrote it in the yard and he gave it to Mercy Slee, and she read the first line and looked up at him, and he said the reason, and she said the reason out loud for him, because she is the person in this ward who will say a man’s own reason back to him before he does.

“You have given away every page this month,” she said.

“Yes.”

“And you have kept the day-book, which is the same thing in a worse binding.”

“Yes,” Tomas Vale said, “and if a man in Fourmile ever wants to know what this yard did on the twenty-eighth of this month he will have to come and ask a person, and that is the whole of what I have got, and I am not going to be a man who improves it.”

---

He tore a page out of the running bench-book at about the fourth hour and a quarter, while the ash-pit was being raked out, and it was the last page of the running entries and it has the weight at the mouth and the weight at the shoulder on it, and the difference, and the two faults, and nineteen days of hours against it, and his own name four times.

He wrote at the top of it, in his own hand, and he signed it, which he had not intended to do when he tore it out and did it anyway: *Taken out of the workshop of the Vale Bellhouse on the twenty-eighth day of this month, in daylight, in the presence of the ward. Given by Tomas Vale, maker, in the yard, at about the fourth hour and a quarter, to Bevin Sarr, of this ward, of my own act and without his being asked.*

And he put it in the man’s hand.

Bevin Sarr had been in the ward since the half past three, in the rain, and had not come further into the yard than the gate, and had not been asked by anybody to be there, and had refused three times in this month to be an invited witness, to be on a public route, and to give an account in a room, and his name is not on the district’s list of the seventeenth day and never was, and he said so himself, to nobody, in the lane, on the nineteenth, in a way that Oren Pike wrote into the gate book: *that Bevin Sarr holds no public standing in matters of signalling and is on no list, because he has declined three times to be on one, and that this is a fact about his character and not about his craft.*

“All right,” Sarr said, and took the page, and folded it into four, and put it inside his coat against his chest, and did not say a word about it, and that was the correct response and Tomas Vale had not known until that moment that he had known what the correct response would be.

“Not one word to anybody,” Sarr said, eventually, to nobody. “Not my wife, not the net shop, not the eleven. And if anybody comes and asks me, I have never had it and I do not know what it is. And you will not tell them either.”

“I will not tell them.”

“Good,” said Bevin Sarr. “And now go and be arrested properly, because they are going to do it in about an hour and I would rather a man did it standing up than sitting in a chair.”

---

The sealing of the Vale Bellhouse was done at about the fifth hour of the afternoon by a Crown officer with an inventory of the objects in the yard, and the inventory is four lines long, and it is the shortest inventory in the history of a shop that has been in the same three rooms for a century.

*The plain carrier, six and a half inches at the mouth, marked VALE KELLS, unstruck since the morning of this day, not in its frame.*

*The trial handbell. Not in this yard. Not entered.*

*The millrace bridge bell. Not in this yard. At the north gate of this town, under a condition of the watch of the twenty-seventh day, and to be returned.*

*The books, taken and destroyed at the fourth hour.*

The plain carrier was taken out of the frame and put on the bench and the frame was put down beside it, and then the door was sealed, and the seal is a Crown wax with the district’s number on it, and Captain Pike put the watch’s own mark on it under the wax, because a Crown seal on a door in a Crown town is not a lock unless the watch is in it, and he did that on his own initiative and entered it, and it is the last entry of the twenty-eighth day in the gate book of Kells and it is nineteen words long and it does not say what he thought about it.

And Tomas Vale was taken at about the sixth hour of the evening, out of his own yard, in his own boots, on a Crown form, and the form is one side of paper and it names four things and the fourth is *the papers, books, records and memoranda of the said workshop, together with any bell, frame, tool and material found therein*, and there was nothing in the yard to take except a bench, a bar of ash, a stump of a founder, a frame with no bell in it, and a floor with a trench in it that had been filled in on the twenty-seventh day by nine men in the rain.

He asked for one thing and got it, and it is in the record and it is the only thing he asked for.

He asked to see the custody receipt.

And the Crown officer who had it in a satchel gave it to him, and Tomas Vale read the margin, in the lane, in the rain, in front of about sixty people, in a Crown’s own hand, and it said *Object received bound. No answer recorded at taking. Maker has not certified the binding*, and under it, a Crown copyist’s initial, which is not agreement, and has never been agreement, and which is now the only document in this town that anybody can hold that says a Crown office once read a bellmaker’s correction and did not agree with it.

He was taken down the Bellhouse lane to the north gate between the sixth hour and the seventh, on foot, and about nine people walked at his side and the rest of the ward stood at the top of the lane in the rain, and the ward board was still up with the district’s four notices on it and Nell Fenn’s three columns and the pinned schedule with the drawing pin through the drawing pin, and the Crown did not take the board down, because the board is not Crown material, and the copyist wrote that on the board as well: *the ward board and its papers are not produced under this form.*

Nobody rang anything.

The millrace bridge bell came off the north gate bracket at about the fifth hour of the afternoon and went back up the hill to a yard with a wax on the door, and Jerm sounded it once on the way, at the bottom of the lane, unasked, with no claim in it and no duty and no keeper but himself, and it was the last bell rung in Kells in this month and there is no record of it anywhere, because the seven-day register was returned to the district on the twenty-fourth and the watch is not required to begin another one, and Oren Pike wrote in the gate book that the bell was sounded once at the fifth hour of the afternoon of the twenty-eighth by Watchman Jerm and not by any keeper and under no duty, and that he was not going to ask Jerm why, and that the reason, if anybody ever wanted it, was in Jerm.

And the last thing out of that yard on the twenty-eighth day of this month was not a bell.

It was a page.
