# Chapter 0039 — The Form, Exhibited

The first thing Nell Fenn did on the twentieth day was get a piece of paper in front of a Crown clerk before the eighth hour, and the paper said one thing and it said it in the words of the Crown’s own notice of the seventh evening, which was the only kind of paper that had ever worked in this ward.

*NOTICED on the seventh evening: that at the renewal upon route L-4 the form is to be exhibited in full and any person admitted as a witness. THE ROUND IS ON THE TWENTY-FIRST DAY. I AM ASKING THAT THE FORM BE EXHIBITED TODAY, IN A ROOM, IN DAYLIGHT, TO ANY PERSON WHO COMES. — N. Fenn, at the board.*

She read it out twice at the board and then she carried it herself, on foot, to the Crown counter under the arcade, and put it in front of the clerk, and the clerk was not Stroud and she noted that and did not mind.

The answer came back in forty minutes and it was a good answer and it was the right answer and it is the answer a Crown office gives.

*EXHIBITED, the tenth hour, this day, at the market court, in the room in which the renewal is to be held on the twenty-first day, on the ground that the room will be the same room on both days. Any person may be present. Admission is by the door. The form will be exhibited entire and may not be removed, and may be read from the floor and may not be touched.*

Nell read it four times and found nothing in it to fight and wrote the four lines of it into the ward’s book and then, because she was sixteen and had learned in nine days that a thing that is not written on a page has not happened, she went and told the eleven people who needed to be in a room at the tenth hour on the following day, and then told about ninety more that there was one.

They came. Not as many as the fourteenth. The ward was smaller than it was on the fourteenth: two houses empty, four people gone to Fourmile, and a residue in the middle of it that had stopped coming out at all, and about a hundred and thirty people came, and a hundred and thirty is not nothing and it is not two hundred. Nell had a slate and wrote down the names of forty of them and gave up on the rest, and the forty is the number the Crown’s door-round came back with on the following evening, and she has thought about that more than once.

---

They got the form at the tenth hour and it took forty minutes and it was the most alarming thing that had happened in this market court in three weeks, because it was completely honest.

The Crown exhibited the **summary form**. It was the form every household in the ward had been given at the posting. It was on the table, entire, and it could be read from the floor, and Ferrow the market court clerk read out every heading on it in order, in a flat voice, and the crowd listened, because everybody in that room had been handed this piece of paper and had been asked to fill in a column on it.

And on that form, there was no column headed *Witnessed by*.

“There is no such column on this form,” said the Crown officer at the table, comfortably, because it was true. “That is the form given to this ward. I am instructed that it is exhibited entire, and it is entire, and there is nothing on it that is not on it. The renewal tomorrow is conducted on this form.”

Nell stood up at the back of the room, on the step, where she always stood, and said:

“Ask him whether that is the form our board’s page came off.”

And the Crown officer said, “I am not aware which form any particular page was copied from.”

“Then you will not mind me saying what I know about it, and the ward can check it, and nobody has to take my word.” She read the heading off her slate. *ROUTE L-4 — RESPONSES ENTERED BEFORE SERVICE.* And she said: “That page is not a summary. It is six lines deep where this one is one. It has a persons field and this one has a households field. And it has a row under the last rule that is not a house. So either there is a longer form in that building, or somebody wrote a page that was not a form, and I do not care which, and I would like to know, and everybody in this room would like to know, because one of those two is a document and the other one is a man.”

The room did the thing a room does. It made a noise.

And the Crown officer at the table, who was a district officer of about forty with a Crown inkhorn and no black case, looked at the door and then at the district clerk, and said:

“The full return is a Crown document and it is exhibited on request at the counter and not in a court.”

“You exhibited it in a court,” Nell said. “You said *in a room, in daylight, to any person who comes*, and there is no room in this town with a door that a Crown office does not come through, and you have come through it, and you have sat down at a table in it, and I have got forty names on a slate of who came in at the tenth hour.”

And then Ansel Stroud stood up.

He had not been at the front. He had been at the side with a satchel, and he had been at the side for about eleven minutes with his hands flat on the table, and when he stood up he did it in the manner of a man who has decided something and is going to be sick afterwards.

“The full return for this round is in that satchel,” he said. “I brought it. I was not going to put it on that table and I have brought it.”

“Serjeant — ”

“No, let him,” said the district clerk, and sat down again.

Stroud carried it to the table himself and put it down and squared the top edge to the edge of the table with both hands, and then he stood back two paces, which is what a man does with a thing he has decided to hand over.

“Ferrow. Read the headings of the full return, in order, entire, and then read the heading of the ninth column aloud, because I have asked the ward to be shown a column and I have not got the right form out, and that is my fault and not theirs.”

Ferrow read the headings. And then he read the ninth, in a flat voice, and the room heard it.

*Witnessed by.*

“Printed,” Stroud said. “It is printed on the form. It is printed on every full return in this district. It is empty on this one, and it is empty on the summary, because a summary form does not carry it, and because a summary form does not carry the reason field either, and the reason field is the one that is not on either piece of paper in front of you, and I am not going to say a word about that, and I am going to have to go back and work out this afternoon whether I have just said a word about it.”

Nobody made a sound.

Sena Dunn was at the side of the room with Mercy Slee, and she had a slate, and Mercy Slee had made her copy it before they came in, and Mercy Slee did that on purpose and had said so on the step.

“A household,” Stroud said, and his voice went slightly, “may put a name in that column. The name goes in a hand from the house. Not a clerk’s hand. The name of a person who was in the room when the answer was given.”

---

That was where it broke, and it broke in the direction nobody in the ward had prepared for, and it broke out of a frightened clerk who had been up since four.

“Then put mine in,” somebody said from the middle of the floor.

It was a woman nobody in the ward board had ever seen, and she was standing on her own with a shawl over her head, and Ferrow had to ask her twice for her name before she gave it.

*Corris. House one hundred and thirty-three.*

And the room made a noise, because the ward knew what one hundred and thirty-three was, and everybody in that room had known it for ten days and had said nothing, because on the tenth of this month an officer had come to that door and read a line off a page that said *two returned, one accompanying*, and had taken a child of thirteen out of it, and the household had given no answer and no name, and nobody had been back to that door since, including Oren Pike, and the reason nobody had been back was that a household which gives no answer and no name is a household that can be handled without difficulty.

Mrs Corris said: “I have wanted to ask somebody something for ten days. I have had nothing to ask with. I have got my grandson’s name and I have got nothing else, and I came here this morning to hear a form read out.”

Nobody in the room told her to sit down.

She said: “Is it true there is a place on it for a person who was in the room?”

“It is,” Stroud said. “For a household on this route, for the round tomorrow, I can enter a name in that column. Today. On this form. It is the ordinary use of the column and it is the first time in eleven years I have been asked to use it, and I will enter it this afternoon if the house names a person, and the name will be in the house’s own hand, and it will be on the return, and it will be read by an office and not by me.”

Mrs Corris stood there and looked at the table, and Tomas Vale, who had been standing at the wall for two hours without speaking, said afterwards that it was the only moment in nine days in which he understood that he had not been the man who had been standing between this ward and the Crown at all.

“Mercy Slee,” Mrs Corris said, without turning round. “Your boy is not on their paper. If I put your name in it, they have a witness, and the witness is a person, and the person is you, and then they have got a woman with a child of nine at a hundred and nineteen, and you have said every day for a week that your house is on nothing.”

“Then I will not give your grandson’s name for nothing,” Mercy Slee said. “Give it yourself. You were in the room.”

“I was in the room when the officer read the paper,” Mrs Corris said, “and I have not got a word to put in a column, because what was in the room was a man saying a sentence and not one of us answering.”

So she said, to the room, in a shawl, in a market court, at the fourth hour of the twentieth day: *my name is Corris, of house one hundred and thirty-three, and I am putting my own name in the column, because I was there, and because it is my house and I am sixty-five years old and I am not frightened of a book.*

Stroud wrote it, in the room, in the margin, in his own name and initials, and it is the second time in this business that a Crown clerk has written something in a margin in a public room, and the ward counted, and there were two, and the first of them was a carter’s.

---

The Crown then offered to fill the column for the whole ward, and it did it in the afternoon, and it did it in the way a Crown office does a thing: by producing a form.

*SUPPLEMENTARY RETURN, ROUTE L-4. TO BE DELIVERED TO EVERY HOUSEHOLD BEFORE THE CLOSE OF THE RENEWAL. FIELDS: the household’s objection to its own line, in its own words; the name of a person present at the answering, in a household’s hand; the reason the answer was entered in office, in a household’s words. No signature is required. A household may return nothing.*

It is, on its face, an answer to everything Nell Fenn had wanted for eight days. The reason field is on it. The witness is on it. The objection is in the household’s own words and no signature is needed and a household may return nothing at all.

It is the best instrument the Crown has put in front of this ward and it is also the trap, and everybody in the ward knew it was the trap inside a minute, and what it cost to know it was the whole of the afternoon.

Because there is a field for the reason, and there is nothing to prevent an office filling that field. There is nothing to say which hand wrote it, because the form does not ask. And a return that goes up with a name in the witness column and a reason in the reason column and an objection in the objection column is a return that reads, in an office, exactly like a return on which the household answered in a room in front of a person, and there is no field anywhere on it that says *this was entered in office and here is a Crown hand doing it*, and the ward’s entire achievement of the last twenty days would be to fill in blanks that the Crown designed, in the Crown’s form, and hand it back framed.

“Nell,” Mercy Slee said, at the side of the step, in about nine words, “they have put the box and the label on the same shelf.”

The refusal was made at the fifth hour, in the market court, with about ninety people in it, and it was made in three columns, and nobody merged them, and it was written on the ward’s page and signed by three households and dated.

**Mercy Slee, of house one hundred and nineteen.** *That the ward will not have a Crown hand write a name in a Crown book about a Crown answering. A name in that column has to be in the house’s hand, and so does the reason, and if the Crown will put both in a hand we can check, we will read it. If the Crown will not, then the column is a place to write a name and we decline to fill it, and that is not an objection to the column, it is an objection to a name we cannot check.*

**Absalom Teage, of house one hundred and fourteen.** *That my household’s line was read to me on the thirteenth day and written in your margin in your own hand, and I have that, and I want you to understand that it is the only page in this business I have ever been able to hold up. I am not going to have a second page written about my house in a book I never see, in exchange for a box. And I will say the thing nobody wants: the column is not the problem. The missing reason field is the problem, and you have just put the reason field on a form that arrives after the round, and a form that arrives after the round is a form for the next one, and the next one is a week away and mine is tomorrow. Put the reason on the form you hand a house and I will fill in every field you have with my own hand and my wife’s and put my name on all three.*

**The market court usher, keeper of the court call bell.** *That the bell of this court is not on your form and is not going to be, and that I am its keeper, and that I said three things about it in this room on the fourteenth day and I am not going to say a fourth, and if somebody in a district office wishes to know what a bell in a loft with a rope and a striker on a thong is joined to, the answer is to a wall, and the wall is a wall.*

Ferrow read all three out. He read them out in three columns, in a full room, exactly as Oren Pike had made him read three positions on the fourteenth, and then he entered that the market court record contains a refusal, dated, with three signatories, and that the refusal is itself a public act of the ward and may be read out at the sitting of the twenty-first.

---

The district officer named Ambrose Rudd offered the other thing at the sixth hour, and he offered it well, and nobody in the room was able to refuse it comfortably, which was the point of it.

He stood up where an objection belongs, at the front, and he did not object. He said:

“I have entered an objection against this ward’s practice and I entered it on the thirteenth day and the captain read it out twice and he was right to, and I still hold it and it is still true, and it has not stopped being true because four people have been good at a table.

“Surveyor Collis goes to Fourmile at the close of this week with a return that says nine houses, and she is an accredited surveyor of the northern district with a seal, and I am authorised this afternoon to offer her certification to any household on route L-4 that wants it. Not the form. A person. She will go to a house, and she will take the household’s answer, and she will write down that she was in the room, and she will certify it, and that goes on the return with a surveyor’s seal on it, and it is worth more than my own hand and yours and it is worth more than mine would be.”

Nobody spoke for some time.

“I am not doing this as a kindness and I want the room to have the real shape of it,” Rudd said. “I am doing it because a commission that is told by a ward that it does not trust its certification, and then refuses to offer any, is a commission that has lied to somebody. And I would rather this ward have a surveyor at nine of its doors and object to a Crown man in a house than have me report a ward of four hundred and eleven that will not have anybody in a house at all. That is the choice and it is not a generous one; it is the only honest one, and it has a price in it, and the price is her, and not me.”

Then he sat down.

Mrs Corris was still in the room, because she had not gone, and she said, to nobody, quite clearly:

“Nine of our doors. Out of forty-one.”

Which is a better sentence than anything anybody in the ward had managed all day, and it went round the market court in about a minute and changed the shape of the offer, and it is on the ward’s page, and it is the reason Nell Fenn wrote the whole day up with a straight line through it and then, after a while, without.

---

On the twentieth day at the ninth hour of the evening, the day before the round, two Crown officers walked the ward.

They were polite. They were two of the ordinary sort with a slate and a lamp and no case, and they went to doors in ones and twos and they said the same four sentences at about forty of them, and the sentences were, in the order they said them: good evening, the round is at first light, the form was exhibited in full this morning and will be exhibited in full again, would your household intend to attend in person.

And at every door, the person who answered said yes or no, and the officer wrote down, on the Crown’s own slate, in the Crown’s own hand, *yes* or *no* beside a house number, and the officer did not have a form for it and did not need one, because a man with a slate and a lamp in a lane at nine in the evening is a form.

It was not a raid and it was not a warrant and it was not on the market court board. It was a man being courteous, in a country where being courteous to a household at its own door after dark is an ordinary thing, and about forty people in the Low Ward said yes.

Every one of them was written down. Not on a return. On a slate, in a hand, in a lane, and the slate went into a satchel at the north gate at the tenth hour and the satchel went to Fourmile with the morning post, and in a district office it will be a piece of paper with forty house numbers on it and a column that is, in effect, *attends*, and the district has never had that column before and did not have to invent it, because the Crown invented it for them, in a lane, politely, at the doors of a ward on the night before a round.

Nell Fenn found out about it at about the eleventh hour from a boy of eleven who had been sent to ask her mother something, and she went out into the lane, and there was nothing to do in the lane at the eleventh hour of the twentieth day about forty names in a Crown slate, and she stood in the rain and understood that she had spent eight days building a practice in which a question made a house visible, and that on the night before the round the Crown had gone round the ward with a lamp and got the same thing, free, by being kind, and had got forty houses in an hour that she had spent eight days failing to get one.

She wrote it up. She wrote it up in full, in her own hand, with the hour, and she read it out to nobody, and then she did the thing that is the only reason she is still in this business: she wrote a second line under it, which is the line that the ward will read in a year.

*That on the evening of the twentieth day the Crown obtained from about forty households in this ward, by asking at their doors, a list of the households that intend to attend the round in person, and did it in a satchel, in a hand, with a lamp, and without a form, and was not required to use a single power I have spent eight days arguing about, and that the whole of my argument to those doors was that a question is a notice, and that the Crown has now found a cheaper notice than mine, and that I am not going to be able to say in a room what I have been saying in a room, and I am not going to pretend otherwise to anybody, and I will read this line out at the board tomorrow before anything else, because the day I stop reading it out is the day this ward stops being able to check anything at all.*
