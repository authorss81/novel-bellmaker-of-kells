# Chapter 0026 — The Name That Went

There were two empty houses in the lane by the time the light came, and Nell had known about one of them since the middle of the night and about the other one since six o’clock, and she had not slept on either.

Number one was 122, and the Cafferys’ cart was gone and their door was open and the inside of it was the inside of a house that has been emptied of the two things a family cannot carry: the churn and the box with the papers in it.

Number two was 136, where the Oakes had taken their two beds and their mother’s chest and gone up past the millrace in the dark, and had left the door shut, and had left a broom in the lane.

Nell stood in the lane with a slate and counted the empty houses and then did not write the count down, because a number in her hand at six in the morning is a number that will be in somebody else’s hand by noon, and she had been burned that way twice.

Her mother came out with the wash basket on her hip.

“I am going to the court,” Mrs Fenn said.

“It is not the hour.”

“I am going to the court, and I am going with the box, and I will carry it to the door and I will say what I can say about my own house, and then they will have to write it down, and then it will be *in* the book.” She was already moving. “Somebody has to say a plain thing to a face. That is all I am offering to do. I will not say anything clever.”

“You will not say anything at all, because you will be asked one question and you will answer it, and it will be the answer they want, and in six weeks somebody will read the book and find that the Fenn household supports the round.”

Mrs Fenn stopped in the lane. She was forty-one and she had been washing other people’s linen for fourteen years, and her face did the thing it did when she was about to agree to something that sounded like work.

“They took children out of that lane yesterday,” she said.

“Yes.”

“And nobody has said a word.”

“Somebody said a word at the board last night,” Nell said. “There were sixty people there. A courier read out a date and it was the best thing anybody has done in this town in ten days. And now I am going to do the part nobody did, which is go down that lane with a copy of a paper and ask nine houses one question each, and I am going to do it on my own because if you come with me they will ask you what you think instead of what happened to them.”

Her mother put the basket down in the lane.

“Say the question,” she said. “So that I know what I am not doing.”

Nell told her, and her mother listened with her head on one side, and then said, “That is a good question,” and picked the basket up and went to the washhouse, which was the whole of what she had been given and all of what she could hold, and it was as much as she had to carry that day.

---

The pinned copy of the schedule was still up, with the drawing pin through the drawing pin, and the charcoal under it, and beside it last night’s slate in four hands, and beside that a Crown notice about further questions on route L-4 that nobody in the ward had read because they had all gone to bed.

The queue for the slate had started before Nell got there and she made six of them do the same thing instead of talking, which is the only technique she knows.

“What do you see?” she said.

“I see forty-one houses and it is not my house.”

“Look down the response column. Do not look at the house numbers. Look down the second column from the right, the one that says RESPONSE, and tell me which of those words you have seen before.”

The first one said *accompanying*. The second said *not returned*. The third said *accompanying* and the woman who was saying it stopped and said, in a voice that had gone strange, “That is my boy.”

Nell let her finish. She let four more people in before she said anything to anybody, and by the eighth she had a count on her slate in her own hand, and by the twelfth she had the ward’s whole day in front of her.

Nine lines said *not returned*.

Six lines said *accompanying*.

Three of those fifteen lines were at houses where a Crown slate was on the door this morning **and** a child was not in the house this morning.

**114. 121. 133.**

That was all of them. Every other marked door on the lane, every other nail and slate and Crown number, was on a line that said nothing except *entered in office* or a count and a tick. Not one of them.

*Not returned* and *accompanying* were not answers. They were notes. A note made by somebody in an office about a person who was not in the house when the office decided what was in the house. And the Crown had read its own notes as the answer, and had gone to the door and said the Crown had an entry for the household, and had taken a child off the strength of a line nobody in that house had ever seen.

Nell sat down on the low wall with the slate on her knees and had a very bad quarter of an hour.

Because she could see what it wanted. It wanted to be a story. *They took our children because our children had been written down as gone.* That would go round this ward by the afternoon and it would be true in the way that a true thing is true when it has one person in it, and it would be carried into every house on the lane, and by the following day every family on the list would be frightened of the list and angry with the families who had been on it, and the Crown would not have to do anything at all.

And the paper that proved it — the one piece of paper in Kells that said a household was not what an office said it was — was in her coat, against her ribs, and it was the Fenn fragment, and it said her own household is three with a fourth line cut away, and the Crown says six.

It is a copy. The original has been in her coat since the sixth day.

She could put it on the board in an hour and it would be the first document anybody in this town had produced against a Crown page, and it would be a document about her own family, about her brother and her father, and the ward would read it and see two names in it, and the ward would be grateful to her, and she would have handed her brother to a crowd the way a clerk hands a house to an office.

She sat on the wall until the sun came over the roof of the washhouse and then she got up and went and knocked on the door of 119.

Mercy Slee had a boy of nine and had been up since four, and she came to the door in a wrapper with her arms folded, and she did not open it more than a hand’s width.

“Are you the girl with the slate?”

“I’m Nell Fenn and I am asking one question at each house on this lane and I have asked you this one at every other door and I would like to ask you the same one.”

“Go on.”

“Yesterday, at five in the morning, did a Crown officer come to this door?”

“Yes.”

“Did they take anything or anyone from this house?”

“No.” Mercy Slee’s face did not move. “But I have a boy of nine who is not on their paper, because I have never been on their paper, and they are going to come back for him, and they are going to come back for him on a day when I am at the washhouse, and I have worked that out, and I am not going to be standing in a doorway saying I never saw it. That is my whole position and I am not giving it to you because you are sixteen and you have a slate.”

“I am not asking what you think happened. I am asking what happened at your door.”

“I have just told you what happened at my door,” Mercy Slee said, “and now I am going to tell you the other thing, and then I am going to shut this door, and you may write it down or not. If you put my door on that list — and they will come with that list, they have been telling people it is the ward roll, and there is a copy on that board with a pin in it — then I will be a house they are coming to. If my house is not on any list and nobody writes it down, then on that day there will be four words on a slate and a number, and a number can be got wrong.”

Nell stood in the sun on a step and understood that she was being told, by a woman who had decided her position before she opened the door, that the truest use of a record might be to leave a house off it.

“I am not writing your house down,” she said. “I am not writing any house down. I am writing *no* or *yes* and the hour, on a slate, and there is no number on my slate and no name on my slate and I take it to the washhouse when I have finished and I burn it in the washhouse fire and you can watch me do it.”

Mercy Slee looked at her for a while.

“Do not be clever,” she said. “Clever is how they get you. Just say no and go on.”

The door shut. In the gap, before it went, she said: “No. And bring the slate round and I will read it before you burn it.”

---

At the ninth hour the Crown came for the covered box on the board.

It was not a raid. It was a Crown clerk with two men and a form, and the form was very short, and it asked the ward board for the surrender of the box and the papers in it as duplicate Crown material retained without authority, in the L-4 matter, and it gave the ward until the fourth hour of the afternoon.

Nell read it out to nine people and one of them asked whether they should give it up, and the answer was that the box held a copy of the market court book, which was the market court’s own document and not Crown material at all, and that the ward board was not the market court and had never pretended to be, and that the ward board kept a copy of a paper it had been given because the people who lived behind it could not get up the hill to read it.

The clerk wrote *ward board asserts the box is not Crown material* and did not press it, which Nell thought was cowardice and which Oren Pike would have called a filing decision, and she did not have anybody to ask.

She also decided, standing there in the lane with a form in her hand, that she was not going to give the Crown a person.

Because it was four streets and one lane away, in a room with two wash tubs in it, at the second hour of the afternoon, and it was the only hour in the day when Ansel Stroud was in a filing room rather than in a room with a Crown officer in it, and because he was a man who had already said one true thing to a watch captain in a room with no witnesses in it, and because he was frightened, and because frightened men will answer a precise question and run from an angry one.

She went at half past one and knocked twice and said her name and asked for the room.

He was younger than she had expected and he had ink to the second knuckle. He looked at the closed door behind her and then at the paper in her hand and then at her, and she saw him decide to be afraid and not to run, which is a thing people do sometimes.

“I have one question,” she said. “I am going to ask it and you are going to answer it in whatever words are true, and I am going to write down what you say, and I am not going to write down your name unless you ask me to. If you would rather I went away, say so now and I will go away and I will not come back.”

Stroud put his pen down.

“Go on.”

“The schedule they pinned on our board is a page off a form.” Nell kept her voice level and kept it slow, because she had learned on the fourth day that a person who is frightened will agree to a fast argument and slow down when it is made slowly. “I know that because I have read your returns before, at the fish market office, when I was copying for the meal roll, and there is a form number at the top of them and a heading. It is not our form. It is a Crown form. It has got a column for the person who was in the room when the answer was given, and it is headed — ”

“You have been reading our returns.”

“Since I was eleven. And the column is headed *Witnessed by*, and on the Kells returns it is empty.”

Stroud sat very still.

“Yes,” he said.

“Then tell me what the column is for.”

“It is for the person who was present at the answering.” He said it quickly and then, hearing himself, more slowly. “Not the person who answered. The person who was in the room when the answering happened, and who can therefore say that it happened in a room with a person in it. On a Kells return it is empty. It is empty on every Kells return I have filed in eleven years and I have filed four hundred and six.”

“Whose handwriting goes in it on the returns where it isn’t empty?”

“The answering household’s.” He swallowed. “Or somebody’s in the house. It is not a clerk’s hand. It is a name, because a name can be asked about later and a clerk cannot.”

Nell wrote it down. She wrote it slowly, in the room, so that he could see the words going onto the slate as she made them, because a woman taking notes wrongly is one thing and a woman taking notes wrongly on purpose is a different charge.

“And how many wards in the district have a name in that column?” she said.

“That is not mine to know. That is a district return. But it is not one ward.” He stopped, and looked at the door again, and then said the rest of it in a rush, as though he had decided that if it was going to come out it would come out all at once. “I will tell you what I can. A return goes up with the column filled where a household answered in a room. A return goes up with the column empty where the answer was entered in the office because there was no room to enter it in, and there is a reason field for that, and the reason field is not in your form because your form is the summary form. The summary form drops the reason. That is what a summary form is for.”

Nell put her charcoal down.

“Stroud,” she said, “yesterday three children went out of that lane and a Crown officer read a line off a piece of paper that said a person was not returned, and I do not think that officer had a form in his hand that said *no person was in the room when the answer was entered*. Did he?”

“I do not know what that officer had in his hand,” Stroud said. “I was not on the round. I file. I have never been on a round in this district and I would not go if I was asked.”

Nell wrote that down too, exactly, and read the whole slate back to him before she stood up, and he corrected one word and she struck it out and wrote his word, and then she put her hand flat on the slate for a second longer than she needed to, which was not a trick. It was a girl of sixteen thanking a man who had just handed her the only thing in this town that could be checked by anybody, and she had not the least idea what to do with it.

“You will want to know what I am going to do with that,” she said at the door.

“No,” Stroud said. “Do not tell me. If I know what you are going to do with it then I am part of it, and I would rather be the man who answered a precise question in a room with the door shut.”

---

She got back to the ward at the fourth hour with the box still on the board, because she had gone back and told them, one at a time, that the Crown wanted it at four o’clock and that she was not going to lie to them twice, and then eleven people had said no in front of each other, and that had taken the rest of the afternoon and had done nothing whatever about the box and had done a great deal about whether anybody in the ward was frightened enough to be alone about it.

The sun was down behind the millrace ridge by the time she got to the washhouse. Mercy Slee was there, at the second tub, waiting, with her arms folded.

Nell read her the whole slate out loud, in order, all of it, including the parts that were rubbish. Mercy Slee listened the way she had done everything, without moving.

“Read the middle bit again,” she said.

Nell read the part about the reason field and the summary form again.

“Right,” Mercy Slee said. “Then it is not a secret. It is a *box* that is missing from the form they gave us.” She pushed her hands back. “Go on, girl. Burn it.”

Nell burned it in the washhouse fire in three pieces, and watched the last corner go, and stood there afterwards with her hands in the smoke for longer than she needed to.

Because that was the whole of the morning, and it was not nothing: there is a column, it is headed *Witnessed by*, it is empty on every Kells return, and a name goes in it when a household answers in a room. That was a fact and it was checkable and it was the first thing anybody in Kells had held that the Crown could not simply fold away.

And then she had thrown it away, in a washhouse, in front of one woman.

She stood there and did the arithmetic, and it came out the way it always came out for her, which was that the true thing and the useful thing are two different things, and that the whole of the last four days had been the distance between them, and that she was sixteen and tired and had just made a decision she could not take back.

She still had the fragment copy, made on the ninth evening at the watch house table because the market court clerk pushed the pen across to her and told her to write it in her own hand and her own spelling, with Oren’s signature under it as the copyist. Six persons on the Crown’s line. Three on the ward’s paper, with a fourth cut away. It was the only piece of paper in Kells that contradicted one specific line, and the line it contradicted was hers.

And she could not use it. Not yet, not like this. If she put the Fenn line on that board this week, then for the rest of her life the thing the ward knew about the Fenn house would be that her brother and her father were in an argument, and she would have chosen that, and she would have chosen it because somebody had pushed the fragment at her.

She went home along the lane past 122 and 136 with the slate gone out of her hand, and two families out of the ward, and a fresh slate in her hand, bought that morning for sixpence at the washhouse, with nothing on it yet, and the whole of a morning’s work in a washhouse fire.

The Crown’s notice about further questions on route L-4 was still on the board, and she read it properly on the way past, because she had learned to read Crown notices, and because it was four lines and would take a minute.

It was four lines and it named the renewal returns, the form, and the district renewal office at Fourmile, and beneath the four lines, in the small hand a Crown notice always has underneath the large one, it said that any person having evidence touching the renewal returns on route L-4 might bring it to that office and that such evidence would be received.

*Renewal office. Fourmile.* Which was a place, and not four hundred and eleven, and which meant that the number was a desk in a building where a person could be found, and which meant that the finding of yesterday evening was going to be answered by a man walking up the north road with a case.

And in the form that the man would bring, in a column that the ward had never been shown, there was a space for the name of the person who had been in the room when the answer was given.

And there had not been one.
