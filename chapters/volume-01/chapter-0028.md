# Chapter 0028 — Publishing a Practice

The trouble with publishing a discovery with nobody’s name on it is that the town will supply one, and it will supply it within an hour, and by the following day two thirds of Kells will be certain that they know who did it, and a man who has been certain of a thing is much harder to reason with in a lane than a man who has a document.

They argued about this for three hours on the thirteenth day in the watch house, and the finding was not what they argued about. The finding was in four copies and a covered box and it was finished. What they argued about was the shape of the thing they were going to put in front of the people.

Tamsin had it in her own hand on a slate and read it out twice before she would let anybody touch it.

*No bell is to be rung for this.*

“Agreed,” said Oren.

“It is not agreed by me, it is a condition,” Tamsin said. “I have been at the back of two things this week that turned a document into a crowd and a crowd into a version. The first was a woman at a chandler’s telling my reach on a market day, and the second was a slate at this court. So: paper, on foot, at doors, read out, and no sound. And I am not carrying anything up the towpath, because the day I carry a piece of paper through the Low Ward on the Crown’s route, the Crown’s route and my route become the same route in a man’s mouth, and that man will be right about the words and wrong about the reason and there will be no way back.”

“Then the ward does it on its own legs,” Oren said, “and Jerm goes with every person who will not go alone, and nobody goes past the toll shed, because past the toll shed the finding is a rumour and not a practice.”

“Right,” said Nell.

Nobody had expected her to speak and everybody turned round.

“You are going to say something about how it is worded,” Tamsin said.

“I am going to say something about who says it.” Nell put her own slate down, which was the only one on the table with anything at all on it. “He cannot read it out. If Tomas Vale reads out the three questions, then the three questions are the bellmaker’s questions, and the first person to say no to a question in a Crown office will be a person who has refused the bellmaker, and in a fortnight it will be said in the district town that the Bellwrights’ Measure has published a procedure for disputing Crown returns, and in a month the guild will be served, and Iven Sore will be served first because he is the one with a challenge outstanding.”

“That is a good reason,” Oren said, “and it is not the reason.”

“It is a reason,” Nell said. “It is the reason I can say in a room.”

“Then who,” said Oren.

“Me. I asked one precise question of one precise clerk in a specific room on the twelfth and he answered it and I wrote down what he said and then I burned it, and the thing I am best at is asking a clerk a question and writing down what he says. And if I am the one standing in that office, then the Crown has to refuse a sixteen-year-old with a slate, in front of her neighbours, in daylight, and write down that it has done it.”

Nobody in the room had an argument against that worth making. Tomas Vale, who had spent ten days learning not to be the man who carries everything, said:

“Yes. Do that.”

---

They had it on the court steps at the ninth hour, in daylight, with a wind off the river, and about a hundred and twenty people came. More than had come for the three answers, fewer than had come for the rope.

Tomas did not speak. He stood at the back of the step in his own coat with the day-book under his arm and he did not speak, and it was the hardest physical act of his life, harder than the bench, and he understood afterwards why: at the bench he had a job in his hands, and here he had nothing in his hands, and the man he was when he had nothing in his hands was a man who took over.

Crown Officer Ambrose Rudd was there with two men and a black case, and he had come to object and he had said so on the way up the lane, and he stood at the front, where a man making an objection belongs.

“I am going to enter an objection before anything is read,” he said. “This is a public accusation that a Crown return has been filled in falsely. I am not asking that the crowd be dispersed and I have no power to disperse it. I am asking that whoever put it together should understand what it will do. A ward that publishes a procedure for disputing its own renewal roll becomes, in the district’s eyes, a ward with a grievance about its renewal roll. Every clerk in this office will be advised of the publication. Every household that uses the procedure will be noted, because the only way to note it is to note the house. And a ward whose houses begin to be noted for asking questions is a ward that will be asked fewer questions in a year, and the fewer questions a ward is asked, the fewer children it has on a page that anybody looks at.”

He had prepared it. It was good, and it was, Tomas thought, probably true, and it was the first argument all week that had not been made by a frightened man in a hurry.

“Then put it in the book in that form,” Oren said, “and I will read it out after, and I will read it out in the morning as well.”

“You will?”

“It is the best objection anybody has made against us this week, and it was made to us by a Crown officer in daylight, and if it is not read out twice then it is a thing that was said in a lane and forgotten, and in a year somebody will be able to say that nobody in Kells ever thought of it, and that would be a lie.”

Rudd looked at him for a second. “That is a very foolish thing to do.”

“Probably,” Oren said. “Read it out twice.”

---

Nell read it out.

She had written it herself and refused three versions other people had offered her, and what she read was this, and she read it in a flat voice to a hundred and twenty people with the wind taking the corners off it:

“Any person in this ward may go to a Crown clerk, in daylight, and ask three questions about their own house. They do not need a reason. They do not need permission. They may ask them in writing or by word of mouth, and they may take a witness, and they may write down what is said to them.

“First: show me the line with my house number on it, and read it to me.

“Second: on what day was that line entered, and whose hand put it there?

“Third: which of the people in my house does that line name, and may I have it corrected here, now, in front of a witness?

“And a fourth thing, which is not a question: anybody may come to this board and say *this line is wrong about me*, and we will write it down in our own book, in their own words, and it stays up, and it does not go to any office unless they hand it over themselves.”

She looked up from the slate.

“That is all of it,” she said. “There is no name on it. We are not saying who did it. We are saying that there is a page, and here is what you can ask about your own line of it, and the answers have to be written down where you can see them. If you go and ask and the answer is *I do not know*, that is an answer and you write it down and it is still evidence. If the answer is *there is no line for your house*, that is a better answer and you write it down. If the answer is *I am not permitted to say*, that is the answer this whole thing is about and you write that one down in the biggest letters you have.”

Nobody cheered, and she had told them in advance not to, and a woman near the back said, “*so that’s where they do it*” in a voice like a knife going into a board, and the man beside her said nothing, and a man further along said, “Some of us have to live in this town,” and Oren let that stand without answering it.

Then the Crown officer said the thing that ruined the morning for about a third of the people there, and he did it in a reasonable tone, and he was right.

“I have to tell you that a question of that kind is a notice,” he said. “The moment a clerk in the district office hears that a ward is putting questions about its own returns, every question you ask will be recorded, and it will be recorded that you asked. I cannot prevent that from being so from this step. I can only tell you it is so, and that the officer in Fourmile is not a fool, and that a fool is easier to refuse than an office.”

It was, of course, right. It was right about the mechanic and it was right about the shape of the harm, and it meant that what Nell had published this morning was not a way of being safer, it was a way of being visible.

Three people withdrew from the ward board’s arrangement in the following hour and a half, and one of them was a woman whose grandmother was on line twenty-eight, and she said, in front of eleven people, that her grandmother had answered the round in person, with a slate, in her own hand, and that she would not have that slate read out in a lane, and that if her grandmother had not been asked, then God help them all, but that the whole of her family was going to know by the afternoon that somebody had published a way of asking whether she had.

Oren wrote her objection down in full and read it back, and then asked her one question.

“Do you want the fact that you were on the line left on the board, or taken off?”

She thought about it for a long time and said, “Left on. And put the reason under it, which is that I have not asked anything yet and I mean to, and I would like the board to be able to say so on my behalf afterwards.”

“Write it on the slate,” Nell said, “and I will copy it and you will initial it, and it goes up in the box, and if I ever use your name in this I will not do it without asking you in front of a witness.”

“That is a promise made by a child.”

“It is a promise made in front of a hundred and twenty people,” Nell said, “which is a better room than a promise made in a kitchen.”

---

At the ninth hour of the afternoon a Crown clerk set up a table under the arcade with a folding chair behind it, and about forty people watched a Crown officer set up a Crown table under a market court arcade and nobody could tell you why.

It was Captain Pike’s doing and it cost him one sentence to the commission and about six years of his comfort. He had gone to Crown Officer Rudd at the eighth hour and said that if the district was going to tell this ward that questions were noticed, then the ward was entitled to put them somewhere other than Fourmile, and that the watch would provide a table, a chair, a stool for a witness and a pot of ink for as long as the Crown would sit behind it, and that if the Crown would not sit behind it then the questions would have to travel.

They would sit behind it. From the ninth hour until the tenth, a Crown clerk answered household questions about their own lines in public, in daylight, under a roof, in front of whoever wanted to stand there.

Absalom Teage was the first man in the queue, and he had brought the slate his household held from the posting day, and he had also brought his wife, and he had also told his wife exactly what he intended to ask, and she had told him to change the order, and he had not.

“Show me the line with my house number on it,” he said, “and read it to me.”

Stroud — because it was Stroud, and Stroud had not said he would be at the table and had not said he would not — found it without any difficulty, because that was the first question and the first question is the one the office is built for, and he read it out: four entered, three returned, one not returned, entered in office the day the round was posted.

“That is right,” Teage said. “Now the second. On what day was that line entered, and whose hand put it there?”

Stroud read the date again, because the date was on the page and the date was the safe part.

“I can give you the day,” he said. “I cannot give you the hand. I am not able to. I have looked, and there is no signature against any of the forty-one, and I have said that on this table and I will say it to any person who comes up here today, and there is no hand on any of them, and that is not a thing I can put in a different place for you by wanting to.”

“Then write that down,” Teage said, “in a place where it stays.”

Stroud took a corner of the sheet and wrote, in a small hand, under the line, in the margin: *No person in this house was asked. The entry was made in office. I cannot say whose hand. — A. Stroud, clerk.* And he initialed it, and read it back, and it was a piece of writing in a margin, signed by a Crown clerk, in public, in daylight, at a table the watch had put there for the purpose.

“I want the word *not returned* answered for,” Teage said. “That is my mother. She is sixty-eight and she has not been out of her room since Candlemas, and you have written that she did not come back, and I have been awake for two nights working out how a woman does not come back from a room she has not left.”

Stroud wrote, beside it: *Disputed at this counter, thirteenth day, by the household, whose slate is produced. The entry stands. — A. Stroud.*

“The entry stands,” Teage said.

“Yes.”

“You have written that it stands, in a room.”

“Yes.”

“Then I will take it,” Teage said, “and I will say what I say in the market about it, which is that the Crown of this ward wrote *my mother did not come back* and would not write down *we were never asked*, and that on the thirteenth day a clerk wrote both of those things down in a public room in his own name, and that the second one is now on a piece of paper and the first one is still on the page, and both of them are true and I have to hold them both.”

He put the slate under his arm. His wife was looking at the table and not at him.

“Is that a win?” she said, on the walk back up the lane.

“No,” Teage said. “That is a witness.”

---

Gib Hask came at the eleventh hour with two men behind him, one of whom was carrying a bar, and he did not go near the step.

“I want to say a thing and I want to say it to the Crown and not to you,” he said. “You have all got very pleased with yourselves. Paper. Slate. Three questions in a clerk’s ear. Good. Now listen, because I have been at this four days longer than you have.

“Every one of those questions is a question about a page. And the page came from a place where a man’s hand goes in a box and a door closes. And there is another thing in this town and this ward that you have all agreed not to say out loud, which is that there is copper under it. Under my house. Under the washhouse. Under the lane the children walk to the landing. Laid by an office, in an ordinary week, with nobody asked, and it is there now, and it was there before four hundred and eleven was writing.”

“Mister Hask,” Oren said, “the wire is in the record, and your argument is in the record, and I have read both to the court twice and I will read them a third time.”

“Then hear the rest of it.” Hask did not raise his voice and it was worse. “I am not a fool. I know what a slate does and I know what a crowd does. So I will tell you what I see, and you may write it down. A town that has learned to argue with paper will spend a year arguing with paper. And in that year nobody will be looking at the ground. And in the second year, when a gang comes up that lane with a bar and a Crown order to *renew the line*, this ward will have a book full of excellent questions about a page, and it will have no warning at all, and the wire will be out of the ground in an afternoon and there will not be a single person in the ward who will be able to say a word about it, because every person who might have said a word will be standing in a queue at a filing desk being *visible*.”

Nobody had an answer to that and nobody pretended to.

“I am not asking for the paper to come down,” Hask said. “Take it down if you like. I want the ground. I have wanted the ground since the second day and I have said so in three rooms. I am not going to stop saying it, and I am not going to pretend that I would not put a bar in the ground if the Crown came with a bar, and I would like that written down in your book in my words, because it is true and it will be true, and if I ever do it I would like there to be a paper that says I said so first.”

“Write it,” Oren said, to Jerm, “in his words, and read it back to him, and I will sign it.”

“You will not stop me.”

“No,” Oren said. “I have no lawful power to stop you and I have no honest one either, because a man who has been told for four days that the ward is being listened to and has watched three children go out of a lane in that time is not in a condition to be reasoned with about a bar, and I have read enough in this town to know what a warning is worth to a man who has been given one he could not use.”

---

At the fourth hour they made the page co-held. It was a piece of ceremony and it mattered more than the finding.

Two sheets, written out twice, one for the watch archive and one for the ward board, so that neither office could be shut without the other keeping a copy. The ward’s copy was to be held by the board and by a named household, because a board can be nailed up by a man with a mallet and a household cannot.

Nell proposed Mercy Slee. Oren proposed old man Crane. They argued for a quarter of an hour about whether a foreman of the market landing was too useful a man to be given a page to lose, and Mercy Slee settled it from the middle of the crowd by saying that if anybody wanted a piece of paper they could come and take it off her, and that she was not going to be a committee, and that her house was not on the Crown’s list and never would be because it was not on anybody’s list if she could help it.

Her name went at the foot of it. Oren signed beside her, and then read the whole thing out twice, once in the evening and once in the morning, because Rudd’s objection had been right about one thing, and the one thing was that a paper only stays in a town’s memory if somebody is tiresome about it.

The Crown’s answer came at the seventh hour of the evening, and it came in the form that Crown answers take when a Crown does not want to be seen to be arguing.

*FURTHER QUESTIONS WILL BE PUT ON ROUTE L-4 IN THE MATTER OF RENEWAL RETURNS, AT THE DISTRICT RENEWAL OFFICE, FOURMILE, AND BY THE SURVEYOR TO THIS COMMISSION. AND NOTICED TO THE MARKET COURT IS THIS: that the market court call bell, and every bell in this town joined to any line, wire, or ground, is to be exhibited and described at the next sitting of the court, on the ground that no such apparatus is to be sounded, carried, or described in public without the authority of the commission.*

They nailed it to the board at eight o’clock. There were about twenty people there to read it, and Nell Fenn read it out twice.

Tamsin Rook stood at the back of the crowd with her hands in her sleeves and looked at the words *the market court call bell* for a long time.

“They have not read it,” she said.

“They have read it,” Tomas said. “It is the first line of it.”

“No. That is the first line of it, and I have been standing here for a minute reading the whole of it, and the whole of it is a threat about the paper.” She nodded at the board, where her own hand had been on a slate two days ago and Nell’s hand had been on a receipt in an office a day ago. “They have just told every clerk in this district to watch for the ward putting questions about its returns. And every question a house asks from now on makes that office stronger, and every answer on this page gets a little less safe to hand out, and that is what this notice is for. It is not about bells. There is not one word in it about a bell that is not a bell they want to take.”

“They will take the court bell,” Oren said.

“They will ask the court for it,” Tamsin said, “and the court will refuse, and then they will take it, and the day they take it the woman who owns it will be a woman who let a Crown office tell her what her own bell is. And that is the part of it they have been building since the eighth day, and they put it in a public notice, in the last hour, on a wind, and they have let us read it.”

Nobody said anything.

Then Tamsin said, “Vale. You have not got a bell. You have got a page, and a page is the only thing in this town that has not been taken off anybody yet. Whatever you do next, do it with the page.”

“And the millrace bell is on their list,” said Oren.

“The millrace bell is on their list and I cannot help you with it and you know why,” Tamsin said, “and I am not going to pretend that the towpath is a resource I have left. That was yesterday. Tell me what you are going to publish next, and I will tell you whether I will put my name to it.”
