# Chapter 0013 — The Low Ward Does Not Answer

The chandler’s ground floor had six inches of water in it by noon, and Nell Fenn had spent the morning moving sacks of wick and tallow above the tide line while the rain came through the shutters in three separate places. Her hands were shaking when she reached the Bellhouse gate, and she was angry about that, because shaking hands had made her mother’s voice go sharp twice that month and she was not going to give it a third reason.

The Crown guards were under the arcade. One of them waved her through without writing anything down, which was a kind of luck she had not earned and would not rely on.

Tomas was in the yard with the river rod leaning against the wall and the trial handbell still under its cloth on the bench inside. He came out when he saw her and did not ask what was wrong. He asked how much water.

“Six inches at the low shelf. The wick is ruined. My master’s got four days of work in that shop and nothing on the high shelf but flour.” She pushed the wet off her face. “The whole row is saying the thing that matters today is the water, not the Crown notice.”

“Which row?”

“The chandler’s row. The chandler’s row, the cooper’s row, and the row behind them, which is my row.”

He nodded and did not say anything for a moment, which she had learned to read as him thinking and not as him deciding.

“Will you ask them?” she said.

“I cannot. They would hear a bellmaker and answer a bellmaker.” Tomas leaned on the bench. “If you ask, it is your question and your record, and I will write it down exactly as it comes out of their mouths.”

“Then write down my question first, so I don’t put words in their mouths.”

“No.” He said it without any weight, which was the honest way. “If I write your question, then three answers come back to a bellmaker and people will believe the bellmaker chose the question. You write it. Read the same words to each one. I will keep the record and I will not add to it.”

She thought about that harder than she wanted to.

“All right,” she said. “I’ll write it at the top of the paper and I’ll read it out, and if anybody asks me what you think the answer should be, I’ll tell them what I think, which is nothing, because I don’t know.”

“That is the correct answer and it is the hardest one to say.”

She went home first. That was the deal she had made with him days ago in a room with a warped door, and she was not going to let a river take it from her.

Her mother was at the laundry vats, which meant she had already come back, which meant she had already decided to be helpful to whoever was standing in front of her. Nell found her with her sleeves pushed up and a list of four names on a slate, and she stood in the doorway and said, “Before anything else. I am going to ask three people today whether they will carry a warning. I am not asking you for the family.”

Her mother’s hands went still in the suds.

“Nell.”

“Two of them may want to know what our house does. The answer is that our house does whatever each of us decides, separately, like the rest of the houses. I am not speaking for you.”

“You are sixteen.”

“I know what I am.” She kept her voice down, because the two lodgers could hear through the wall and the landlady repeated everything. “The notice they pinned at the west road went up two days after they took Jory. So nothing that is happening now began with the notice. I want three people to say out loud what they will do about the thing that is happening now. That is all.”

Her mother’s face did something complicated. “I will not have your name on a paper for the Crown.”

“It is not the Crown’s paper. It is the watch captain’s, and the market court’s, and mine. And the third part is that no one may answer for our house. Not you, not me, not for Jory.”

Her mother wiped her hands on her apron and looked at the list of four names, and then put the slate face down on the table, which was the first time in a year she had put it down while there was still room on it.

“Then go and ask your three,” she said. “And come back and tell me every single one, including the one you expect to be unkind to you, because I will not have you building a wall out of the ones that are kind.”

The ward board was under a lean-to at the corner of the west road, and it was where any notice for that part of Kells went to be read and argued with. Nell pinned her own sheet to the board’s lower rail before she asked anybody anything, which was a decision she had made without telling Tomas, and which she defended to herself for twenty minutes afterward.

The sheet said:

*I am Nell Fenn and I am asking this because I live here. A Crown notice was filed three days before the fixed carrier sounded. It says there will be a dawn renewal inspection on route L-4, and it says unregistered minors may be held as public assets. The notice went up first. Three days first, and nothing had sounded yet, and I cannot tell you why a renewal was written down before there was anything to renew. The water is up two marks on the sill at the low gate and the low lane is shut.*

*I am not asking you to move your children, or your goods, or to believe me. I am asking whether you will carry these words, exactly as they are written, to people who ask you for them. You can say yes. You can say no. You can say you will do part of it. You can say you will wait until you have found something out first. Whatever you say, it does not speak for your house, for your neighbours, or for anyone else. I will write it down as you said it.*

*If you would rather not answer at all, that is also an answer and I will write it as silence, and not as a yes.*

She read it aloud to the first man twice, because the first man said he wanted to hear the part about not being asked to move his children again.

Foreman Crane was the first to answer properly, and he did it standing in the rain with his hat in his hand, which Nell had not expected of him.

“I will carry them to the river landing,” he said. “I will knock. I will not ring anything and I will not shout in a lane. I will read them out if somebody opens a door, and if they do not open it, I will knock the next one and I will not tell them they were warned.” He thought about it. “And I will not carry them up past the landing, because up past the landing is the ferry road and those people are not my row and they did not ask me.”

“That is a good answer,” Nell said, and then wished she had not said it, because a good answer sounded like praise, and praise was what made a person agree with the next question.

She wrote his answer in his words, in her own hand, and the handwriting shook so badly that she had to rule a line under it afterward.

The second was Wenna Cray, who kept a slate of her own by the bread stall and who had lost two people to a round in the spring.

“Time,” Wenna said, before Nell had finished the question. “I will give you time. Not no. Give me until tomorrow, and I will say my piece when I have been to the house where they sent my sister.”

“You know where they sent her?”

“No. I know the address of the woman who came back from it and did not come back with anything.” She touched the slate. “The board keeps a copy of the round. Every round posts a paper the week before with the street and the numbers. If I can get the paper for the round that took my sister, I can stop being a widow of a rumor. Until then I will not put my hand on a sentence about an inspection, because the last time I did that they used my hand.”

“That is time asked, then.”

“That is time asked, and it is not a yes, and you will write that it is not a yes.” Wenna Cray looked at the board. “Is that a bad thing to have on a public board?”

“No,” Nell said, which was the truest answer she had given all day.

The third was Garn Fisk, who worked stone, and who said no before Nell had got to the second line.

“No,” he said.

“Will you let me read it anyway?”

“No, and I will not be written down, and I will not say it at the board, and I will not say it to the watch, and I will not say it to the Crown when they come.” He was not angry. He was worse than angry; he was doing arithmetic. “I was written down when my boy was born. The clerk wrote me as the house, and the house was a number, and when my wife died the number kept standing and I had to pay to be a man again in my own front room. I have been written down. It did not go well.”

“You do not have to give a reason.”

“I am giving a reason. I am not giving an answer.” Garn Fisk nodded at the rain. “You can write that a man declined and put nothing else, and I will not argue with the words *declined*, because that is a true one and there are not many of them left.”

The Crown officer from the Bellhouse gate was standing six feet away, and had clearly been listening for some time, and now stepped over with a wet notebook he did not open.

“Who called this meeting?”

“Weather did, and then I did,” Nell said.

“This is not a meeting. There is no notice of a meeting.”

“No, there isn’t. I pinned a sheet to a board. Anyone can read it or not read it.” She kept her hands at her sides, which cost her something. “Are you writing my name?”

“I am not writing anything.”

Jerm, who had held the north gate for nine years and had a square hand and no interest in anybody’s opinion, took out the watch book.

“I will write it,” he said. “Time, place, three people asked, three answers given, one refusal heard. I will not write the name of the man who refused, because he did not give it and I am not going to take it off him.”

The Crown officer put his notebook away and went back up the lane with the air of a man who had been told to be present and had failed to find anything actionable, which Nell suspected was the most useful thing that had happened all day.

She waited until they were out of the lane to sit down on the board’s stone bench, and discovered that her legs had decided, without consulting her, that they were finished.

The water in the ward had come up the lane behind her and stopped against the raised sill of the cooper’s shop. Three boys were moving a cart of something heavy up the higher street. Nobody was shouting, which Nell had expected and was grateful for, and which she also knew was not calm. It was people doing arithmetic of their own.

She got up and went up the hill to the Bellhouse.

Tomas was in the yard with his back to her, writing something on the header sheet, and he turned when he heard the gate. He read her three lines before he said anything, and she watched his eyes go over the same sentence twice, and she saw the exact moment his mouth tightened at the third one.

“He was not rude to you,” Tomas said.

“No.”

“He is not a coward.”

“No.” She took the paper back, which she had not meant to do. “You are going to want to say he is frightened. He is. He told me he was, in the middle of it. But being frightened is not the reason, and if I write *frightened* on that paper then the next person who is frightened will think that is what fright does to you, and it is not, and I will not have a lie in my own hand.”

“All right.”

“You were going to ask me to change it.”

“I was going to ask you whether you wanted to change it. I decided not to.” He looked at the third line again. “This is a better record than a yes. I will not pretend otherwise. A yes in your hand would have been worth less than his no, and the reason is that you are not the person with the most to lose by being written down.”

That was not comfort. Nell took it anyway.

“Write it like this,” she said. “One person will carry the words to the landing and stop there. One person has asked for time until she has found a record. One person has refused the route and has refused to be named. Do not say that three people refused. Do not say the ward has answered. Say that three different choices were made on the same afternoon in three houses, and put my sheet on the board as the thing they were asked, so that nobody can say they were asked something else.”

“That is exactly right and I did not think of it.”

“You thought of the four parts. I thought of the board.” She was aware of the shape of her own pride and distrusted it. “Is the claim finished? The sentence?”

“No. It will not be finished while the water is doing that.” He nodded toward the west road. “I have a man walking the west road at the second hour after noon, by his own choice, and a gate keeper who will only speak for his gate, and a claim that has to be able to contain both of those without losing either of them. I do not have that sentence yet.”

“Good. Do not have it yet. Do not have it at midnight by yourself.” She said it the way she had said it to her mother once and had been told she was eleven.

He accepted it. He was getting better at that, or more tired.

She took the household fragment out of her coat and held it up.

“I’m not giving you this. I said I’d keep it, and I keep it. But I’m going to read the two lines out loud to the watch captain, in front of the board, and the clerk can copy them, and the copy goes where the fragment is not.”

“Nell, that fragment came from your mother through a board clerk. If a copy is in the watch house, the Crown will be told there is a copy in the watch house.”

“Yes.”

“They will come for it.”

“Yes,” she said. “And then there will be a record that somebody made a copy on purpose, in daylight, at a board, where the people who took the name can see it happen. That’s a different kind of protection than hiding it, and it’s the only one I’ve got.” She put the fragment back inside her coat, against her ribs, where it had lived for days. “You can write that down too, and it will count as one of your facts and not as an act of kindness.”

He wrote it down. She watched him write it, and did not tell him that she had already decided to do it before she came up the hill, and would not have come up the hill at all if he had not asked her what she wanted recorded.

At the bottom of the hill, where the lane divided, there was a rider from the wool road with a square of paper tied to his saddle, wet through at one corner, bearing a stamp she had seen on two documents in her life: the Bellwrights’ Measure.

She took it from him and read it standing in the wet.

It was short. It said that the Measure had received a complaint concerning the private binding of signal apparatus at the Vale Bellhouse, that unbound route work and a held claim were held to be a hazard to public order, and that the trial handbell would be submitted for guild inspection on the morrow afternoon, and that any refusal would be entered as a challenge against the Bellwrights’ licence held by the workshop’s keeper.

She carried it up to the Bellhouse and put it on the bench beside the broken blank and the split file.

Tomas read it twice.

“They have called it a challenge,” he said.

“They have called it *hazard to public order*,” Nell said. “You told me two days ago the answer is not consent. That is the answer written on a piece of paper and put in a guild.”

“It is the answer written down. That is worse,” he said, “and it is also a room I have to walk into tomorrow and speak in.”

She looked at the bell under the cloth, at the sliver of paper wedged in its frame, and then at the yard gate and the two Crown guards and the river beyond them, rising in the rain.

“Then read them the same words,” she said. “That’s what your master said. Read them the same words and let the three of them answer however they are going to answer, and write down every one.” She pulled her coat tighter. “Including the one that is going to say you should have left the bell alone.”
