# Chapter 0035 — The Order He Could Not Defend

The Crown officer was at the low gate at dusk with a form, and the low gate is Hollis Renn’s gate and the end of Tamsin Rook’s route, and Oren Pike had spent nine days being careful about both of those facts and then a man in a satchel walked up to the gate post with a sheet of Crown paper in his hand and both of them stopped being careful at the same moment.

He was the same young officer from the ferry road, the one who had asked about the closed road and gone away with a sentence, and his name was Teale and he was a serjeant of the commission and about thirty, and he had come down the lane on foot.

“Captain. I have a form and I would rather give it to you in a room than in a lane, and I have brought it out here because the low gate is a signal place and if a form is read in a signal place it becomes a thing people repeat, and I would like this one to be a piece of paper between two men for one evening and a decision in the morning if you can manage it.”

“Read it in the lane,” Oren said. “And I will write it in the watch book in the lane, and then whatever you tell me I do with it will be in a lane and not in a room, and you may go back to Fourmile and say it was a piece of paper between two men and it was not.”

Teale looked at the low gate and the water coming through it and the millrace on the other side, and then he read it.

*TO THE WATCH CAPTAIN OF KELLS. FOR SEVEN DAYS FROM THE HOUR OF THIS DELIVERY: a record of every bell sounded within the ward of Kells, with the hour, the place, and the name of the person who sounded it. A keeper register, to be returned at the close of the seventh day. Any bell sounded by any person, and any bell joined to any line, wire or ground, is within this return.*

Ardle, who had been standing at the gate out of habit rather than instruction, said, quietly, “That is a keeper list by the back door.”

“That is a keeper list by the back door,” Oren said.

He took the form, and read it himself, and asked Teale three questions in the lane, in this order: whether the Crown would distinguish a bell belonging to the market court; whether a bell sounded by a person holding no duty would be entered; and whether a bell rung in the open, in a lane, with sixty people standing in it, was within the return.

To the second question Teale said that a person holding no duty was still a person, and that the form did not ask about duties and had no place to put them.

To the third he said that a bell rung in a lane with sixty people in it was the case the form was written for, and that he had been told on the way down that the ward had three public questions on a board and a courier who read a date off a slate, and that a bell with sixty people in front of it was not a private matter, and that he entirely agreed.

“Entirely agreed is in your own words or the office’s?”

“It is the first thing I have said that is my own words,” Teale said, “and I have been in this commission for eleven weeks and I have not made a habit of marking the difference out loud.”

---

Oren did not say no.

That is the thing about Oren Pike, and it is the thing that has cost him more this month than the seizure did, and he had worked it out himself in a lamplit watch house on the night of the ninth day and had been using it ever since: a watch captain in a Crown town has exactly two shapes available to him, and they are *obey* and *refuse*, and every Crown office in this district is built on the expectation that a man in that chair will pick one of those, and every office that has been handed the other one has had to invent a new procedure for it.

So he did not refuse, and he did not hand a Crown officer a register of the ward’s keepers on the seventeenth evening of the month on which three children had gone out of a lane on the tenth.

“Serjeant,” he said. “I will comply. I will keep a record for seven days of every bell sounded in this ward, and I will record the hour and the place of each. I will not record the name of the person who sounded it, and the form does not oblige me to because the form does not say *shall* or *must* anywhere in it, and I have read it twice to be sure, and a Crown form that does not say must is a request with a seal on it, and my clerk is in the market court at this hour and I will have it read by him in the morning and he will write what I have just said and I will sign it and you may take a copy of it to Fourmile in your own hand if you wish.”

Teale said, “Captain, that is not compliance.”

“It is compliance with the words on the paper. I have given you the two things the paper asks for and I have refused the third thing the paper implies, and I am doing it in a lane, in front of two of my own men, and I am going to write down that I did it, and then I am going to read what I wrote into the watch book and into the market court in the open, because that is the only thing I have got.”

“Will the office accept it?”

“I do not know. If the office does not accept it, then the office will have to send me a second form that says *must*, and it will have to say it in a form that names the fields, and a form that says must and names the field *keeper* is a different document from this one, and it will have a date on it, and a document is a thing I can answer, and what I have in front of me is a piece of paper with a shape on it.” He folded it. “And I will tell you, because you marked a difference out loud and I am going to return the favour. I do not think the office minds.”

“What do you think the office wants?”

“I think the office wants a page,” Oren said, “and a page is a better thing to hold than a lane, and I have given them the page.”

---

He wrote it at the watch house at the eighth hour with Jerm holding the book, and he wrote it in the plainest sentences he had, and it is the shortest entry he has ever made.

*Seventeenth day, at the seventh hour, at the low gate, a serjeant of the commission named Teale delivered a form requiring for seven days a record of every bell sounded in this ward, with the hour, the place, and the name of the person who sounded it.*

*I accept the hour. I accept the place. I accept every bell, joined or unjoined, held by any person, of any size, on any day of the seven, including bells rung in the open with people standing about.*

*I do not accept the name. Not because a keeper has a right of privacy, which is a thing for a person and not for a bell. Because a register of names of persons who sound bells, made by a Crown office in a ward, in the week of a round, is a list of persons who can be removed from a route, and a Crown office that has a list of the ward’s keepers by the front door is refused it and comes by this one.*

*I am aware that the Crown’s public position is that a name is how a ward is worked with and not how a ward is held. I have entered the Crown’s public position, which I did not learn from this form, and I set it down here so that nobody in four mile can say the watch did not understand it.*

*Entered, seventeenth day, eighth hour: the watch will keep a register of bells for seven days. It will contain hours and places. It will contain no names. And if it is returned without names, that is the whole of the return and I will sign it as it stands.*

He read it aloud to Jerm, and Jerm wrote it, and then Oren said: “Read it back and if you have heard one word of it that I did not mean, you will tell me now and I will change it.” And Jerm read it back and said, “It is what you meant.”

“It is what I meant,” Oren said, “and it is also the narrowest thing I could write, and I want that noted somewhere that is not a book, because a man who has spent nine days telling a ward to keep its record straight has just narrowed his own, and one of these days somebody is going to notice that Captain Pike keeps a good record of the wrong things, and they will be right.”

---

Ardle and Cask manned the north gate on the eighteenth and the nineteenth and the twentieth and the twenty-first, and they were angry about the covering page, and Oren left them there.

He did it on purpose and he wrote it in the book that he did it on purpose, and the sentence he wrote was: *I have put the two men who disagree with me at the gate for the days that matter, because a gate manned by two men who agree with their captain is a gate with no eyes in it, and because if there is a Crown wagon coming up the north road on the twenty-first, I would rather it met two men who think I have been wrong all week and have had time to think about it.*

He was in the lane at the seventh hour with Tomas before that, though, and he said the thing he had come to say, and he said it without any preamble at all, in the yard, with the door shut and the three seals on the outside of it.

“I am going to give you something and I am going to tell you what it costs, because you are the man who has spent this month learning to say what things cost, and I am not going to be the only one in this town who can do it.”

“Go on.”

“If your bell goes, the watch writes down the hour and the place, and the watch does not write down your name. That is a protection and it is real, and there is no form from that office that can make me put your name in, because I have refused it in a book with two witnesses and a serjeant’s name on it.”

“I understand that part.”

“The other part is the leash.” Oren stood against the bench with his hands behind his back, the way he stood in every room. “For seven days from last night, the watch is a record and the watch is not a route. I have said it in a lane and I have said it in a book, and the next time there is a woman in a doorway in this ward who needs a man in a lane to stand in it, I have told that woman in advance that the watch will come and write down the hour, and the place, and nothing else, and I have told her that before she asks. And I have told her that the watch will not stand there. Not because I am frightened of the Crown. Because I am two men and a gate and I have been two men and a gate for eleven years, and on the tenth of this month I was asked to put two men between a lawful order and seventy-odd people, and I did not do it, and I entered why, and I was right to enter it, and I would do it again, and I have not slept properly a night since.”

“So there is nobody to stand in the lane.”

“There is nobody to stand in the lane with a warrant, and there was never anybody to stand in the lane with a warrant in this town, and what I mean is that there is not going to be a man whose *job* it is.” He took his hands from behind his back. “Vale. I am going to say this once and I am going to say it in this yard with the door shut, and then I am going to go and stand at a gate. If you ring a bell in this ward, there will be no second answer from me. There will be a page with an hour and a place on it and my name at the foot, and there will be a Crown serjeant in Fourmile at the end of the week with a page, and the page will say where, and *where* in this ward is a hundred yards. That is the leash. It is not a rope round your neck. It is a record of where you were, made by a man who is your friend, for an office that is not, and there is no version of the next four days in which I can take that back.”

Tomas stood in his father’s yard and looked at the sealed door and thought about the whole of the last month, and about a page with a hundred and forty-one people on it and a page with two hundred and six, and about a girl with a slate, and about a man in a filing room who had said *do not tell me what you are going to do with it*, and about a courier who had said that she would answer a request and refuse a request and that he had no standing to know which.

“All right,” he said, and he got the day-book out of his coat, and he wrote it down, standing up, in about forty words, which is the shortest entry he made all month.

*Seventeenth day, ninth hour, in the yard. Oren Pike states that the watch will record the hour and place of any bell sounded in this ward for seven days and will not record the keeper’s name, and that this is a protection and also a leash, and that I have no standing to ask for a second thing. I have written it down. I have not asked for anything else and I am not going to.*

Oren read it over his shoulder and said, “That is a small page for a man with a whole month in him.”

“It is the right size,” Tomas said. “If I had written four sentences you would have taken one of them as a request.”

“I would have.”

“I know. That is why it is four words and no request in it.”

---

The yard got dark at about the fourth hour, earlier than it should have, and Tomas put the lamp on the bench and stood there for a while with nothing to do, which was the condition he had been in on and off for a fortnight.

Then he went down the lane to the watch house, which was not on his way to anything, and which Jerm saw him do and said nothing about.

Oren was at the table with the gate book shut and the register open in front of it, empty except for one line, with a ruled column and a date at the head of it: the seventeenth. The seventeenth had a single entry on it, which was the market court’s iron handbell rung once in the court loft to open a sitting, and Oren had written the hour and the place and, in the third column, the single word *none*.

“I have one question,” Tomas said, “and I have had it since the eighth hour and I have been walking down the hill trying to find a better way of asking it, and there is not one, so here it is.”

“Ask it.”

“If a bell rang in this ward tonight, and nobody answered it — ” Tomas stopped, and started again, because the first version had been a question about procedure and the second version was the one he had actually come to ask. “What would the watch do?”

Oren put the pen down.

“That depends entirely,” he said, “on which bell.”

“That is not an answer.”

“It is the only answer that is true and I am not going to give you a false one because you have walked down a hill to ask it.” He turned the register round so that Tomas could see the ruled lines. “I will give you the shape of it, because you are going to build something in the next two days and I would rather you built it with this in your hand than found it out on the twenty-first.

“If a bell rang in this ward tonight and nobody answered it, then there was no signal. That is the first thing and it is a hard thing and it does not depend on how good the bell is. A bell with no answer is a noise, and a noise in a ward is something people talk about at the board, and what they say about it afterwards is worth more than what they heard. And the watch would write down the hour and the place, because that is what I have just agreed to do for seven days, and the watch would not do anything else, because the watch is not a route and I have said so in a lane and in two books and I am not going to start now.”

“And if the answer came four hours later?”

“Then the watch would have four hours of page with nothing in it,” Oren said, “and that page would be true, and the page would be the thing that did the harm.”

He said it in exactly that tone, the way a man states a fact about a bridge.

“Because here is what I have understood in eleven years and what I am only now able to say out loud in a room with one other man in it. When a ward is frightened and something does not happen for four hours, what kills the people is not the four hours. It is that afterwards there is a document that says the ward waited four hours. A crowd that has waited four hours is a crowd that has been shown that waiting works. And the next time, the next time they will wait, and the waiting will be longer, and the page will be longer, and there will come a morning when there is a page in four mile with four hours on it and a Crown officer on the north road at the end of it, and he will not be lying. Every word of it will be what we wrote down in the dark with the best intentions we had.”

Tomas did not say anything for a while. The lamp made a small noise. The register lay on the table with its ruled lines and its date and nothing in it, and Oren Pike sat opposite it and did not offer him anything, and the watch house was very quiet.

“That is the whole of it, and I did not have to say it, and you would have found it out on your own in about four days at the rate you are working, and I would rather you had it tonight because I would like to be the man who gave it to you and not the man who let you work it out in a lane with a bar in it.” He shut the register. “I am not your protector, Vale. I have never been your protector and I have stopped pretending to be, and the reason I am telling you where the teeth are is that I have signed your name off a page of mine, and a man who signs a thing he did not read is responsible for what it is used for.”

He got up and put his cap on.

“Go home. It is the ninth hour and the lane is the lane and the ward is awake, and there are nine people standing at the mouths of the low lane tonight because a gang dug a trench in it on Monday and it has been filling since, and every one of them is looking for somebody to tell them what to do, and there is nobody, and that is not a failure of tonight. That is what tonight is.” He was at the door. “And if you do build one — and I have watched you for eleven days and I know what your hands do when they are idle — build it so that it says what is known and what is not known and who is responsible, and if you cannot get those three into it, then do not build it, and I will not think less of you for that, and I will think a very great deal more.”

He went out into the lane and left the door open behind him, and Tomas stood in a watch house with an empty register in front of him and understood that he had been given a leash and a reason for it in the same breath, and that Oren Pike had done it on purpose, and that Oren Pike would go on doing things on purpose for as long as there was anything left to spend, and that this was the man whose brother died on a night when a warning stopped.
