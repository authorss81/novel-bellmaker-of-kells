# Chapter 0029 — The Route She Will Not Give

She had not heard a note since the fourth day. That was the plain fact and she had been carrying it about the way he had been carrying his day-book, checking it, putting it down, picking it up.

A handbell is not one sound. A courier does not use the strike; everybody uses the strike, a boy in a market will hit a pot with a spoon. A courier uses the note, the part that hangs on after the hammer has stopped, and that part had gone out of Tamsin Rook’s left ear somewhere between the eighth hour of the sixth day and the second hour of the ninth, and it had not come back, and she had noticed it properly on the twelfth, in a washhouse, when a woman two tubs away asked her a question and Tamsin had answered the woman’s face correctly while the woman’s voice arrived at the back of her skull like somebody talking in another street.

So she could not carry. She could walk, and she could ring, and she could read a face from four feet, and she could put a hand on a wall and feel a bell two streets off in the stone. But a courier’s work is the note, and the note was gone, and no one in Kells was going to be told that, because a courier who says her note is gone is a courier whose bell is free.

Which is why, on the evening of the thirteenth day, she went out and walked her own route with Jerm and a slate and did not ring anything at all until the second hour.

She started at the north gate, because that is where the route starts and a person who has been frightened for a week starts at the beginning and works down, not at the part that frightens her.

“Here,” she said. “This is the gate. This is inside. Whatever happens from this step, whatever I do, nothing past the low drain gate is on this route, and I have said so in front of eleven witnesses and it is written in the watch book in my own words.”

Jerm wrote it. He had a lamp, and the wind was off the water and cold, and there was the particular smell of a towpath in the evening that she had not noticed was a smell.

They went down through the ward. That took a quarter of an hour at the pace of a woman who is checking.

“Here,” she said at the washhouse. “In the route. You can hear the gate bell from this step in ordinary weather, can you not?”

“You can. My mother could.”

“And past the stage?”

Jerm thought about it. “From the stage you can hear the gate. Not from the road above the stage. From the road above the stage you would hear the landing, if the landing was loud, and that is a different thing.”

“Right. That is the difference between a route and a reach, and it is the whole of the second argument I have had with this town, and I have won it.” She turned round and looked at the lane behind them. “I am going to say something at the gate in a moment. The words are *the three questions are on the board and there is a Crown notice up, and the ward board will read it to anybody who comes*. That is all. Nobody has asked me to say anything and nobody has asked me to repeat anything. I am going to say it once, here, with you beside me, and I am going to write the hour on the slate, and then I am going to ask you the same seven questions at the same seven places going down and coming back, and you are going to answer me before each one.”

Jerm stopped her at the gate with a hand on the rail before she could take the bell out.

“Say the thing you are about to do out loud, so that it goes in the book as what it is and not as what we would like it to have been.”

“I am going to ring my handbell at seven places, once each, with a named man beside me who can say what the sound is, and I am going to ask him the same question at each place, and I am going to ring once at a door a mile outside my route where nobody has asked me for anything. Under the rule I set on the ninth day I may not do that last one at all.”

“No.”

“No. Write it down. I will not pretend a measurement I made by breaking my own rule is something else, and I will not be the courier who calls a thing a rule when it is an excuse.”

He wrote it in the watch book, and she initialled it, and the entry reads: *one ring outside the route, at the instance of the keeper, against her own written rule of the ninth day, entered as an exception and not as a precedent, at her insistence.*

They did it. The word was four days old by then and it did not take long.

Here is the slate.

*North gate. Asked: from here, would you expect to hear it? Said: yes. Rang. Heard: yes.*
*Washhouse. Expected: yes. Heard: yes.*
*Market lane, above the ward. Expected: no, it is above the route. Heard: yes, and I will say that I heard the ward bell first and this one after, and the two were in the same stone, and I could not tell them apart at the time and I have thought about it since and I still could not.*
*Landing stage, a stone’s throw above the low drain gate. Expected: at the edge, yes. Heard: yes, and loudly, and I would say that a boy standing here hears a bell as though it were in the room with him.*
*Road above the landing, forty yards. Expected: no. Heard: yes.*
*The low drain gate itself. Expected: at the end. Heard: yes, and I put my hand on the gate while it was still moving and I could feel it in the gate, and the gate is a stone block four feet thick and the bell is nowhere near it.*
*First cottage past the toll shed, one mile. Expected: nothing at all. Heard: yes. And the woman came down to the road to ask who was calling the market, the same woman who came down on the ninth day, and she has now come down twice in three days on a bell I told her was not going to reach her, and I had no answer for her then and I have none now.*

She looked at that for a long time.

“There,” she said. “That is the whole of it, Jerm, and it took me thirteen months to be able to write it in a straight line.”

He waited.

“The route ends at the drain gate. That is the rule, and the rule is mine, and it is written in a book, and it has been the rule since before the wax. And from inside the route I cannot tell where the route ends.” She turned the slate round and pointed at the last three lines. “I hear it at the gate where it belongs. I hear it at the washhouse where it belongs. I hear it on the landing where the boy is standing at the authorized end and it is loud and he is supposed to hear it. And I hear it on the road above, and I hear it at the gate, and I hear it a mile past the toll shed at a cottage that is not on my route and never was.

“Now. Where does my rule stop being true? Not where the wax stops — I know where the wax is, it is in the crown of the bell in my bag and I put my thumb on it every morning. Where does the *reach* stop being my rule? I have been standing in it for a fortnight and I have been listening to it, and I cannot find the edge, and that is because there is no edge. It does not stop anywhere. It thins out. And a thing that thins out instead of stopping is not a route, and you cannot govern a thing you cannot find the end of.”

Jerm said, carefully, “So it is not safe to use.”

“It is *legible*,” she said. “That is the word, and I have been avoiding it for eight months because it is a soft word and soft words are how a courier loses a village. Legible means I can say what it is doing, and where, and how far, and what it is not, and I can say all of that out loud to a person standing next to me. It is not safe. I will not ring it with a child beside it, because the reach is uneven and I cannot say which houses are inside it, and the four of eleven who did not hear it are four people who will not hear a warning the next time either.”

“Then what is it for?”

“For a door.”

She said it, and then stopped, and made him write it down before she went on, because a decision she had not written down was a decision she could be talked out of, and she had watched a man in this town be talked out of a worse one.

“I am going to offer four things and I am going to offer them in the plainest words I have, and then I am going to say them once and not improve them.

“One: paper, on foot, at doors, with the words written on it and a person with me who can say what the words mean. I do that with my legs and it costs me my legs.

“Two: the handbell, rung once, at a door, only when somebody at that door has asked me to ring and has said what they want repeated. Not by the ward, not by the board, not by a keeper of a gate, not by a bellmaker. By a person at a door.

“Three: the handbell as a *call to come to a door*. That is a different job and it is a smaller one and I will say so every time. It says *there is something at this door*. It does not say what. It does not carry a claim. A person with a name rings it and then stands at the door and says the words themselves, in their own voice, to whoever opens.

“Four: nobody repeats it. Not for me, not for a keeper, not for a ward, not for a Crown. If a person at a door asks me to carry a thing to another door, I decide, and I decide on that door at that hour and I do not have to give a reason, and I will not be asked for one by anybody, including the man who paid for the wax in my crown.”

She had got to the end of it and her left ear was going and she could feel the note she had been missing, somewhere under the water noise, a half-tone below where it should have been.

“Vale,” Jerm said, “is going to ask you whether the second one can be arranged.”

“Yes he is,” Tamsin said, “and I want you here when he does, and I want you to write down what he says, because I have found that the things he says are honest and that I do not always like them.”

He came down the towpath at the fourth hour and she was still there, sitting on the parapet at the low drain gate with the bell in her lap, and he was carrying a slate as well as a day-book, and she looked at the slate and said:

“You have written something already.”

“I have written the heading.”

“Read it to me.”

“It says *what Tamsin Rook will and will not do, the thirteenth day*.” He showed her. “I have not written anything under it because I do not know what to write under it, and I was going to ask you.”

“You were going to ask me whether the second one can be arranged,” Tamsin said. “You were going to ask me whether I would walk the route tomorrow, on your account, with the finding, to the landing and the ferry road, so that the finding is in four villages instead of one. And I was going to say no. And I was going to say no in a way that made it hard for you, because you have had a bad fortnight and I have found that a refusal given gently gets tested twice.”

“That is not fair.”

“No,” Tamsin said. “It is not fair and I will not be fair. I am going to be exact. Ask me properly and I will answer you properly and you will get a real answer, and I would rather you got a real answer at two in the morning than that I had been kind to you at four.”

He looked at the water going out through the gate for a while.

“Tamsin Rook. Will you walk the route tomorrow and carry the finding to the landing and up the ferry road as far as the toll shed, on your own account, with nobody named, so that four villages have it in their own hands?”

“No,” she said. “And I will tell you three things for free, and then I am going home.”

“Go on.”

“One: my hearing is worse than I have told anybody in this town except you, and the note is gone, and I cannot carry, and I will not be able to carry again, and I have been a courier for eleven years.” She said it without any particular weight, the way a person says the price of a thing they have already accepted. “Two: I have had no paid run this week and I have turned down two, and the ferry company has written to say the ferry notice may not be carried on a bell described in a Crown notice, which is correct, and which means the towpath pays me nothing, and it paid me nine coppers a month, and that is not a large sum and it is the difference between a week that is hard and a week that is a different kind of hard. And I am not going to look for other work with the bell. Not because of the Crown. Because of the thinned edge.”

“And three?”

“Three,” Tamsin said, “is the thing you are actually here for, and you have not asked it yet, and I am going to say it so that you can write it down.

“I will answer a request and refuse a request. Both. In the same voice, on the same day, for reasons that are mine and that I will not give in advance. There will be a morning when you will ask me and I will go, and there will be a morning when you will ask me and I will not, and you will not be able to tell from anything — not from my face, not from the hour, not from how urgent the day is — which is which. That is not a policy. That is what it is to have a route that belongs to somebody. It is the reason the wax in my crown is a crime and the reason your custody receipt is a document and not a catastrophe.”

“Then I have no way to plan.”

“You have no way to plan, and I cannot give you one, and if I could give you one it would not be my route, it would be a delivery.” She stood up and shouldered her bag. “Write that down too, and underneath it write the other half, which is the half that is yours to write.”

He wrote it: *Asked on the thirteenth day whether I would carry the finding up the towpath. Refused. She will answer a request and refuse a request and I have no standing to know in advance which, and she will not give a reason in advance, and that is the shape of a thing that belongs to somebody else.*

He showed it to her and she read it and said, “That will do.”

“Vale,” she said, at the parapet, before she went. “One more thing about the paper, and then I am going.”

“Say it.”

“You published it this morning and you did not speak, and I want to say the part of it nobody is going to say to you because everybody is being careful with you. You have spent eleven days learning that you are not the man who says what a thing means. This morning you stood at the back of a step in daylight and let a girl of sixteen read your words to a hundred and twenty people, and then you did not correct one of them when you had an idea about how to phrase a sentence, and I watched you not do it, and it cost you something visible.” She shifted the bag. “I do not know what you are going to do with the rest of it. I do not think you are going to be asked to stand at the back of a step again very soon. And I want you to know that a person who can do it once has not been made humble. He has been made *out of practice*, and it is a different thing, and it wears off.”

Then a rider came up the towpath at the same moment, at a trot, with a lamp in his hand, and neither of them had to ask who it was for, because it was for the towpath.

He pulled up at the drain gate and got his breath first, which put him in the category of men who have ridden hard for a small thing.

“Court clerk sent me up,” he said. “There is a Crown man at the board with a notice and two questions, and he is asking the board, in front of about sixty people, whether this ward has appointed a keeper of any route at all this week.”

Tamsin said, “And what did the board say?”

“The board said nothing, and I was sent up the towpath to ask a courier, and I have been up it for an hour.” He wiped his face. “And then I passed two houses up the ferry road and both of them stopped me, and the two of them are the same two, and they are not the Crown’s friends, and they have each got a hold of my bridle and they want to say the same thing and they will not say it in front of each other.”

“What thing?”

“That they want a route of their own,” the rider said. “That they have gone to Fourmile, and they have asked to be put on a line. They have asked the Crown for a bell.”

Tamsin Rook stood on the towpath at the low drain gate with a handbell in her bag that could not find its own edge, and a Crown notice nailed to a board four streets away that said the Crown would rather be asked than refused, and the two of them, an hour apart, arriving at the same door.

“Well,” she said. “That is the end of the week I have been walking.”
