# Chapter 0021 — The First Hour of Light

The lamps in the market court had been burning since the middle of the night and by the first hour they had gone the colour of weak tea, and the court clerk kept trimming them anyway, because trimming a wick is a thing a man can do with his hands while his mouth is being used against him.

There were about a hundred and forty people under the tiles. Tomas had counted twice. More than a hundred. He counted the way he had counted everything for ten days, by faces and not by families, and then he stopped counting, because the number told him nothing except that a market court in a river town can hold a town.

Deputy Crake had the emergency custody order on the clerk’s table with his own hand flat on it, and he had been standing in that position for twenty minutes without speaking, which Tomas understood was a choice.

“It is half past two,” Crake said. “The round opens at the north gate at four. If the commission leaves this court with nothing before then, it does not open at all.”

“Then open the round,” Oren said.

Crake turned his head. The watch captain was standing at the end of the clerk’s table with his ledger shut under his arm, and there were two watchmen behind him, and neither of them had sat down, which Oren had made a rule of at the eleventh hour on the previous night.

“I am going to,” Crake said. “I have a schedule and forty-one households and a surveyor coming off eleven days of road with mud in her boots. What I do not have is a lawful answer to put in the box beside them. That is what this hour is for.”

“Then ask the question,” Tomas said.

Crake finally looked at him.

“Maker,” he said. “Your workshop is the whole of the matter. I am not asking you about your workshop, I am telling you that I have it, and that the hearing is being held, and that you are in the room, and that in about a quarter of an hour I am going to make a lawful thing happen in front of a hundred and forty people. I would rather you were standing at the side where you can see it than out in the lane finding out afterwards.”

“I will be at the side where I can see it,” Tomas said.

“Good.”

That was the whole of their exchange, and it was the first conversation Tomas had had with the deputy reeve in three days that had not been an argument, and he did not trust it, and he took the place he was given.

Oren opened his ledger.

“Before the commission applies,” he said, “the watch has four things to put on this table and the first of them is an objection, and it is mine, and I am putting it before anybody answers anything.”

Crake’s clerk reached for the ink. The black-case man had already got his own out.

“First.” Oren turned to the court. “The commission asserts that the market court’s call bell is a connected route. It is not. It is unbound iron, a leather-wrapped handle, a striker on a thong, a rope, no wire under it and no groove in its crown and no wax in it. Watchman Jerm inspected it on the sixth day in the loft, under the bell, and at the beam, and his inspection is in the watch book and in the watch archive. The court’s own usher is its keeper and has said so in open court. The bell counted three answers on the eighth day, including one refusal, and a count is not a line.”

“Entered,” the black-case man said, writing.

“It is entered as an objection and not as a finding, and I will not have it improved in either direction.” Oren put his thumb on the page. “Second. The custody order as read by the commission’s clerk covers *the whole of the signal apparatus held in the Vale Bellhouse*. That description has been standing for a day and it covers a bridge bell that a miller’s household depends on and eighteen objects on shelves. I do not have authority to narrow an order of this commission. I have authority to enter what the order will physically do, and I am going to enter that, and the court clerk will read it back.”

“Captain,” Crake said. “You have the order in front of you.”

“I have read it,” Oren said. “Go on.”

Crake took the paper up, and read the operative words himself rather than have them read for him, which Tomas had not expected and which cost him something to watch.

*The commission asserts and finds that between the eighth and ninth days a public signal was sounded in the market court of this town bearing the name of the workshop as keeper of the words, that the said signal was made in answer to a matter then pending before this commission, and that the apparatus by which it was made, together with all apparatus in the workshop capable of sounding a signal to the ward, is hereby taken into Crown custody for the term of the hearing and thereafter as the commission shall direct.*

“‘Thereafter as the commission shall direct,’” Oren said. “Read that phrase aloud, Deputy, and then say how many bells in that yard you are taking this morning.”

Crake read it again. Then he said, “All of them.”

“Then the millrace bridge bell goes this morning,” Oren said, “and the water in the Low Ward has no warning, and four houses at the foot of that ward took water to table height on the seventh day, and the man who keeps the gate cannot make the gate come up in an hour because his pawl slips. You may put that in the record as my objection, and I am putting it in the record, and I am also putting in the record that the commission has read *thereafter as the commission shall direct* as a present instruction and that if the bridge bell is taken this morning it is taken over my objection and not with my consent.”

“I have your objection,” Crake said, and it was, Tomas thought, the first entirely honest thing the deputy had said in the room.

Then the Crown’s filing clerk put a page on the table.

It was a single sheet, and it was clean, and Tomas’s stomach went cold before his eyes had finished with it.

It was the three answers.

Crane’s, Dace’s, Sarr’s. All three of them in one column, under one heading, in a clerk’s round hand that was not any of the three men’s hands. The withdrawal column was gone. The fifth column with Crane’s two withdrawn doors was gone. The word *amended* against Dace’s late entry was gone, and in its place Dace’s amendment stood as a clean second promise, as though it had been made at the same moment as the others and not twenty minutes later after a man had walked a road in the rain.

At the head of the page, in a heavier hand, it said: *RESPONSE RECEIVED ON ROUTE L-4, SINGLE, FROM THE LOCAL WARD.*

“Where did that come from?” Oren said.

“It is the commission’s own filing,” said the clerk, “in the form the filing requires. There is one response column on this form. If a ward has three answers, they are three answers to one question, and the question was put once and answered once, and the record of it is one line.”

“My column is not a question,” Oren said. “There is no question on this table.”

“The question is the matter before the commission.”

“Read the heading on the ward record,” Oren said, “and then read the heading on that, and let the court hear both.”

“I am aware of the difference.”

“Then read both.”

The market court clerk — Ferrow, his name was Ferrow, he had signed nine days of other people’s sentences and had not slept properly since the first day — put his hand flat on his own book, and Tomas saw the moment he decided, and saw it cost him.

He read his own heading. He read the merged page’s heading. He read them twice, which took longer than it needed to, and gave the room the two sentences side by side without a word of comment, and the room understood it in about a second and a half and then made a noise that took the clerk twenty seconds to write over.

“Enter that,” Oren said, “in the words the clerk used and without the clerk’s opinion.”

Ferrow wrote it.

Then Oren closed his ledger, which was the signal his two watchmen had been waiting for since midnight, and Tomas understood what was about to happen before it happened, and understood it two beats too late to do anything about it.

“Captain,” he said. “Do not.”

“I told you last night and I told you at the door tonight,” Oren said. “I do not have the votes. I do not have the Crown. I have two men who will do a lawful thing at four in the morning, and I have decided that I would rather it be done at this hour, in this room, on this record, than in a lane in the dark where one of them has to look a woman in the face while he does it. I am not protecting them. I am using them, and I am going to put that in the book as well.”

He turned round.

“Ardle. Cask. Take the handcart and the wrapping sheet from the yard. Bring the small trial handbell on its frame, do not unframe it, and bring the eighteen on the shelves. Nobody touches the millrace bell. If anybody in the lane puts a hand on you, you come back into this court and you stand where you are standing now, and I will deal with it in here.”

“Aye,” said Ardle.

“Aye,” said Cask.

Nell Fenn was on the second step and she was on her feet before Oren had finished, and the sound she made was not loud.

“You are doing it now,” she said. “You waited ten days to be sure and then you did it at three in the morning in front of everybody, and the point of doing it in front of everybody is that everybody is standing in the way.”

“Yes,” Oren said.

“Then what is it for?”

“It is for the record, and it is for the two men, and it is not for you, and I am sorry.” He did not soften it, because softening it would have been the cruelty. “I was going to be somewhere else at four.”

Tomas found that he had come off the step without deciding to, and was standing in the open floor of the court between the clerk’s table and the door.

He had a whole speech. It had been coming up his chest since the second hour of the night and it had four parts in it, and he knew what the four parts were, and they were: the order is overbroad; the trial bell carries one keeper and one answer and no second claim; the seizure will not make the ward safer; and I am willing to say so in a document if you will read it into the record before the bell leaves this building.

Every one of the four parts was true. That was the difficulty. Every one of them was also an argument that a man was making about a bell he wanted to keep, in a room where a hundred and forty people could not tell the difference between a man defending a workshop and a man defending a neighbourhood.

Ardle came back at ten past three with the handcart. The wrapping sheet was the good one, the oiled linen his grandmother had used for a bell that went in the water, and Tomas watched him shake it out and knew every motion of it.

The trial handbell came down the lane on its own frame, and it was still not rung, and the L-4 posting was still wedged against its crown with the crown showing on all four sides, exactly as it had been every day for ten days.

Oren did the inventory himself, and he read it out, and Ferrow wrote it out, and the Crown’s clerk checked it, and it was the most careful half hour of Tomas’s life.

One small handbell, newly cast, bronze, on its original ash frame. Frame sound, lip unworn, no tuning marks. One wooden sliver of paper bearing the market court’s L-4 posting, wedged in the frame, not in contact with the rim, removable by two fingers. One wax line in the casting, the maker’s mark of his father’s stamp. Nothing else in the crown. No line, no coupling, no second wire, no fastening to any bellwire in the town.

“State the condition of the paper,” Crake said.

“Wedged dry and uncreased,” Oren said. “Marked, not folded, and the ink is unbroken.”

“Take it out,” Crake said.

Oren did not look at Tomas. He put two fingers in the frame and drew the sliver out, and it came out easily, which was the whole point of how it had been wedged, and he held it up.

“One piece of paper, the market court’s L-4 posting, removed from the carrier and packed separately as witness matter at the commission’s direction,” he said. “Held loose in the packet, not under the wrap, so that it may be read by any person who asks.”

Nobody asked. Tomas understood that they would have, if there had been a clerk in the room who had been willing to be the one who asked, and there was not, and that this too was now a fact on the page.

Then the bell was wrapped, and the wrapping was done in the oiled linen with the frame, so that what came out of the Bellhouse lane was a long bundle with a shape in it, and two watchmen carried it down the hill in front of the court doors where the whole hundred and forty could watch it go.

The eighteen on the shelves went in a handcart behind it, under a rope.

Tomas stood on the second step and did not follow it down, because following it would have been a thing he did, and it would have been a thing the room would have been invited to read.

At twenty-five to four the market court clerk came to the front of his table with a Crown page in his hand, and a Crown clerk behind him read from it in a voice pitched for the tiles, and it was read the way a tally is read.

“Route L-4. Renewal response schedule. Forty-one households entered. Persons entered as answering, two hundred and six.”

It went through the room as a small flat sound, like a stone dropped on a floor.

Ferrow did not move for three seconds. Then he turned back nine pages in his own book, going slowly, the way a man does when he knows what he is going to find and wants the room to see that he had to look.

“Low Ward roll, same round, the day the notice went up,” he said. “Thirty-eight households. One hundred and forty-one persons.”

The room did the arithmetic. Tomas watched it happen: a hundred and forty people discovering at the same time that there were sixty-five people in a document who were not in the ward, or who were in the ward and had never been counted, and that a Crown clerk had just read the larger number out loud in front of an emergency hearing.

He got the clerk’s eye and held it, and Ferrow gave him a small nod that meant *not yet, and I know, and later*.

Tomas wrote the two figures on the back of his hand in the charcoal stub he had carried since the third day. Then he wrote the difference under them, because a difference of people is a different kind of number from either of the two it came out of, and he was not going to do that subtraction on a stair in the dark.

At the north gate, in the cold, Rhea Collis came off the last of the north road with her rod over her shoulder and a sealed case in her hand, and Oren met her there, and the round opened at four on a morning when the sky had not begun, on a schedule that had already been answered, by a surveyor who had never seen the page.

Behind them, up the hill, the Vale Bellhouse door carried three things and no fourth: the no-ringing notice, the Crown’s wax, and Oren’s watch mark, all in the same weather, none of them removable alone.

There was nothing of his left on it but the crookedness.
