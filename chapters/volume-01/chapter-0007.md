# Chapter 0007 — A Warning Without an Order

The no-public-ringing notice appeared on the Vale Bellhouse door before noon.

It was a narrow sheet of Crown paper, sealed with gray wax and weighted against the wind by a nail. It prohibited the ringing of any bell connected to the north-gate route after the hour unless a Crown mechanic had inspected it. The fixed carrier was named separately. So was every loose relay bell stored in the workshop.

Tomas read the notice twice, then used it to weigh down the paper holding his own measurements.

“Only public ringing,” he said. “It does not say I cannot make a bell.”

Oren, who had come to watch the door after delivering the order, glanced at the seal. “It says any bell connected to the route.”

“My workbench is not connected to the route.”

“The notice is not a debate.”

“It is a sentence. It has edges.”

Oren’s gaze moved to the locked casting room. “You are going to find every edge.”

“Yes.”

The Crown officers had left two men at the north gate and a clerk in the market court. The Bellhouse remained open because its ordinary customers still needed repairs, but no one wanted to be the first person to ask for a sound while the notice hung on the door. Access to the carrier was suspended, so Tomas had already lost the survey contract’s remaining fee, and the market customers were beginning to take their smaller jobs elsewhere.

He did not try to hide the loss. He wrote it in the day ledger beneath the survey contract: access to casting room restricted; labor stopped pending inspection; payment suspended by order. The ink made the problem smaller than it felt, which was useful.

Nell arrived after the market bell had rung once for noon. She wore a blue scarf over her hair and kept one hand pressed to the front of her jacket, where the household fragment was hidden.

“You said you were making a trial bell,” she said.

“I said I would try a bounded test.”

“Someone told the watch you were going to ring the carrier.”

“I was not.”

“The notice says you were.”

“The notice says what Halden wants people to believe.”

Nell looked at the locked door. “Does that make it false?”

“It makes it a claim. You can ask what I have actually done.”

That answer seemed to satisfy her more than a promise would have.

Tomas brought her into the casting yard, where the inner wall kept the north-gate sound from carrying directly into the workroom. He set a small crucible on a three-legged stand and measured ordinary bellmetal from the workshop stock: copper, tin, and a carefully weighed piece of lead. Nothing in the pile was Crown material. Nothing had a claim attached.

Sabin had given him no material. He had only said, “Use what you can afford to ruin.”

Tomas melted the alloy. The familiar smell of hot metal filled the yard. He poured the first small blank into a prepared mould, waited until the surface lost its mirror shine, and tapped the frame. The metal gave a dull, promising note.

Then he heard the crack.

It ran from the upper edge of the lip toward the crown, thin but unmistakable. Tomas set the tongs down and looked at the blank. A flaw that small would not hold through tuning. He could have cut it away, but the crown would have been weak. He would have made a bell that looked whole until the first hard ring.

He broke the blank out of the mould.

Nell watched the broken metal. “Can you make another?”

“Yes, if I have enough stock.”

“Will it cost you?”

“More than I planned.”

Tomas put the cracked crown fragment aside as a record, then set the rest of the blank back in the crucible. He remelted the failure rather than hiding it. This time he altered the cooling shelf, moving it nearer the wall where the wind could not disturb the pour. The second blank came out duller than the first, but sound. He trimmed its lip with a small file, then found a raised ridge where the metal had cooled against a flaw in the mould.

He corrected it. The old tuning file caught on the ridge and split along its length. Two teeth bent outward, leaving a bright notch in the wood. Tomas looked at the file, then at the blank, and understood that a successful casting would not repay the damage.

He repaired the blank with a narrower file and stopped before his hand began to shake. The afternoon light had moved across the yard. Sweat cooled on his neck despite the cold air.

“You are going to bind it?” Nell asked.

“I am going to try a private claim.”

“What claim?”

Tomas took a sliver of the posted Crown notice from inside his coat. He had cut it clean along the blank margin, leaving the route number, date, and broken seal visible. The paper was dry despite the rain. It was not a magical relic. It was an ordinary public record that could be pointed to and challenged.

He placed the sliver on the workbench.

“Here is what I know,” he said. “The notice schedules a dawn inspection on route L-4. It permits custody of unregistered minors. The fixed carrier rang after the notice was filed. I do not know who ordered the seizure, who removed the clapper, or who will answer for the danger.”

Nell looked at the paper. “You want the bell to warn people.”

“I want it to carry a narrow duty. I will warn that the posted inspection may take unregistered children, name the evidence and the limits, and leave each family’s decision to them. I will not say that the inspection is the crime. I will not say that every child is in danger. I will not tell anyone to move, hide, or obey.”

“If people hear it, what will they do?”

“They will do what their own judgment and circumstances require.”

Nell’s eyes narrowed. “That sounds like saying nothing.”

“It is saying exactly what I can support.”

He touched the paper. “The physical witness is this notice. I am the keeper. I accept the duty to warn about the inspection and the evidence I have. The bell cannot carry a conclusion I have not earned.”

“And what do I do?”

“That is the question I cannot answer for you.”

Nell looked at the broken blank, then at the notice sliver. “You need an answer.”

“I need a free answer. I do not need agreement.”

The distinction made her angry. Tomas understood why. A child who had learned that adults turned instructions into obligations might hear the phrase as another way to make her useful.

“If I understand it,” she said slowly, “I can answer as myself.”

“Yes.”

“And I can refuse to carry it.”

“Yes.”

“And no one can make me into a keeper because I say yes to one duty.”

“No. The duty would be limited to what you choose.”

She looked toward the yard gate, where a watchman could see them if he passed. “I understand the warning. I accept the duty to carry those words to one neighboring household, the one above the dyer’s shop, if that household chooses to hear them. I will not carry it to the market. I will not answer for the Fenn family or any other family.”

Tomas felt the shape of the claim settle in the room. It was not a command. It was not complete. It was enough to test.

He placed the trial blank in a small wooden frame and set the notice sliver against the crown while the metal cooled. He did not wrap the paper around the bell. The paper remained outside, a visible object that anyone could take away and examine.

“Keep your hand on the frame,” he said. “Not the bell. If the metal moves, let it move. If the claim becomes unclear, say so.”

“Why?”

“Because the answer has to remain yours.”

Nell placed her hand on the frame. Tomas took up the striker and waited until the last heat had left the crown.

Tomas struck the trial handbell once. The first note was low and plain. It carried no hidden sentence in the metal. It was a sound Tomas had shaped to survive a nearby room, not a sound meant to overwhelm the street. The notice sliver remained visible on the bench.

He spoke the duty once, naming what he knew and what he did not. The words were not a spell. They were an acceptance that another person could hear and test.

Nell answered. “I understand the claim. I will carry it to one household, and only if that household asks to hear it.”

Tomas struck the small bell a second time. The note acknowledged her answer without adding a command. He lifted the striker and did not sound it again. The trial handbell had carried a private claim, and the claim now had a shape that could be examined rather than repeated.

For a moment Tomas felt only the ordinary aftermath: hot metal cooling, the scrape of the broken file, Nell’s breathing, and the watch bell beyond the wall. Then the air seemed to tighten around the second tone. A pressure gathered near the bell’s rim, thin and uneven, as if a thread had been pulled through a knot.

He could not see who was on the other end. He could not hear a name, a feeling, or the reason for the pressure. He knew only that Nell’s answer was strained.

The sensation passed. Tomas staggered and caught the edge of the workbench. A high ringing filled his left ear, then his right, until Nell’s voice seemed to come from somewhere behind the wall.

“Are you hurt?” she asked.

“No.”

“You cannot say no and then lean.”

He took a breath and waited for the yard to stop tilting. “I can hear the answer is not easy. That is all.”

“Is that bad?”

“It is information. It is not a verdict.”

Nell pointed at the small handbell. “Did it work?”

“It carried the claim.”

“Did it tell me what to do?”

“No.”

“Then it worked.”

Tomas looked at the bell. The new metal was plain and uneven where the file had been corrected. The broken first blank lay beside it, useless but evidence. The split tuning file rested on the bench, its bent teeth catching the light.

“Yes,” he said. “Within the limit.”

The yard door opened. Oren entered with two watchmen behind him. He had seen the notice and heard nothing from the north gate, but his gaze went immediately to the small trial handbell.

“Put it down,” he said.

Tomas set the striker beside the frame. “It is private and it is not connected to the public route.”

“You rang it.”

“Two notes in a closed yard.”

“Under a no-ringing order.”

“The order says public ringing.”

Oren looked at Nell. “Did you answer it?”

“She accepted a limited duty to carry words to one household,” Tomas said. “She did not become a keeper. The signal is not a town answer.”

Nell met Oren’s gaze. “I can leave if you require it.”

Oren’s expression changed. He had expected Tomas to claim authority and perhaps expected Nell to hide behind it. Her answer made the situation more difficult, not less.

“No,” he said. “I require the bell to stay here. I require no public ringing. I require the witness record to include who accepted what.”

Tomas nodded. “That can be written.”

“When the carrier repeats, I will require the witness record.”

The words stopped him.

That evening, while the watch changed at the north gate, the fixed watch bell rang twice. The first note was low. The second was thin and clear.

It was the same two-note pattern Tomas had struck in the closed yard.

He had not connected the trial handbell to the gate. He had not touched the buried route. The notice was pinned outside, the casting-room door was locked, and the fixed carrier remained in its mounting frame.

Oren listened until the last vibration faded. His face had gone pale.

“Who repeated it?” Tomas asked.

No one at the gate could answer.
