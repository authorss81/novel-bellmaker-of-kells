# Chapter 0005 — The Child Who Was Not Named

Nell Fenn’s room was above a chandler’s shop in the Low Ward, reached by stairs that leaned toward the river. The door did not close fully because the frame had warped with damp. Inside, a narrow bed stood under the window, a cook pot hung over a small hearth, and three people had made the room their office without a clerk to record the work.

A woman with flour on her sleeves was sorting buttons into bowls. A man with a limp sat by the window, sharpening a knife that did not need sharpening. Neither looked pleased to see Tomas.

“My mother is at the laundry,” the woman said. “If you’re here about the rent—”

“I’m here about the paper,” Tomas said.

Nell slipped inside and closed the door as far as it would go. “You said you would ask whose hand it is.”

“I did.”

She took the charcoal copy from him. The smaller lines were clearer than the palm. Two fingers had been bent inward when the print was made. A thin crescent of ink marked the heel of the hand where someone had traced around it later.

“Jory,” she said. “My younger brother. He’s eight.”

Tomas looked at the child-sized handprint again. The wax on the fixed carrier had been made by someone small. The proportions fit. That was all he could honestly say.

“Is Jory here?”

“No. He was taken six days ago for a renewal check. My mother went to the public board to ask where the children were going. The watch said the notice meant they were being counted. Then Jory disappeared.”

The man by the window stopped sharpening. “The child was at the notice board,” he said. “He was asking why the new roll had crossed out the Fenn line. He was not a number.”

Nell’s shoulders tightened. “They said he had no civic entry. They said his father’s name was already used.”

Tomas looked at the copied print. “Your father?”

“Edan Fenn. He went to answer the renewal notice five days ago. A clerk gave him a paper and told him to bring the family rolls. He said he would return before the market closed. He did not.”

The woman at the table stopped sorting buttons. “Edan was a good rope-maker. He did not run from debt. He ran toward trouble.”

“Did anyone see where he went?”

“The west road,” Nell said. “A Crown cart took him and two other men. They called it a work detail.”

Tomas asked for the record. Nell retrieved a folded sheet from beneath the bed. It was a fragment of a household register, damp at the corners and marked with a survey seal. The seal was pressed into the paper, not written beside it. A route number had been added in blue ink.

The Fenn household was listed as three people, but the names did not match the family Nell knew. A fourth line had been cut away so neatly that only the edge of the letters remained. The children’s names were written below in a different hand, as if added after the family had already been recorded.

Under the surviving lines, a clerk had added: Nell is a person in her own right. Jory is not here. The cut line was where Jory should have appeared, though the fragment alone could not prove that.

Tomas read it twice. “This is not a civic roll.”

“It came from the board,” Nell said. “The clerk gave it to my mother when she asked why Jory was missing.”

“Someone made this fragment.”

“Someone with a Crown seal.”

“A seal can be copied.”

Nell’s eyes flashed. “You sound like the clerk.”

“Which clerk?”

“The one who says a thing is proper because it has a seal.”

Sabin’s words came back to Tomas: a record can make a lie look like law. He laid the fragment on the table. “I am not saying your family is not in danger. I am saying this paper is evidence of a process, not a complete account of what happened.”

Nell sat on the bed. “Can you find him?”

“I can look for the record of what was done. I cannot promise that the record will give him back.”

“My mother wants you to make a bell for him.”

“I know.”

“She says if everyone hears his name—”

“The bell will not find him merely because it carries his name.” Tomas kept his voice low. “A bell can carry a duty someone has accepted. It cannot carry a wish and turn it into a road. If I made one that sounded like your father, people might follow it toward danger, or toward a place that only the maker knew. The sound would be true as a promise and false as a guide.”

Nell looked toward the window. Outside, a child called to another across the lane. “Jory asked the same thing. He said if the adults kept writing names in different books, the names might come loose. He wanted to draw the missing line on the board.”

“Did he?”

“He did. A watchman erased it.”

Tomas looked at the copied handprint. “When you brought this to Sabin, what did you expect me to do?”

“Make a bell that would make them listen.”

“And if they listened?”

“Maybe they would find Jory. Maybe they would find my father.”

“Maybe they would panic. Maybe they would accuse the person nearest the road. Maybe the Crown would use the bell to prove that your household had accepted a duty you never agreed to.”

Nell’s mouth tightened. “You are good at explaining why not to help.”

“I am trying not to help in a way that makes the harm larger.”

“That is not the same as trying to help.”

“No. It is what I have instead of help.”

The admission did not make him feel useful. It made the room feel smaller.

Nell picked up the household fragment. “What do you need?”

“Names, dates, places, and the people who saw the Crown cart. I need you to ask your mother before I question her. I need you to tell me who copied this line, if you know. And I need you to tell me what you want to know first.”

She blinked. “What do you mean?”

“If you want to know where Jory was taken, I can start with the route. If you want to know why he was removed from the roll, I can start with the survey seal. If you want to know whether your father is alive, I cannot promise an answer, but I can look for his last recorded act.”

Nell considered the three choices as if they were doors in a dark room. “I want to know who decided he was not a person who counted.”

Tomas felt the answer settle between them. It was not the answer he could give quickly. It was also not a request for a miracle.

“All right,” he said. “We start with the roll.”

Nell gave him the date beneath the seal. It was the same date printed on the Crown notice Tomas had seen in the landing office. Three days before the fixed carrier rang.

Jory had been taken six days earlier and Edan five days earlier. This notice therefore could not be the beginning of their disappearances, though it might be part of the same process. Tomas wrote that possibility beside the date and did not turn it into a conclusion.

Sabin’s shop, the route beneath the Bellhouse, the west road, and this household record had all arrived at the same point in time. Tomas wrote the date on a clean sheet and did not add a conclusion.

Nell noticed. “You are not writing that it means something.”

“I am writing what I know.”

“My mother says you always keep the danger in your hands.”

“She may be right.”

Nell rose and tucked the copied handprint into her jacket. “If you find the person who crossed out Jory, will you tell me?”

“Yes.”

“Even if the person is important?”

“Yes.”

“Even if the person says the roll is correct?”

“Then I will show you what the roll says and what it leaves out.”

She studied him for a moment. “That is not a promise to bring him home.”

“No,” Tomas said. “It is a promise about what I will do with the evidence.”

At the stairs, Nell stopped. “The watchman who erased Jory’s line had a brass key on his belt. It was longer than the keys they use for doors.”

“Did he write anything before he erased it?”

“No. He pressed the line flat and said the child had never been entered.”

Tomas looked at the fragment in his hand. The missing fourth line, the survey seal, the child’s handprint, and the date all belonged to a record that had been made to appear complete. Somewhere behind that appearance was a person who had chosen to erase a name.

He copied the seal, date, and surviving lines onto a separate sheet, then returned the original fragment to Nell. “Keep this with you. If I take it, someone will say I stole it.”

She nodded. “I know how records disappear.”

It was the first useful thing she had said that made him want to stay.
