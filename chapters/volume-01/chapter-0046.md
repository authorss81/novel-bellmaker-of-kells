# Chapter 0046 — The Measure

The Bellwrights’ Measure did not sit on the twenty-sixth day out of friendship. It sat because on the fourteenth day of this month it had lodged a notice, and a notice becomes a sitting on the day the guild’s own rule says it becomes a sitting, and the guild’s rule says four days, and Tomas Vale had known the date since the middle of the month and had not said it out loud to anybody.

The room was the two rooms above the fish market that it had taken on the fifth day and had not given back, with the shutters open and the roof leaking into a butt of water that nobody had emptied, and eleven men and one woman at the long bench and a clerk with a wax tablet at the end of it, and the shutters open because a room above a fish market is a room people can hear from the street.

Sabin Fell was not there. He had not been asked to be there. He had said on the fifteenth day of this month, in a yard, that he would not be in the room when Tomas decided what to do with his objection, and he had said it in the same tone he used for everything, and he was sixty-one and he had meant it.

Nobody mentioned it in the first hour. That is how a guild works. You do not say a man is absent; you sit and you take the item and at some point in the second hour somebody mentions the item and everybody looks at the empty chair and nobody has to say a word about it.

---

“Two items,” Iven Sore said, “and I will take the second first, and I want the clerk to record that I asked for it and that nobody objected, because the order I take them in is the whole of my conduct today and I want it on the page before anybody can improve it afterwards.”

The clerk wrote it.

“The first item is the standing of the workshop of the maker of Kells, named in the commission’s notice of the fourteenth day of this month as the place in which the practice of this ward originated, and the commission has said it will consider it. The Measure has no view on that and will not be supplying one, and the clerk will enter that, and if anybody in this room thinks that is neutrality I would like him to say so now while it can be said cheaply.”

Nobody said so.

“The second item is a bell.” Sore put his hand on the bench. “Not the trial handbell, which is Crown property in a district building and has been since the tenth, and which I have not seen since the fifth day of this month, and which is not this Measure’s business today. A bell made in Kells on the nineteenth day of this month, out of the last of a shop’s own stock, in a yard under a Crown declaration, under a written permission from a Crown-appointed officer of the watch. I want it entered first, and entered separately, that the Measure has not seen the bell since the nineteenth and has not heard it sound, and does not propose to hear it sound, and that everything I am about to say about it is said from a record and not from an ear.”

“Entered,” said the clerk.

“And I am going to say the thing I am right about,” Sore said, “and I am going to say it before anybody else in this room has had an afternoon to prepare a better one, and I want that in as well, because a finding that is only correct after two hours of argument is not a finding, it is a truce.”

Nobody in the room had expected that, and Tomas, who had come up the stair expecting to be destroyed, found that he had to stand up and start again.

“Go on.”

“No. Sit down. You will get your turn and I am not going to have it before I have laid mine, because you are the man who is going to want to answer and I want the answer to come after the finding and not before it.” Sore turned to the clerk. “Enter the following as a statement of craft law and not as an opinion.”

---

He read it off the tablet, and Tomas wrote it down in the day-book that night in his own hand, with the hour, and it is the only page in this whole business that Tomas has ever wanted to keep in a drawer rather than on a board.

*One. A stock of metal is a traceable thing or it is not. A bell is traceable when the metal of it can be shown, to a competent person, to have come from a source that keeps a record of what it sold and to whom. Iron out of a hearth is not a source that keeps a record. A bell cast out of a hearth is not a bell of unknown origin; it is a bell of origin that cannot be shown, and those are different defects and the trade has kept them different for four hundred years for the same reason that a lock is kept different from a key.*

*Two. Where there is no certificate of a stock, the origin of a bell is established, if at all, by the making. The making is the mould, the pattern, the mark, the date, and the hand. A bell with all five may be certified by a master. A bell without one of the five may not be certified, and may not be made certifiable afterwards, and the deficiency attaches to the bell and not to the maker, and it may not be cured by the maker afterwards, however long he keeps it and however loudly he rings it.*

*Three. And where a bell is made in a place that is under a declaration of a Crown commission, that declaration does not touch the making. It does not make the metal better or worse, it does not make the hearth into a Crown foundry, and it is not a bar to the making of a bell. It goes into the record as a fact about the ground the bell was made on.*

*Four. But the fourth is the one that has a man’s liberty in it and I will read it slowly. Where a bell is made on ground that is under a declaration, and its stock cannot be traced, then there is in this county a bell which exists, which is in use, which is carried on a public route, and whose maker cannot show where the metal came from and cannot show it tomorrow, and which an office may require to be produced and which no person may prove is the bell that office has in mind. That is not a fault in the trade. That is a hazard to the public, and it is a hazard created by the declaration and not by the maker, and the Measure is bound to act on hazards created by declarations because a hazard created by a declaration is a hazard created by somebody who is not a bellwright and will not be looking for it.*

*I entered that bell into the record at the seventh hour of the nineteenth day. I did not enter it as a hazard. I entered it as competent work with two faults recorded, in a maker’s own hand, with the weight at the mouth and the weight at the shoulder and the difference named as his. I have read that entry four times this week and I did not see the fourth point, and it was in front of me, and it is in the fourth of these clauses, and I did not see it because on the nineteenth day I was a man looking at a bell and not at a rule.*

That was the whole of the finding that mattered and it took him four minutes.

The four houses wanted the other thing. That came out afterwards and it came out in the ordinary way, and one of the four was a man of about seventy who said that a man who loses a bell out of his own shop on the second day of this month and loses a child out of a lane on the tenth ought to be off the roll while somebody counts, and there was no malice in him at all and two of the others agreed with him in single sentences, and the fourth did not speak.

“And I am one of the four,” Sore said. “I said on the tenth day of this month, in a room, and in the words of the question put to me, that a man who loses a bell and loses a child in a morning should not hold a licence, and I still hold it, and I am not withdrawing it, and I would like it entered that I am right and that I am right about a different thing.”

“What thing?” somebody said.

“He has made a bell whose origin cannot be certified, in a yard under a Crown declaration, on metal out of a hearth, and he has rung it in a public lane in a market town, four times, at night, in a rain, and he is going to be asked to do it again, and the fourth clause is the reason, and he cannot cure it and I cannot cure it and the commission cannot cure it and nobody can.”

---

Tomas stood up at the point where he was asked to.

“I am not going to argue the fourth clause,” he said, “and I am not going to argue the second, and I am not going to defend myself, because I have nothing, and because Iven Sore has just said out loud in a room that he did not see a rule that was in front of him, and I am not going to be the man who gets up and uses a competent man’s honesty as a lever against him.”

The room did not expect that either.

“Two things and then I will sit down. The first is this: I did not write the fourth clause. It was given to me in a different form by a man who is not in this room, on the nineteenth day, at the first hour of the morning, in a kitchen, in one sentence, and I wrote it out and put it in the bell and rang it four times anyway. *A bell is a mouth. If you send only the true easy part, you have sent a version and not a claim.* Sabin Fell gave me that and did not think of it as a rule about stocks, and he has not been in this room, and I am not going to use him as a defence in front of you, because a defence that requires an absent man to have been more thorough than he was is not a defence. It is a way of making somebody who is not here carry a thing I am carrying.

“The second thing is this and I want it in the record in my own words. A man in this ward has been ringing a bell in a market lane and calling it a claim, and it was not one, and a man on the ferry road put a Crown’s words in the middle of it and repeated it four times, and I knew the bell was too bright on the day I cast it because I wrote it in the condition, and I read the condition, and I rang it. Iven Sore says a man who does not know the difference between a claim and a broadcast has no business carrying one. That is right. I did not know the difference on the nineteenth day. I do not know it this morning. I have had twenty-two days and I have learned a great deal about paper and nothing at all about bells, and I am going to say in this room that I do not know what a bell is for, and the room may put that in the minute book, and it is the first time I have said it anywhere and I would rather say it here than find out in a lane.”

“Entered,” said the clerk, and wrote it, and Iven Sore looked at the tablet for a while and then said, “That is the first true thing anybody has said in this room in eleven years, and I am going to record it, and I am going to record that it was not in my favour.”

---

The Measure’s finding took four minutes and it is the narrowest document any trade in this county has produced in a generation, and it is on the page in three places.

*That the bell made in the yard of the Vale Bellhouse at Kells on the nineteenth day of this month, being six and a half inches at the mouth and marked VALE KELLS and bearing no wax in the crown and no dressed groove, is a bell of which the origin cannot be certified, for the reasons in clause four of the statement of craft law read into this room; and that no certificate of it can issue; and that no master of this Measure may certify it; and that the deficiency attaches to the bell and not to the maker.*

*That the bell is thereby barred from the public practice of this Measure, and from any route, and from any notice, and from any bellframe in this county maintained under a Measure licence, and that it is not to be rung in any public place in this county.*

*That this finding is not a finding against the licence of the maker named, which is not before this Measure and is not questioned today; and that the Measure declines to extend the finding to that licence, and has been asked to by four houses, and refuses, and refuses because a stock that cannot be traced on the nineteenth day could not be traced on the fifteenth, and a deficiency that is a year old is not made into a hazard by being repeated.*

And under it, in Sore’s own hand: *Competent and expensive, and the second half of that phrase is what has kept him in business, and on the twenty-sixth day of this month I do not know which half is carrying which.*

---

The Crown’s notice of the fourteenth day — that the practice of the ward board originated in the Vale Bellhouse and that the commission will consider the standing of an originating workshop — was the third item, and it took nine minutes, and Sore was very good at it.

“The Measure has read that notice and the Measure does not accept it and will not help the commission with it, and the clerk will enter that in these words, and I will give the guild’s reason once and then refuse to be asked twice. A workshop does not acquire or lose standing from a district notice. A workshop is a set of hands, a bench, a licence, a stock, and a record, and those five things are what standing consists of, and a notice in a lane about where a practice came from does not alter one of them, in either direction. It can take the work. It cannot make a man answerable in a room for the fact that people did a thing in his building and then did it somewhere else for nine days.

“And I will go further than that, and the clerk will enter it, and I would like the commission to have it. **The Measure will not supply the commission with any matter touching the bells of this ward, and will not certify, and will not inventory, and will not give an opinion, and will not attend with a spade.** There is a Crown authority in this ward to ascertain what is in the ground of that yard on the twenty-seventh, and it is the commission’s own, and it is not ours, and if the commission wishes to put four men in a bellmaker’s floor it may do so with four men of its own and its own paper, and the Measure will be nowhere near it and will say afterwards in this room that it was not near it.”

He said it and sat down, and the clerk wrote it, and nobody asked him a second question, which was the point he had made in advance.

---

And then Tomas said the other thing, and it is the part of the day he had come for and the part he had decided in this room without asking anybody, and he has never denied that and has entered it himself.

“Oren Pike will not be able to answer the district’s question of last night,” he said. “They have asked the watch, in the watch’s hand, whether any bell in this ward is bound to a claim and by whom, and they have written that they do not accept *none* as an answer and do not accept it as a refusal. And Captain Pike will refuse it, because the whole of what he has done for nine days is to refuse that question, and because the four bells in the market lane on the nineteenth have a maker and if he says the maker’s name then there is one name in a district file for ever.

“But there is a second thing in the district file about this trade and nobody in this room has mentioned it, and I am going to mention it because I am not going to say it in a lane and I am not going to say it at a grave.”

He put a sheet on the bench. It was a copy, made in his own hand, of the receipt, and the receipt was the Crown’s receipt for the Bellwrights’ Measure’s road map into a district file on the fifteenth day of this month.

“The district has a form with your fifty-three withdrawals on it,” he said. “And the officer read the *refused* column, and asked the year of the first, and was told that the Measure keeps dates, and then said that he must file it as a schedule of non-response. Non-response. That is the finding. That is what is on the file, and it is on the file in your own hand and in mine, and in about a year somebody in that office is going to read a document called a schedule of non-response and use it on the next man who comes up a lane with a form.

“And I have not been to Fourmile and I am not going to. What I have done is this, and I am going to hand it to your clerk and let the clerk read it into the book, and I am going to leave a copy with the market court, and I have not spoken to Sabin Fell about it, because on the fifteenth day he asked me not to be in the room when I decided what to do with his objection, and I have honoured that in a way I do not think he is going to thank me for, because what he asked for was that I decide it and he would find out.”

He read it out.

*To the district renewal office at Fourmile. From Tomas Vale, maker, of Kells, in the matter of the return of the road map of the Bellwrights’ Measure received into the district file on the fifteenth day of this month: that the map is the working road map of a craft and not a return; that every mark on it is a ward that has taken its answers back, with a date and a reason taken from a key of four; that a withdrawal is a craft record and is not a non-response, and that the key distinguishes* line cut by the office, *from* keeper withdrew, *from* answers falsified, *from* ward dissolved; and that the district, having read the refused column, has not read the key, and that if the district wishes to file the map as a schedule of non-response it is asked to enter on the same file that the map is a map of refusals, and that the Measure keeps dates and not reasons, and that a ward that has withdrawn has answered.*

The clerk read it twice and then asked whether it was to go on the minute book of the Measure, and Tomas said yes, and it went on the minute book, in the hand of the Measure’s clerk, with the hour, and the clerk initialled it and initialled the copy and gave a copy back.

Iven Sore, at the end of the table, had not moved for the whole of it and said afterwards, to nobody in particular:

“That is a well-made paper and it will be in a file in about a week and it will change nothing, and I want that entered as my opinion, and I want it entered as the opinion of the master of the Measure, because the master of the Measure has been in this trade for thirty-four years and has read a great many well-made papers go into files and change nothing, and it is the one thing I can teach anybody in this county and I cannot teach it in a room like this one, because a room like this one is where well-made papers are made.”

---

Sabin Fell came up the Bellhouse lane at about the seventh hour of the evening of the twenty-sixth day of this month, and he did not come into the yard, and he had not been in the room, and he was sixty-one and he had asked not to be there and had found out what was done in about three hours from a clerk’s boy.

He stood at the gate with his coat on.

“You put it on the minute book.”

“Yes.”

“Of the Measure.”

“Yes.”

“You did it in the room I asked you not to be in.”

“Yes,” Tomas said.

“You did not ask me. You did not write to me. You did not come to my shop, which is four streets from where you are standing, and which you have walked past eleven times in three weeks.” Sabin’s hands were behind his back and he was not using them. “Not once. Not a line. Nothing. And I have known you nineteen years and I taught you the first four of them and I have refused to protect you three times in a month and I gave you a rule on the morning of the nineteenth that I have not given anybody in twenty years, and you have gone into a room and answered a question about **my** map, in **my** trade, on **my** behalf, in writing, with **my** key, and the district is going to read it and file it and not act on it, and it is in the minute book of a guild under a Crown licence, which means it is a Crown document, which means that in about four months a man in an office is going to be able to say that the master of the Measure was served with notice of this and did not answer in his own hand, and that is a thing I can be finished on.”

“I know.”

“You do not know, you are nineteen years into a trade and you have lost a bell and a child and a shop in a month and everything feels like the biggest thing that has ever happened to you, and I am sixty-one and I have been losing things since before you were born, and the biggest thing that has ever happened to you is a boy’s errand and I have to hold my own life up.” He stopped. “I did not come up here to be told you did the right thing. I have known for a week that the right thing is to say that word in an office and I would have said it myself in about a year, when I had decided whether to say it at all, and I would have said it in my own hand and with my own reasons and I would have consulted the four masters whose map it is. And you have got there in a day and a half and you have done it out of my office with my key and my words and I have not been asked, and you have honoured my request in the exact way a man honours a request he is pleased with.”

“Yes,” Tomas said. “That is what I did.”

“Then write it down.”

“I have written it down. It is in the day-book and it is in your own words and it is on the page under the heading of what it cost, and it says that I decided in a room you asked not to be in, and that I did not ask you, and that I have no answer to that except that I had two days and the district is coming on the twenty-eighth and I could not do it on the twenty-eighth.”

“Do not do that.”

“Do what?”

“Do not be able to do it on the twenty-eighth,” Sabin said. “That is the sentence of a man who has found a good reason and would like a witness. Write this instead: *I was in a hurry, and being in a hurry felt like duty, and it was not duty, it was the other thing, and the other thing is what I have spent nineteen years trying to get out of you and I have failed.*”

He stood at the gate for a moment longer.

“Give me the copy,” he said. “Not to read. To put in my own drawer and to know it exists, which is a different thing, and which is the only thing I am entitled to.”

Tomas gave him the copy and Sabin Fell put it inside his coat and did not look at it and went down the lane at his own pace, and at the corner he turned round and said one more thing across about forty feet of cobbles, which is not a distance at which men of sixty-one shout, so he said it quietly and Tomas heard all of it.

“And when they put a spade in your floor on the twenty-seventh, which they will and which is lawful, do not stand in your own yard and let it happen. Stand in your own yard and watch every inch of it, and write down every hour, and hand the writing to somebody other than yourself, and *do not let it be the fourth thing in a week that you have kept in a drawer nobody reads.*”

---

Sore came down the lane at about the ninth hour, alone, without the map case, having sent the clerk home first, which was the first time in this month that anybody from the Measure had done anything in this town for the sake of a conversation.

“Two minutes,” he said. “I have a licence in my own name to look after and I am not spending ten on a man who has been found right about in front of his trade today and has not left the county.”

They stood in the yard with the door shut and the three seals on the outside of it.

“You came to the room on the fourteenth day of this month with a Crown docket and put three questions to me about a withdrawn service entry,” Sore said, “and you did not get an answer and I told you why, and the reason has not changed and I am not going to improve on it this evening. I can support that I have never held a Crown survey commission in this ward. I cannot support the rest. I have not said I did not sign it, and I am not going to say it tonight, and if you write down tonight that I did not say it, I will read it and correct it, because that sentence would be doing something in this town that I have spent thirty-four years refusing to let a sentence do.”

“Understood.”

“But there is one thing about that entry that I can support, and it is a question, and I am going to put it to you now because after the twenty-eighth I will not be able to put anything to you, and because a man who is going out of a town is the only kind of man a guild master may say a true question to without it becoming a matter of craft discipline.”

He took a folded sheet out of his coat and did not open it and Tomas understood that it was the docket and that Sore was not going to look at it either.

“A Crown service entry is made by an office, in a book, in one hand, and it means that a man of a craft has accepted a commission. It is not a record that he did the work. It is a record that he was **asked**. And when a Crown office withdraws one, it strikes a line through the name in its own book, and that is all it does. It does not consult the craft. It does not write to the man. There is no step in that procedure in which a person learns that his name has been taken off him.

“And in this county there is one other place where that fact exists, and it is not a Crown office. It is the service book of the Bellwrights’ Measure, and it is in a room above the fish market with the shutters open, and there is a name in it in a guild clerk’s hand, and there is a page beside it with no entry on it, and I have looked at that page four times in twelve years and I have never once been able to make it do anything.”

Sore put the sheet back in his coat.

“So here is the question, and it is the only one I have, and I am aware that you cannot answer it, and that is the whole reason I am giving it to you tonight instead of writing it down and lodging it.

“Vale. In this ward, on this route, in the last twelve years: **did the Crown withdraw a commission that the Bellwrights’ Measure granted — or did it withdraw a name?**”

He waited about as long as a man waits for an answer he has already decided he is not going to get.

“I cannot tell you which that is either,” he said. “Not because I am being careful. Because a man in my position has been in this trade thirty-four years and I have never been able to tell the difference between the two in a document, and it took a Crown foreman with a spade in a lane in the middle of this month to teach me that the difference is real and checkable and is in a book, and that a man can find out which one happened by going and looking at the ground with a bar and his own eyes.”

He put his cap on.

“Do not answer it. I have not asked you to answer it. I have asked it so that it is in the air once, in a yard, out loud, between two people, and so that when somebody comes to you in three weeks or three years and asks you what you are looking for, you do not have to invent a reason, because you will be able to say that a master of the Bellwrights’ Measure put it to you in your own yard on the twenty-sixth day of this month and would not write it down, and you can prove that by the fact that there is nothing written down.”

He went to the gate and then stopped with his hand on it.

“Your bell is barred,” he said, “and you should know what that is worth, because a man who has been told by his trade that his bell cannot be traced may be a man in a room in Sennet in two years with a great deal less protection than a man with a licensed bell and a stock book. That is not me being kind. I have entered the finding because it is correct and I have refused the other finding because it is not, and I would like you to go on being alive, and the two things are not in the same place in my head and I would find it difficult to explain if you asked me.”
