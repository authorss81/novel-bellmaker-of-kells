# Chapter 0032 — What Was Under the Ground

The copper came up at the fourth hour in the low lane and it was not what anybody expected, and the reason nobody was ready for it was that the ward had spent eight days arguing about a page and the page was about children and the children were eleven of them and they went down that lane twice a day whether anybody was in it or not.

It came up in three lengths, hand’s breadth, sheathed in a black cloth that had gone to the consistency of tar, and Brann’s men laid it on the bank in a line the way you lay a fish, because that is what a gang does with a thing it did not expect and has decided to respect.

Below the copper, at about a foot and a half, there was lime that had set like a floor. Below the lime, on the far side, was not lime at all. It was a stone channel, pitched with flat stones in lime, a hand wide and a hand deep, running the length of the lane under the crown of the old cut, and it was not the Crown’s, and it had a grating on it that the ward had been walking over for four generations.

Tomas was standing in the trench when Perry found it, because Perry was taking notes and the notes had run out of things to say about a trench.

“Mister Vale,” she said. “This is a drain.”

“It is.”

“It is in the Crown’s plan.” She had the works order and the sheet that came with it spread on the bank, weighted with three stones, and she had been reading the ground part of it since the ninth hour. “There is a line drawn on it and it says *existing culvert* and it is in a different ink and it is dashed. Sir, it is in a different ink.”

“I saw that.”

“It is in a different ink,” she said, “and my foreman dug three hundred feet of Crown renewal today on a sheet where the ward’s own drain is drawn in a different ink from the Crown’s, and somebody in an office chose that ink, and I would like to write down that I said so to the maker before I knew what it meant.”

“Write down that you said it to me and not that it means anything,” Tomas said. “You do not know what it means yet. Nobody does. If you write down what you think it means in a Crown book it will be in the file for ever and it will be a boy of nineteen’s opinion about an office, and it will be read by somebody choosing between two explanations, and they will pick the one that makes their week easier.”

Perry wrote that down instead, which was a thing he was not going to get used to.

---

Halden Crake came down the lane at the fifth hour with a black case and did not go in the trench, and stood at the top of it on the bank with his hands behind him and looked at the channel for a long time before he said anything at all.

Then he said: “How old is it.”

“I do not know the year. I know the shape,” Tomas said. “That is a ward drain. It is a pitch of flat stones and it goes to the washhouse trough and it was laid when there was a mill on this reach and somebody wanted the washhouse to have water without going to the river, and the river is forty yards that way and takes the children’s ankles off when it is in, so I would say somebody clever laid it. The pitch goes three courses and then the courses go ragged, and ragged means a hundred years, and I would not put a date on it in a court.”

“Will it carry the new trench above it?”

“For about eleven feet of it, yes. It is laid across the channel and not over it, which means the channel has been bridged in four places, and a bridge of Crown lime on a hundred-year-old pitch is better than the pitch and worse than the bank.”

“Will it carry a man.”

“It will carry a man who weighs eleven stone standing still. It will not carry a man who weighs eleven stone with a bar in his hands on the tenth of a wet month, and it will not carry a foot of rain in a hard hour, and the water has nowhere to go at the low end of that lane except through Renn’s gate, and Renn’s gate has not come up since the seventh day and there is lime and gravel packed behind the sill that nobody has cleared, because nobody has been able to tell the watch it is a fault and a Crown man would put it in a return as a Crown work.”

Crake’s hands came out from behind his back.

“Say that again.”

“I am not going to.”

“Say the part about the gate again, Vale. It is the part I want.”

“You have got it. I said it once and it is on your own ears and I will write it down and give you the page and then it is on paper, and then you may have it twice, and if you ask me for it a third time you will be asking a man to say a thing three times, which is a different act.”

Nobody at the top of the lane said anything, and at the low end of the turn Gib Hask was standing with his crowbar and had heard all of it, and Tomas understood that Hask had heard all of it on purpose and that a man with a crowbar who has understood the drainage is a man who can now say something true about why the copper is dangerous, and that true thing will be in a street by noon.

The deputy reeve looked at the channel and then at the water coming past the millrace on the right, which was out and low and making its ordinary noise, and then at the eleven feet of lane and the flat stones where the children crossed the grating.

“Here is what I have,” he said, in a completely different voice. “I have a Crown crew at the foot of a ward on the fifteenth day, on a works order, at a point an office marked out for me on a plan on the eighth day, and now on a different point on the same plan. The plan is wrong about the ground. I have been told so by a bellmaker in a lane and I have verified it myself with a spade and the truth of it is not in dispute this morning. And if what he has just said about the drain is also true, then a rain puts a foot of water in that lane in an hour, and Renn’s gate will not come, and there are eleven children who cross that lane twice a day, and there are four hundred and eleven people above it who have spent a week being frightened of a piece of paper.” He shut the case. “I have a commission to renew a line in this ward and not a flood authority, and if I write what I think you have just told me, I will be reporting that a Crown work has created a hazard in a ward and the work will be suspended, and the line will not be laid this year, and the ward will have no warning in the ground at all.”

“Then do not write it.”

“I am going to write it,” Crake said, “and I am going to write it in my own name, and I am going to write it because it is true and because if I do not, then the next time it rains there will be a foot of water in that lane and it will be in your lane and every person in it will say the Crown put it there, and they will be right, and I will have had a day in which I could have said otherwise. What I want, Vale, and I am asking you plainly and in front of these people, is a name.”

“What for?”

“For the file.” Crake did not raise his voice. He never raised his voice. “I have to file a correction to a ground plan and a correction takes a source. If the source is a Crown foreman who read his own sheet, then I have a document, and the office corrects a sheet, and a man is not ruined over a sheet. If the source is the maker of a workshop that has an inventory on it and a notice nailed to its door and a practice on a board that the commission has declared originated in that workshop, then the source is a man with a notice, and the source gets cross-examined in Fourmile instead of a sheet getting corrected.”

“That is a good reason and you have said it in the open and I am grateful for the honesty of it,” Tomas said. “And the answer is still no. You get the page. You get the hour and the words and the fact that four men with a spade verified it. You get a young woman’s name as the hand that wrote it, because she asked for it to be there and she is owed it. And you do not get a man, and if you put a man in it I will read it out at the market court tomorrow and the sentence I will use is that the Crown office corrected its own ground sheet on the word of a man it has already listed, and then I will ask what else in this ward came off a sheet nobody read.”

Crake considered that for about as long as it takes to count five.

“That is not a small threat and you have made it in a lane with eleven children in it,” he said.

“Yes.”

“All right.” He unhooked his case and took out a page and gave it to Tomas, which is not what Tomas had expected. “Write the ground. Not for me. Write it, and give me a copy, and I will put my own hand on the copy and file it and it will be in the Crown’s file and not only in yours. And write the gate in it, and write the children in it, in the figures your own girl counted, because a file that says *children* without a number in it is a file a clerk can move.”

---

He wrote it in the trench with Perry’s back as a screen against the wind, on the back of a Crown works order because there was nothing else to write on, and it took a hundred minutes and it is the most careful page anybody in this town has written.

*What is in the ground at the low lane, the fifteenth day, from the fourth hour, in the words of Tomas Vale, maker, and of six other people, four of them Crown road hands, one of them Deputy Reeve Crake, and one of them the writer.*

*One. Under the Crown renewal cut there is an old filled service cut, the washhouse run, sixty years old or more, of which the visible traces are the paler line across the lane and a bell offcut with a casting seam on it, found at three feet in the new trench at the first mark.*

*Two. Under the Crown copper at a foot and a half there is a lime bed. Under the lime there is a stone channel, hand wide, three courses, running the length of the lane, which is a ward drain and not a Crown work, and which the Crown sheet sent with the works order shows on the same drawing as the Crown line in a different ink and in a dashed stroke.*

*Three. The Crown line is laid across that channel and not over it, and is bridged at four points in the length dug today.*

*Four. A conduit from the millrace to the washhouse trough runs within a yard of the channel for about forty feet, and we did not lift it and did not try to, and I cannot say what is on it or whether anything is.*

*Five. The water at the low end of this ward stands or goes according to Hollis Renn’s gate at the foot of the landing, and that gate has not come up since the seventh day of this month, and the reason it has not come up is in the watch book in Renn’s own words, and it is not a Crown work and no Crown man has been asked to clear it.*

*And this is the part I am able to say and the part I will not improve: I have no instrument. I am a maker of bells and I know where a trench is by looking at it, and I know where the Bellwrights’ cut ran by my father’s hand in a bench-book that was never copied and was never sent anywhere. If there is a live line under this ward I cannot find it, and if there is one I cannot tell whether it is live. Every word on this page was either dug out, lifted, or read by six of us together. There is not one word on it that I took out of the air.*

The last line is the reason the page is worth anything, and Tomas wrote it last, and it took him longer than the rest of the page together.

Deputy Crake read all of it standing up, in the wind, and then signed it, and wrote under his own signature: *I have verified items one, two and three myself at the fifth hour of the fifteenth day. I have not verified item five and I accept the watch’s record of it. I am filing this with my own name on it against my own works order of the eighth day.*

Then he said, without any particular weight, that he was right about the children, and that he had been right about them since the tenth day, and that he had said so in a return and had been right and had been thanked for it by nobody including himself.

---

The watch house at the ninth hour, and Oren had the map case on the table with the lid off, and the case was empty and had been emptied at the eleventh hour and had not been in the building since.

“Sit down. I have to tell you about the eleventh hour and then I have to give you something and I would rather do them in that order because you will be angry about the first and I want you listening for the second.”

The road map of the Bellwrights’ Measure had gone to the district at ten minutes past the eleventh hour of the fifteenth day, in a sealed leather case, into the hands of a district officer with a receipt for it, and fifty-three dated and keyed marks went with it, and Oren had produced it and had read into the watch book that he produced it under compulsion and not as a witness to its contents, and a Crown copyist had written that in too.

“What did he say?” Tomas said.

“He read the list aloud. All of it. Fifty-three wards, every one of them a withdrawal, and he read the *refused* column and not the *agreed* column, and I want that entered, because I expected him to read the other one and he did not. He read it in about four minutes and he asked two questions. He asked what year the first one was, and he asked whether the Measure kept the reasons or only the dates.” Oren shut the case. “Kept the dates, I said. He wrote that down. And then he said, *that is unfortunate, because I have to file it as a schedule of non-response*.”

Nobody spoke in the watch house.

“Nobody asked Sabin,” Oren said. “That is what I am angry about, and I am not angry at you. He was not in the room, he was not asked, he was not sent for, and nobody in that office has any idea that the fifty-three marks on that map are the work of a man who is sixty-one and lives four streets from where I sit. He will find out tomorrow because somebody in a shop will tell him, and by then it will be in a district file under a seal, and I could not stop the file and I could have stopped the man, and I did not, because I was busy.”

“You could not have known.”

“I could have written to him at the seventh hour and told him a district officer was coming for the map. That is all it would have taken, and I did not do it, and the reason I did not do it is that I did not want to be in a room with him while it happened.” He said it flatly. “Enter that in your book as the reason, because it is the real one and it is worse than the other one.”

He pushed a sheet across the table. It was a copy of Tomas’s page, and Oren’s own hand was on the bottom of it, and the bottom line of the bottom said: *Entered in the watch gate book, fifteenth day, ninth hour: that the ground of the Low Ward is not correctly recorded in any sheet held by the Crown, and that the watch holds a page describing it, and that the page was made in a lane by the maker of the Vale Bellhouse and six other people, four of them Crown road hands.*

“I am not going to read that page out in a court,” Oren said. “I want that understood. It is the only paper in this town that says something about the ground that anybody has checked, and the Crown has a copy, so there is no point in reading it, and I have a week to get to the twenty-first and I am not spending a morning of it on a page about lime and a drain while a Crown office is learning how to make a procedure out of us.”

“Fine,” Tomas said.

“There is a second thing and it is the reason I have been sitting in this room since the fifth hour.” Oren took his cap off, which he had not done in eleven days in front of Tomas, and the watch house was very quiet with the lamp in it. “Vale. I have not slept and I am going to say a thing and then I am going to ask you to go away and let me be wrong in private. Come to the yard with me.”

---

The Bellhouse yard at about the tenth hour was in shadow with the ridge behind it and the millrace making its noise on the far side of the wall, and the yard door was shut and had three things on it, a no-ringing notice, a Crown wax, and Oren’s watch mark, and none of the three would come off without the other two.

Tomas unlocked it. The yard was as he had left it. The millrace bridge bell on the north wall under its condition. The bench with the wheelwright’s finished counter bell on it and a cloth over it. The casting room door shut, with the pale rectangle of unfaded floor inside it where the carrier’s frame had stood for sixteen days.

“Look at your floor,” Oren said.

“The floor is out.”

“Look at the floor in the rain.”

It had rained in the night. It was a heavy rain, and it had stopped by the seventh hour, and the yard was a yard with a stone floor laid in a slope from the door to the back wall with a shallow channel at the low corner, and it drained in about a quarter of an hour on a good day.

It was not draining. There was a strip of it a foot wide running from the ash-pit wall to the back door, the whole length of the shop, that was wet an hour ago when the rest of the yard had gone, and the wet was not a puddle, it was a damp strip with the colour of ground that has been wet all night and has not dried because there is water under it.

Tomas got the ash-pit bar out and put it down the strip, from the wall, one pace at a time.

It went in easy for two feet. It went in hard for a foot. It went in easy again.

He marked the first place with chalk and went round the whole floor with the bar and found it four more, all on the same line, and the line ran under the wall and out from under the shop in the direction of the millrace.

“That is not the Bellwrights’ cut,” he said. “The Bellwrights’ cut comes off the bench and turns south to the gate. I have laid that pipe myself and I know where it is under the floor. That is a different line and it is deeper than the Bellhouse and it is newer than the Bellhouse and it goes the other way.”

“Which way is the other way?”

“Out under the yard wall toward the millrace.”

Oren took out his book and wrote nothing. He stood with the lamp in one hand and the closed book in the other and looked at the chalk.

“Now listen to me,” he said, “and then we will decide what is going in the book and what is not, and I have already decided most of it, and I want you to hear the reason before you argue, because your argument is going to be that the town should be told.”

“The town should be told.”

“No,” Oren said. He did not say it loudly. “And here is why, and it has taken me four hours at this table and a piece of ground with chalk on it. If this office learns there is a line under the Vale Bellhouse yard that is not on any sheet and not in your father’s book, then what comes back is an order to find out what it is. Not from me. From the district. A works order, on a schedule, with a date, and a Crown man in your yard with a spade, and they will take the floor up because a floor is a floor. And then they will want to know what is on the other end of it. And the other end of it, on the evidence of that chalk, is under the millrace, which is Crown ground, which is a Crown works, and by the fourth question they will be asking who put it there and when.”

“They cannot possibly know.”

“They will not need to know,” Oren said. “They will have a line and a yard and a date. And the answer to *who put it there* in this town is one word long, and the word is Vale, and I do not care in the slightest whether it is true, and neither will the man reading the file. That is how a house gets put in a return. Not by somebody lying. By somebody asking a fair question in the right order.”

Tomas stood in the wet strip on the floor of his father’s shop with a bar in his hand and had no answer at all, which had happened to him twice since the second day and both times had cost him something.

“What do you want written,” he said.

“The day, the hour, and the finding. That is all. *The yard of the Vale Bellhouse has a damp line running under the floor from the ash-pit wall toward the millrace, in ground not recorded in the Bellwrights’ cut book and not shown on any Crown sheet held by the watch. It was found on the fifteenth day by the maker. No person has lifted it. No person has traced it. No person knows what it is.*” Oren wrote it as he said it, slowly, in the flat hand. “That is a fact and it is checkable and it is useless to anybody except us, and that is the correct way to write down the first fact of a thing nobody is ready for.”

“Who knows?”

“You. Me. Jerm, because Jerm keeps this book and I am not going to have a thing in his book that he did not know was in it. That is three, and three is the number that can carry a thing in a town of this size, and any more than three and one of them talks in a tavern, and there is nothing either of us can do about that and it is not a reason to make it four.”

“Not the Measure.”

“Not Sabin, and not Iven Sore, and not the girl.” Oren shut the book. “And the word *line* does not get said in a lane this week. Not because it is a secret. Because the moment it is a word people use, it stops being a thing we found and becomes a story we are telling, and a story gets repeated, and a repeated story has a shape by the third telling, and the shape is always the same one, which is that the Crown has put something under the ward. It has not. That is what we have actually got, and it is worth more, and it is worth nothing at all if we spend it in a lane in nine days.”

Tomas put the ash-pit bar back against the wall. He got the door threshold stone up on its edge — it had always been loose, since his father’s time, and he had shimmed it twice — and under it there was iron. Not copper. Two flat bars, a foot apart, running out under the wall, wrapped in tarred rope gone to a black stone, with pitch between the wrappings, and where the pitch had gone the iron had bloomed and there was a rust scale on the stone three inches thick.

He put the stone back and shimmed it with two wedges and a third, and shut the door.

He wrote it up in the day-book in the yard, standing, by the light of the lamp on the bench, and it is a short entry and it is the last thing in it that matters.

*Fifteenth day, ninth hour of the night. In the floor of my own yard there is a line of iron under the ground going out toward the millrace which is not the Bellwrights’ cut and is not in my father’s book and is on no Crown sheet the watch holds. I have written it in the watch book in five sentences and it is in three books in this town and I am not writing one word more about it than those five sentences. I want it written, here, in my own hand, where nobody will look for it, that the reason I am not putting it on the board is not that I am protecting the workshop and not that I am ashamed of it, and it is that Oren Pike sat on the bench in my father’s shop and argued me out of the only thing I wanted to do today, and he was right, and I want it in a book that nobody will read in case there comes a day when I am not able to remember being argued out of it.*
