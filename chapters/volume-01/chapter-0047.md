# Chapter 0047 — The Line Under the Yard

The order came with the rider who brought the district’s reply, and the order was inside the reply and had been inside it since the seventeenth, and Captain Pike worked that out in about a minute and a half and then sat down on the step of the watch house and did not get up for a while.

Then he found Tomas Vale in the lane outside the Bellhouse with a slate under his arm and a piece of chalk in his hand, and he said, without any preamble:

“I did it.”

“You did what.”

“I wrote it down.” Oren had not moved from the step. “On the fifteenth day, at about the ninth hour at night, in your father’s yard, with a lamp on the bench, I sat down and I wrote a paragraph into the watch gate book about a damp line under a bellmaker’s floor, and I read it out to Jerm before I shut the book, and I wrote it because I was proud of it. Three sentences. *Found on the fifteenth day by the maker. No person has lifted it. No person has traced it. No person knows what it is.* I said to you in that yard that three is the number that can carry a thing in a town of this size, and I was pleased with myself, and it was the best paragraph I have written this year.

“And on the seventeenth day I made a fair copy of the gate book’s entries for the fifteenth and I enclosed it with my return, because a return that refers to an enclosure has to have the enclosure, and enclosures are ordinary, and I did not read it again.”

“How many copies go to the district with a return.”

“Two. One is kept. One goes.” Oren put his cap in his hands. “Vale. The district has had my three sentences in its own hand with my name and my rank under them since the eighteenth or the nineteenth of this month, and on the twenty-fifth a Crown officer came into this ward with an authority in writing to open the floor of your yard, and the only reason that office knows there is anything under it is a page I wrote in a yard at night to show a man that I had been careful.”

Neither of them said anything for a while, and it was Jerm, coming down the lane with the book under his arm, who ended it.

“Captain. I have been in the book and I have read it and I want to say one thing and then I will stop.”

“Say it.”

“You told me on the fifteenth that I was to know what was in my own book, and you were right and I would have found it out from somebody else inside a month and I would not have been able to say anything. If you had left it out, I would never have known, and then one of us would have had to lie about it in a room, and it would have been me, because I am the one who cannot keep a face.” Jerm shifted the book. “I would rather have been in it. I want that written down somewhere and I have written it in here.” He had. It was in his own hand, with the hour, and it said that he had been told and had chosen to be told and had not been asked to choose.

---

They decided it separately, as three people who were not permitted to decide it together.

Oren Pike decided first and told nobody. He worked out at the second hour that a lawful order to ascertain what is in the ground of a yard may not be refused, may not be delayed, and may not be argued in a lane, and that the only three things available to a man in Kells about an order like it are to obey it, to be absent from it, and to be in it and write. He entered that in the watch book in his own flat hand, in a paragraph, and the paragraph says: *That the watch will attend at the Vale Bellhouse yard on the twenty-seventh for the purpose stated in the authority of the district, will admit no person into that yard except a person doing the work and a person keeping the record, will not obstruct, will not assist beyond the keeping of the record, and will not take any part in the finding.*

Jerm decided second and told nobody, and what he decided was about a book, and it took him the whole morning: he wrote out in advance, on a leaf, in his own hand, every hour he expected the work to be in progress and what he expected to see at each of them, so that when the spade went in he was not a man writing down whatever he saw afterwards, which is what a book becomes in about six months if the man holding it was not ready.

Tomas decided last, in the yard, at about the fourth hour, and it took him about four minutes and it was not a difficult decision, and he wrote it down in the day-book that night and it is the shortest of the week’s entries:

*Twenty-seventh day. I am opening the floor of my own shop in front of the ward, in daylight, with the Crown doing it and their own paper, and I am going to stand in my own yard and watch every inch of it and write the hours.*

*The reason I am not asking to be shut in, and I want the reason written and not the feeling: if the floor is opened at night with two men in it, then in about a year there will be a page in a file that says a bellmaker was not present at the opening of his own floor, and there will be no way on that page for anybody to put my name against it, and the floor will be the Crown’s account of my floor. A ward that has spent a month learning that a record is worth more than a conclusion cannot ask for the record of an event to be made in an empty yard.*

*Cost, before the act, as I asked the ward for a bar out of its own drain and was given it: the bench-books come out of their drawer in daylight, in front of about forty people and a Crown copyist, and my father’s hand is in them, and there is a name in a Crown lock register in a district building that echoes a name in a book of mine, and I have never asked anybody about it and I am not going to ask anybody about it today, and if somebody in that yard asks me about the lock register I am going to say what I have said every time, which is that a name in a book and a name in a hand are two different things and the echo is not a proof and I know it is not a proof and I would not know what to do with it if it were.*

---

They came at the fourth hour of the afternoon: a Crown officer with a black case who read the authority entire, in the lane, out loud, because he had been told to read it out loud, and Surveyor Rhea Collis, who had not been in this county since the twenty-first and who had come back for it, and who had a spade that was not a Crown issue spade and was her own.

Oren refused the district’s other question in front of her, in the lane, before the yard door was touched, and it took four minutes and it is in three books.

“Fourth line of your own return, officer, of last night. The district requires an answer in the watch’s hand whether any bell in this ward is bound to a claim, and by whom, and does not accept *none* as an answer and does not accept *none* as a refusal. Captain Pike declines. He declines in his own hand and he declines on the ground that the answer, whichever way it went, is a list of persons, and that the four bells in the market lane on the nineteenth have a maker, and that the district cannot have the maker’s name out of the watch’s book while the Crown’s own register holds four lines in that market lane with the word *none* in the name column, and that if the watch gives a name the district will have a name, and if the watch does not the district will have the watch’s refusal, and Captain Pike has been giving this district refusals in his own hand since the seventeenth and will continue to do so in his own hand until somebody in Fourmile puts on a form the word *must* and names the field, and then we will have a document to answer and not a request to refuse.”

“Entered, captain,” said the district’s copyist, who was about forty and had a cough and gave his name to nobody in that lane, including the court clerk, who entered him in the watch book only as a copyist of the district.

“Entered,” said the copyist. “And the surveyor is witness.”

“I am witness,” Collis said. “I am not an officer of the district renewal office and I will not certify a refusal of the watch, and I will say so in my own return, because the last thing this county needs is a surveyor putting her seal on the Kells watch’s conscience.”

---

The yard door came off its hinges in the sense that the Crown wax was broken and Oren Pike took his own notice off the door with his own hands and put it in his coat, and the breaking of the seal was entered in the copyist’s book with the hour by the copyist and in the watch book by Jerm, and both entries say the same thing, which is that the seal was broken at the fourth hour of the afternoon of the twenty-seventh day of this month under the authority of the district, at the request of the district, on the door of a workshop already under a Crown declaration, and that nothing was taken out of that yard on the twenty-seventh except the floor.

They took the floor up in about three hours.

The stones came out from the back door end and were laid in a line on the lane side with the sort of care of men who have been told the floor is a floor. The damp strip was exactly where it had been, from the ash-pit wall to the back door, a foot wide, and the earth under it was black to a hand’s depth and cold, and there was a smell off it that was not a drain smell and was not a cellar smell and that about forty people in the lane were careful not to discuss.

And Tomas Vale stood in his own yard in his father’s boots and watched every inch of it and wrote down the hours, and when the man with the bar went down to the damp strip and put it in, Tomas wrote down the hour, and the depth, and what colour the earth came up, and what came out of it, and he wrote it in the same words and the same order each time, and he did not do it once differently in three hours, and that is how anybody watching from the lane could tell that he had decided to do it that way before he started.

The bench-books came out at the fifth hour, because the books are on a shelf under the bench and the shelf is in the corner the floor came up in.

Nobody asked for them. They came out because the floor came up and the bench had to be moved and the man moving it put them on the wall in daylight in front of about forty people and a Crown copyist who wrote *three quarto volumes and one ledger, the property of the workshop, taken from a shelf in the yard at the fifth hour* in his own hand, and the copyist did not ask to look inside them, and that is on the page, because Tomas wrote it down afterwards and it matters.

---

The finding was entered in three books at about the seventh hour, in daylight, with the ward standing in the lane, and it is the same finding in all three and it is in the words of the copyist, who read it out before he wrote it and let the ward hear it.

*That the workshop of the Vale Bellhouse is on ground containing a line of iron bars, two flat bars at a distance of about a foot, wrapped in tarred rope and pitch, running beneath the floor of the yard from beneath the ash-pit wall towards the boundary wall; that the line is not recorded in the cut book of the workshop and is not the work of the workshop; that it is not shown on any sheet held by the Crown road office of this district; that no person in this assembly has lifted it, and two persons have now lifted it; and that nothing further concerning it is known to any person present.*

“Two persons,” Tomas said. “I heard that.”

“Two persons,” the copyist said, and struck it out, and wrote *one person, and the surveyor*, and read it back.

Then the direction.

The surveyor’s own rod came up from the bottom of the trench and it was put on the surface and sighted, and the two men dug the last two feet along the line, and at about the eighth hour of the evening the floor of the yard had a trench in it four feet deep and nine feet long and the line was lying in the bottom of it in the open, wrapped in rope gone to a black stone, with a bloom on the iron, and it was visible to about forty people standing at a gate.

And it did not go out through the wall toward the millrace.

It went the other way. Under the floor, at a foot from the back door end, it turned, and it came back across the yard at a right angle, and it went out under the front of the shop, under the threshold, and up under the floor of the front room, and it left that floor at the wall that faces the hill and the north road.

Tomas knew which way that wall faced. He has worked in that room for nineteen years. He has had his mother’s table in that room. He knows the alignment of that wall with the north gate from the inside of a building every working day of his life, and he had known the alignment of that yard and that gate since he was a boy setting out a mould on the floor with his father and a chalk line and a corner square.

Nobody said it.

Jerm did not write it. That is on the page and it is a thing that Jerm has thought about every day since, and it is in his own book in his own hand with the hour: *That at the seventh hour of the evening of the twenty-seventh the maker looked at the trench and at the wall and did not say a word, and that I wrote the finding in the words of the district copyist and did not write that word or the direction in those words, because it was not in the copyist’s mouth and it is not mine to put in, and I have been keeping this book for nine days and I know exactly what a page with a man’s inference on it looks like in a year, and I would rather the wall and the gate be found by somebody with a rod.*

Oren Pike stood at the back with his cap in his hands and did not say it, and afterwards told Tomas, in a voice with nothing in it at all, that the reason was that a watch captain who puts a direction in a district finding is a watch captain who has decided that the Crown’s instrument is a man.

Tomas himself said nothing about it, then or afterwards, and the wall and the gate are in two books in Kells and in one in Fourmile, with the bearing, because a surveyor’s finding is a finding about bearing, and the copyist took a bearing, and read it out, and the copyist read it out in a lane in front of forty people, and forty people heard a number, and about nine of them knew what the number was, and the other thirty-one did not, and none of the nine said a word in a lane that week or the next.

Then the floor was put back. The stones went down in about an hour and the yard drained in about a quarter of an hour, and the damp strip was still there at the back, a foot wide, and would be there for ever, and Tomas Vale put a bar of the Bellwrights’ own ash across the top of it so that a person standing on it can feel that it is not solid, because his father’s bar was a thing you laid over a bad floor in a workshop and there is no other word for it in this trade.

---

And at about the ninth hour of the evening, with the floor back and forty people still at the gate, Deputy Reeve Crake arrived with a paper in his hand and asked for the watch, and he did not put a black case on the ground and he did not begin with the Crown.

“Captain. I have an application. I am applying in writing, in my own name, under the commission, for the millrace bridge bell.”

Oren took the paper.

“It is in the right form,” Crake said, “and it is the fourth time I have written it and I have shown it to nobody, and I have had it in my coat since the twentieth day, and I am showing it to you tonight because on the twenty-eighth at first light I am required to have this ward at a gate, and the Crown has no instrument in it that anybody here is obliged to answer, and that is not a reason to take a bell, it is a reason to want one, and the wanting is mine and the order is mine and I have brought you both in one sheet with the hours on it.”

“What does the sheet say.”

“It says: the millrace bridge bell of the Vale Bellhouse is a Crown instrument by the commission’s determination of the tenth day and is under a watch condition of the same date. I apply to the watch captain of Kells for that condition to be amended, in writing, for the morning of the twenty-eighth only, to the following effect. The bell to be carried at the seventh hour of this night from the north wall of the Bellhouse yard to the north gate of this town and there set on the gate bracket; to be sounded at the north gate at the sixth hour of the twenty-eighth by a keeper to be named by the watch, and the keeper’s name to be entered; and to be returned to the Bellhouse yard on the afternoon of the twenty-eighth, and the condition re-made by the watch’s own hand at the hour it is replaced. No other bell in this ward is asked for. No keeper is asked for. No bell is to be rung in the market lane or on the towpath. The application is made at the request of the district and against my own inclination as to method.”

Oren read it twice. Jerm read it over his shoulder.

“That is the narrowest application I have ever seen in this county,” Oren said.

“It is a well-made paper,” Crake said. “I have been told by a master of the Measure last week that there is a shortage of those and that they go into files and change nothing, and I have been taking it personally.”

“Captain,” said Collis, from the wall, “I am going to witness this and I am going to make one objection as a surveyor and it is a real one. A bell sounded from a gate bracket at the sixth hour is a signal, and a signal wants a duty and a limit and a place where it stops, and I have read four hundred signals in eleven years and I have never read one that did not have all three. You are about to make the fourth that does not, and I would like the record to say I said so before it happened and not after.”

“Then make the record say it,” Oren said.

He granted it in writing, in the lane, in front of about thirty people, in the flat hand, with the hour, and he wrote six lines, and the first three were the grant and the fourth was the keeper and the fifth was the limit and the sixth was his own reason and it is the paragraph he would be asked about in a room in a year.

*Fourth: that the keeper be Watchman Jerm, who is to be named in the grant and who has asked to be named and who is to sound the bell at the sixth hour of the twenty-eighth and at no other hour, and who may take himself off it at any time without reason and without consequence, and whose refusal is entered as a refusal and never as a silence.*

*Fifth: that the bell is to be sounded at the north gate only, and that it is not to be sounded in the market lane or on the towpath or at the low gate or in any yard in this ward, and that the whole of the signal is one hour and one gate and it stops there because the wall is there and not because anybody has decided that it should.*

*Sixth, and I enter it because I am the man who grants it and I have granted it: that I grant this because on the morning of the twenty-eighth this watch is two men and a gate and is required to attend at this ward at the ninth hour in a market court, and a Crown officer is coming to a board at the ninth hour with a list of what he is to bring, and if the Crown has a lawful instrument in this town then the Crown will use it, and it will use it better than I would have used it, and there is no version of the twenty-eighth on which the Crown does not use it if it has one, and I would rather it had one in my hand and on a paper with a limit on it than have it send a woman with a slate.*

Nobody said anything about that for about a minute.

Jerm said: “I will sound it. I want it entered that I asked to be named and I am not being brave and I have a family and I have read a gate book every day for nine years and I would like somebody to write down the reason I asked, which is that a watch that says the Crown has no instrument is a watch that has handed the Crown a reason to invent one, and I am not going to be the reason.”

“Entered,” said Jerm, in his own hand, in the gate book, which is the same book, and which was now also his book for the night.

---

The district’s copyist rode out of Kells at about the eleventh hour with a book of entries and a yard’s floor in it and a bearing on the wall, and he had written *two persons* and struck it and written *one person and the surveyor*, and he had not looked inside a single one of the workshop’s books, and he was about forty and he had a bad cough and he said, at the gate, to nobody in particular, that he had been a copyist for nineteen years and had never in his life put a bearing in a finding.

He said it to Tomas. And Tomas wrote it down that night in four words, which were: *He said it to me. He wanted it said.*

*Cost of the twenty-seventh day, in figures and in hours, because the figures are for them. One Crown wax broken by the Crown at the Crown’s own request and entered in two books. One floor taken up at the fourth hour and laid again at about the ninth. One trench nine feet by four, filled in. One bearing taken, read out in a lane, and now in a file in Fourmile with about nine people in this ward knowing what it means and thirty-one not knowing. Three quarto volumes and a ledger of a working shop’s own record on a table in daylight in front of forty people and a Crown copyist who did not open them. And a millrace bridge bell taken off the north wall of a workshop under a Crown declaration at about the seventh hour of the evening, under a Crown seal broken the same afternoon, and set on a bracket at the north gate of this town, to be sounded at the sixth hour of the morning, by a keeper who asked to be named, for a Crown officer who wrote the application himself and did not want to.*

*And I am going to write the last line of this and I am going to write it in the open, in the place where anybody in this town can read it if they have spent nine days learning to look: the line goes toward the north gate, and I have known the alignment of that wall since I was a boy with a chalk line and a corner square, and nobody in this ward said it out loud last night, and Oren did not write it, and Jerm did not write it, and I did not say it, and the copyist took a bearing and read it out, and it is in a book in Fourmile, and it is on the wall of my own shop.*

*And I want it entered that the reason none of us said it is not that we are frightened of the Crown. It is that the four people who know it are all of us people who have spent a month arguing that a thing a Crown man writes about a Crown instrument is not the same as a fact about a bell, and if any one of us had put the direction in a Crown’s finding we would have been doing the thing we have spent a month telling this ward is not done. So the finding says what a spade can see and the bearing says what a rod can see, and neither of them says that a line under a bellmaker’s floor is a road.*

*And the Crown will read both books tomorrow morning, and a Crown man in Fourmile will put the two of them together, because that is what a district is, and he will not be a villain and he will not be wrong, and there will be a spade in this yard before the day is out.*
