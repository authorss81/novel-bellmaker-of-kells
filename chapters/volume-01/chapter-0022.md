# Chapter 0022 — What the Dawn Inspection Found

Oren had been a watch captain for eleven years and he had signed a great many things he did not believe, and he had a private rule about it: you sign, and then you go and find out what it is going to cost, and you pay it, and you do not go back and take the signature back. He had never before signed anything that was going to cost a child, and he got to the north gate at four with a whole hour in front of him to work out how a man does that, and he did not work it out.

Rhea Collis came down the last of the north road in the dark with frost in the seam of her case and a lamp she did not need because the sky was already thinking about it.

“Captain Pike,” she said. “I am at your gate, on time, which surprises nobody in my district.”

“Surveyor.”

“I have a commission to certify a renewal visit on route L-4 at the rescheduled dawn, at the hour of the original posting.” She held out the paper. “Read it before your officers touch anything, because I have read mine twice and I would rather you knew what it says than be told later that you had.”

Oren read it. It was four lines and it granted her exactly one power: to be present, to witness, and to certify that a visit was made and completed against the ward roll of route L-4. It said nothing about consent. It said nothing about custody. It said nothing about children, and he was grateful for that in a way that told him the other document existed somewhere and he would be handed it at the worst possible moment.

“You are not Pever,” he said.

“I have said it twice in three days and I will say it again now because it matters. I did not certify the carrier. I have never held the L-4 entry. I am a surveyor from the northern district, I was delayed by a ferry I could not pay for, and I am here at the hour with a commission that is one page long.”

“What will you do?”

“Witness. Certify. And record.” She looked at the gate, and at the two officers already standing there with their coats buttoned, and at the filing clerk with the black case. “And stop the first of those men from telling me what I may see.”

“Deputy Crake’s instruction is that you see the ward roll and nothing else.”

“Then Deputy Crake may certify his own visit, because I will not put my seal to a visit conducted against a roll I have not been shown, and I will say so in the open at the first door rather than in a report you will read in a week.”

The filing clerk spoke for the first time, from under the arch.

“The roll of route L-4 for this round is the response schedule. Forty-one households, entered in office, one mark each. That is the roll. The commission is not obliged to produce a second instrument because a surveyor would prefer one.”

“Then produce the per-household authority,” Collis said, “and I will work off it and be finished by noon. Produce it or produce nothing, and I will work off the schedule, and I will certify that I was shown a schedule and not a roll of signed households.”

Nobody said anything for a moment. The sky over the ridge had gone the colour of a knife.

Oren said, “Captain to the watch. Enter this: no per-household authority has been produced. The survey proceeds against the ward response schedule, which is Crown paper, and the surveyor has stated in the open that she has not seen a roll of households. Enter it before the round opens, and read it back.”

Jerm’s pen stopped. “Captain, I have one thing before we go into the ward.”

“Say it.”

“I am not writing names on the door slates they intend to leave,” Jerm said. “I know what it is to have a Crown slate with your house number and a mark on it on your own door. I will write the house numbers. I will not write the marks.”

“Write the house numbers and the time and the officer’s name, and nothing else, and I will put my mark under yours so that it is the watch’s record and not yours.” Oren looked at the two officers. “That is not a refusal. It is a thing a watch captain may do with a pencil. If the commission wants the marks in the ward’s own hand, Deputy, they may come and ask the ward for a pencil.”

Nobody argued with that, and the two Crown officers looked at each other, and Oren understood that they would be able to write the marks themselves and had already decided they would.

---

The round opened at four.

They went down through the Low Ward in a line of six — Collis with her rod, the two officers, the filing clerk, Jerm, and a case-man Oren had not seen before — and Oren went with them, at the end, in a coat he had not changed in two days, because a watch captain who is not present at his own ward’s round is a watch captain who has delegated his conscience to a piece of paper.

The low lane was drying mud and cart track and the smell of the seventh day had not entirely gone out of it. At the foot of the ward, where four houses had taken water to a table’s height a little under three days before, there were four men with pails and a woman washing a step, and none of them stopped working, and Oren was glad of that and ashamed of being glad of it, because it meant they had got used to him.

Line four went fast. An old man answered the door, looked at the slate the officer held up, and said, “I have answered since I was forty,” and the officer read the mark aloud and compared it with the schedule and found the number the same, and the man was passed, and a slate was nailed to his door with his house number and the time and nothing else.

Line seven took longer. A woman wanted to know what the mark was and was told that her household was entered as having returned five persons, and that the entry was dated the day the round was posted, and that she had not been asked. She said she had not. The officer wrote *household disputes entry* in his own book, which was the most honest thing anybody wrote all morning, and moved on.

At line twelve the round stopped.

Absalom Teage was a carter with a bad hand and a good voice. He came out of a door with a slate under his arm, and he got there before the officer had the schedule open.

“114,” he said. “That is me. And I will save you the walk. Three of mine came back to the board on the posting day and I have the slate they wrote it on, and it is in the house, and you may send a man for it or you may come and take it off the nail yourself.”

“Step aside, please.”

“Read it to me,” Teage said. “Read me what it says about my house. There is no one in this ward who has read me what it says about my house, and you have come to my door at four in the morning, and you can start by being the first.”

The filing clerk read it. He read it as though he were reading somebody else’s line, which is how a professional reads a line that is not his, and he read: *four entered, three returned, one not returned, entered in office the day the round was posted.*

“Three returned,” Teage said. “I will tell you what that means. It means I have three, and my wife has none, because she was at her sister’s, and the man they have wrote *not returned* is my mother, who is sixty-eight and has not been out of that room since Candlemas. And you have come for the not-returned.”

The officer said, “Are the children of the household registered on the ward roll?”

“Two of them are not and two of them are, and I have the book and I will fetch it, and I will fetch it now, and you may read it in the lane.”

Nobody stopped him. He went in and came back with the roll book and the slate and put both in the officer’s hands, and the officer read the roll book, and the roll book said two of four, and the schedule said three returned, and there was a number in one of them and not in the other, and the officer had to have it both ways.

Then the case-man took the boy.

He was nine. He had his shoes on, which Oren thought about afterward more than he thought about anything else, because whoever had sent for him had not given anybody time to take his shoes off, and the shoes meant somebody had thought about his feet and nothing else.

Mrs Teage came out and did not scream. She said one sentence, four words long, and Oren wrote it down because it was the sentence that would be asked for later, and it was: *He is not on your paper.*

That is what she said. Not *give him back*, not *where are you taking him*. *He is not on your paper.* And the astonishing thing, the thing Oren turned over for the rest of the day and the rest of the week, was that she was right, and that being right made it worse, because the officer was not asking whether the boy was on the Crown’s paper. The officer was asking whether he was on the ward’s, and the answer was no, and the answer being no was the entire machinery.

“He is not on your paper,” Oren said, out loud, to nobody, and Jerm wrote it down in the watch book because that is what Jerm was for.

“Captain,” Collis said, from the lane, in a voice with nothing in it at all, “I will require that logged as an event and not as a complaint.”

“It is logged,” Oren said.

“I will require more than logged,” she said. “I will require that the log say that a child was removed from a household whose entered response records a return. In those words, or words like them, and I will countersign that line and not the rest of the book.”

Oren looked at her, in the grey light, in the mud, at a woman eleven days off a road with a case and a rod and a seal she had not used and was now going to use.

“Write it,” he said. “Write that, and countersign it, and I will put my mark beside yours and the Crown filing clerk may initial it or not, and if he does not I will enter that he was asked.”

At line nineteen there was a woman standing in her own doorway with her arms folded who had been in the court on the eighth day and had agreed to carry a warning and had withdrawn in open court and whose name was in no book in the town.

“Dunn,” the officer said.

“Four entered, two accompanying,” said the filing clerk.

Sena Dunn said, “Two accompanying.”

“Two accompanying persons are recorded against your household, unnamed, and they are to be taken up with the entry.”

“Taken where?”

“With the entry.”

“Then I am going to ask you the question four times,” Dunn said, “and I would like it written that I asked it four times, and the question is: which child.”

Nobody wrote it. The officer took a step forward and the case-man came up on the other side, and the boy who was taken was eleven and had a scarf on that was not knotted properly, and Oren did not write the sentence about the scarf either, because there are limits beyond which a record becomes a thing the record will not survive.

At line twenty-two the officers read *six entered, two accompanying* off the schedule and told Mrs Fenn that her household was entered as six persons and that two unnamed persons were recorded as accompanying her entry, and Mrs Fenn said *there are two of us* and then said, immediately, in a completely different voice, *I can do that, I can take them there, I know where they live, I will fetch them.*

Oren stopped that himself.

“No,” he said. “You will not fetch anybody.”

“I could find them. I know every door in this ward. I would only be walking.”

“You will not be walking anywhere for the Crown this morning, and I will put in the book that I said so and that you offered and that I refused you, and both of those are facts about me and about you and neither of them is a compliment.” He looked at Nell, who was standing four feet off the step and had gone the colour of the door. “Miss Fenn, take your mother indoors.”

Nell did not take her indoors. She stood on the step and watched a Crown slate get nailed to her door with her house number and the hour and nothing else, and the six was not on the slate, and she looked at the slate for a long time as though the number might change.

At line twenty-eight an old rope-maker came out of a door with a Crown iron in his hand and gray in his beard and asked, before anybody said anything, whether the officer could tell him whether the copper under his house was put in with the ward’s consent.

“Step aside, please.”

“That is not a step-aside question,” Gib Hask said. “That is a yes or no, and you are Crown, and I have asked the deputy and nobody in this town will answer me, and there are forty people on this lane and I am asking you in front of them, and I would like you to say *Crown* twice so that a man can write it down.”

The officer, who was twenty-six and had been in the district nine months, said, “The line under this ward is Crown-maintained, it was laid before this commission, and it exists to carry warnings that have kept this town alive. The Crown’s position is that the line serves the ward’s safety, and that its removal would be a matter for the ward to take up with the commission in writing.”

It was a good answer. It was, as far as Oren could tell, substantially true, and it had been given by a young man standing in a lane at four in the morning, and it had now been said out loud on a public street in front of the ward.

Hask stood there with his iron in his hand and did not move for a long moment.

“Right,” he said at last. “Then it is said. It is said in the lane and not in a room, and I wanted it in a room and I have got the other thing, and I understand the trade I have made.”

Oren wrote down that the argument was made, that it was made in the open, and that the Crown officer answered it in the open, and then he wrote the four words he had wanted to write and did not: *and the argument is now public and is therefore of no use to the man who made it.*

He did not write those four words then, and he has not written them since, and they are not in the book, and there is a version of this afternoon in which a watch captain protects a man who has publicly accused his office, and he could have had that version, and it cost him more than the three children did to let it go.

Three children went. Oren wrote that number at the head of the page and underlined it, and the town found a great many larger ones in the following days.

Twenty-two doors were marked. Oren had sent them ahead in threes with a watchman, and the surveyor had gone to nine of the twenty-two and had watched the other thirteen get a slate and a nail and no conversation at all, and she had said so once, in the lane, in a flat voice, and then had not said it again.

He wrote the line numbers and the house numbers and the hour for each of the twenty-two, and against line twenty-two, the Fenn house, he wrote *six entered against a household of two*, and he wrote it in his own hand and read it back before he signed it.

He wrote the removals separately. That was the whole of his work in the second hour and he did it to the exclusion of everything else, standing at the back of a handcart with the book on a barrel, and each one has its own heading and its own line and its own hour.

*LINE 12. HOUSE 114. MARK: THREE RETURNED, ONE NOT RETURNED, ENTERED IN OFFICE THE DAY THE ROUND WAS POSTED. THE HOUSEHOLD’S OWN ANSWER, GIVEN IN THE LANE BY ABSALOM TEAGE, CARTER: THAT HE ANSWERED WITH HIS OWN MOUTH AT THE BOARD ON THE POSTING DAY AND HOLDS THE SLATE. THE HOUSEHOLD’S OWN ANSWER, GIVEN AT THE DOOR BY HIS WIFE: HE IS NOT ON YOUR PAPER. A CHILD AGED NINE TAKEN INTO CROWN CUSTODY. NOT REGISTERED ON THE WARD ROLL. TIME, FOURTH HOUR AND FORTY MINUTES.*

*LINE 19. HOUSE 121. MARK: FOUR RETURNED, TWO ACCOMPANYING, UNNAMED ON THE PAGE. THE HOUSEHOLD’S OWN ANSWER, GIVEN IN THE DOORWAY BY SENA DUNN: WHICH CHILD, ASKED FOUR TIMES. A CHILD AGED ELEVEN TAKEN INTO CROWN CUSTODY. TIME, FIFTH HOUR AND TEN MINUTES.*

*LINE 34. HOUSE 133. MARK: TWO RETURNED, ONE ACCOMPANYING, ENTERED IN OFFICE. THE HOUSEHOLD GAVE NO ANSWER AND NO NAME. A CHILD AGED THIRTEEN TAKEN INTO CROWN CUSTODY. TIME, FIFTH HOUR AND FIFTY MINUTES.*

Collis countersigned the first of those three and would not countersign the other two, and Oren asked her why, and she said, in a low voice, walking beside him, “Because I can certify that a child was removed and I cannot certify the age, and if I put a seal on a child’s age in an office in the district town then a person with a ruler in that office has a number for a child in a file, and I have spent eleven days on a road to get here and I am not spending them building that.”

“Then that goes in the book as your reason, with your name,” Oren said, “because I would like a surveyor’s reason in a watch book more than I would like her seal.”

“Do not put my name on it.”

“I have to put somebody’s name on it. It is either you or the officer, and it is not going to be the officer.”

“Then put mine,” Collis said, “and do not thank me, because a seal of mine is worth more to you this morning than a child is worth to me, and I have had eleven days to think about how much that is worth, and the answer is that I do not know.”

---

They came back up the lane at six, and the sun came up over the ridge behind them, and the Crown wagon was already on the north road with its tailboard up, and the ward stood about in the lane and watched the whole thing go, and did not make a sound that Oren could hear, and Oren understood that it was not because they were contented.

He could have stopped it.

That was the sentence, and he wrote it at the foot of the page in his own hand because he was the only man in Kells who was both able and unwilling, and he had better have it down in black and white before somebody said it for him in a worse tone.

*I had two men and a warrant that would have carried, and seventy-odd people in the lane behind me, and I did not put them between those children and that order. I did not do it for the law’s sake. I did it because a captain who puts his men in front of children in a lane at five in the morning is a captain whose men will not stand in a lane at all after that, and then there is no Crown line in this ward and no ward either. That is the arithmetic. I made it. I would make it again and I would like it written that I made it.*

Ferrow read it back and did not soften it, which was the reason Oren had spent nine days building a relationship with Ferrow rather than with the ward.

And then Surveyor Collis, on the step of the watch house, with the sun on her face and her case in her hand, said:

“Captain Pike, I would like to see the schedule.”

“There is a copy in your file.”

“There is a copy of a *return* in my file,” Collis said. “That is a clerk’s summary of what an office says was entered. I have certified a visit this morning that was conducted against a page, and I certified it because if I had refused, another man would have certified it and refused to read it aloud. So I want to see the page.”

“It is Crown property, and the only copy of it in this town is the one my watch holds. The joint receipt I signed on the second day covers the carrier, not the file, and it expired at the fourth hour this morning, and since it expired there is nothing at all in writing that says the watch may hold this.”

“Then you are holding Crown paper with no paper of your own about it,” Collis said, “which is a worse position than holding it lawfully, and you have put yourself in it deliberately, and I would like you to know that I noticed. I am asking you to do something that is either a breach or a duty, and I have not decided which, and I have not got time to decide it, because the wagon is on the north road and there is one of those children on it who is not on the Crown’s paper either, and the only way anyone will ever know what was entered on that page is if somebody who is not afraid of the man who entered it says the words out loud.”

Oren stood on his own step with the book shut under his arm.

Eleven days, a hundred and forty people at a hearing in the dark, a lawful taking done in the open so that two men would not have to choose in a lane, a surveyor’s seal on a line about a boy in shoes, and now a woman asking him to hand her a Crown page that belongs to her own commission.
