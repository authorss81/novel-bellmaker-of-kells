# Chapter 0038 — Further Than the Rule

The water came on the nineteenth day at about the sixth hour of the evening, which was a day early, and Tomas Vale wrote the hour in the day-book before he did anything else at all, because a man who is going to be wrong in public should at least have the wrongness in ink.

It came up out of the low turn and it came up the ward’s own channel and it stood above the flat stones by the time the light went, which is the exact thing he had said would happen on the twentieth day between the second hour and the fifth hour, and which had happened on the nineteenth at the sixth hour, and he had put a date and an hour in the middle of a sentence about a drain and both of them were wrong.

Hollis Renn came up out of the culvert with his coat to the knee and water to the thigh and said the only thing that mattered.

“It will not come up.”

“How do you know that at the sixth hour?”

“Because I have been pulling at it since the fourth hour and it has not moved, and because the sill is the sill and there is lime and gravel packed behind it, and because I have written that on my board in my own words for nine days and the water has read it.” Renn put his hand flat on the gate. “And because the man from the Crown office signed for a cart across the low-turn culvert on the second day of this month and it is still there, and the culvert and this drain are one water and have been since before I was born, and if you want to know where your water came from, Vale, it came from under a cart.”

Tomas had known that. That was the whole of it and it took him about four seconds to admit it, standing in six inches of water in a lane with eleven children somewhere above him.

He had known about the cart. It was in the record, in Bevin Sarr’s eleven witnesses, and it had been the second thing in this whole business that anybody had put a name on. He had known it, and he had written a claim about a bridged drain and a slipping pawl, because those were two things he could stand in the water and prove, and he had left the cart out of a sentence about where water comes from because a cart two streets away is a Crown work and a Crown work in a warning is a sentence that gets a Crown office to the ward’s attention, and he had decided, on the nineteenth of the month, on a bench, alone, that the Crown could not be trusted with the reason.

So the sentence was true and the reason in it was wrong, and he had a day and a half of people to say so to.

---

He rang at the seventh hour, from the middle of the yard, out of the wind, once, with the leather, and he said the words and not the reason, because the reason was two sentences long and he had found out in nineteen days that a bell is a mouth and mouths are not for two sentences.

Renn sounded once at the low gate, at the seventh hour and some minutes, and Cray sounded once at the ward board, and there were two answers and both of them were in the two hundred yards they had agreed to, and it was correct, and it was a correct and completely insufficient thing, and the ward did what a ward does with a correct and insufficient thing, which is to believe it a bit more and act a bit more than the words justify.

The children were stopped on the lane. That was the first thing the bell bought. Eleven of them, coming down early in the rain the way they do when the sky is like that, and they stopped at the top of the lane and turned round and went up, and Tomas did not know that and did not find out for two days.

Hask’s four men at the low end of the turn were on a dry step and stayed on it, which they would not otherwise have done, because Hask had been in that turn every evening for four days waiting for an argument.

A carter coming up the ferry road with a load of wool and salt stopped at the low turn because the ford was under, and turned round and went back up and lost a day.

And that was all of it. Two keepers, two hundred yards, a lane and a turning. It bought four things and it did not buy the market and it did not buy the ferry road and it did not buy the twenty or thirty houses on the mill end of the west road, where a bar and a chain were still across it in the dark with a man and a lamp.

And at the eighth hour Siss Larke stopped him in the lane at the top of the ward with a rope round a box and her boy of fourteen behind her carrying the other end of the rope.

“Vale. I have been packing since the second hour. I have got a box and I have got a boy and I have a sister at Fourmile, and I can send one of them on the twentieth with the cart or I can send both and I cannot send both, and the woman at the washhouse says if the boy goes he does not come back, and my aunt at Fourmile says if the boy stays somebody comes for him, and I have been going round this ward for two days asking people what they think is going to happen and everybody has told me the same thing, which is that they do not know, and they are right, and it does me no good at all.”

“Siss. I cannot tell you.”

“I know you cannot. I have come to you last, so you are the fourth person who cannot tell me and you are the only one of the four who writes things down.” She put the box down. “But you made the bell. I heard it, down at the turning. And I want to know one thing and I am not asking you what to do. If the water is coming tonight in this ward, and if a Crown wagon comes up the north road tomorrow or the day after, is there a chance that it comes up a road that is shut?”

“Yes,” Tomas said. “There is a bar and a chain across the west road at the mill narrows and a man with a lamp, and if somebody in a Crown carriage does not like it, he can go over the bank, and I have written that down and the watch has it and three keepers said it, and it is a real bar on a real road and it is there tonight.”

“Then what do I do with the box,” Siss Larke said, “because if the box is worth having it is worth having, and the boy is worth having, and I have one cart on the twentieth and it is not a big one, and I have been standing in this lane for ten minutes asking a bellmaker who has been standing in this lane for nineteen days, and I am not blaming you, I am just telling you where I am standing.”

And Tomas Vale stood in the rain in a lane with a woman and a box and had nothing whatever to offer her, and he told himself there was nobody in Kells he could go and ask, and he had told himself that on the fifteenth as well and it had not been true then either.

Because a paper takes a day. A bell takes a moment. He had watched a paper work for nine days — he had watched Absalom Teage get two sentences written in a Crown clerk’s margin with three ordinary questions and a slate — and he had watched it take a whole morning, in daylight, in a building, with a witness, and it had been magnificent, and it could not do this. It could not do this by tonight.

And he had two keepers and two hundred yards, and Cray’s two doors were on this side of the ward, and there was no keeper on the mill end, and no keeper on the ferry road, and no keeper in the market lane, and the mill end was full of wool carts because it was market day tomorrow, and about two hundred people would be standing in that market lane at the seventh hour tomorrow with stock on a barrow.

He went back up the hill and got the bell off the frame.

---

He did not ask anybody. That is the sentence that has to be written first and in the plainest words available, and Tomas wrote it first, that night, in the day-book, and then never took it out and never softened it and never gave anybody a version of it that put anybody else in the room with him.

*On the nineteenth day, at about the eighth hour of the evening, I took the bell off its frame in my own yard and carried it up the lane into the market lane, and I sounded it four times, and I did not ask. I did not ask the two keepers who had taken a duty. I did not ask the ward. I did not ask the woman whose box it was. I did not ask the man whose market it was, and I had been told his market was the twentieth, and I went into it early on purpose.*

*Nineteen days of this month I have said, in a court and on a step and in a lane, that the whole of the craft is asking before the act, and I have written it down twice, and I heard Sabin Fell say it to me on a stool in the corner of his shop at first light this morning, and on the nineteenth day at the eighth hour of the evening I did the one thing I have spent this month learning not to do, and I did it in a rain, and I did it because a woman with a box stood in front of me and I could not bear it.*

He rang it in the market lane at twenty past the eighth hour, in the dark, in the rain, on a stone in the middle of the lane where the market strikes, and he rang it four times, and he said the words each time.

*There will be water above the flat stones at the foot of the low lane.*

That is all he sent. Not the hour — the hour was wrong and he knew it was wrong and he left it out. Not the reason — the reason was two sentences. Not *I do not know when it will come*, because it had come. Not *nobody knows why*, because he knew why and had it wrong, and the only honest version of the reason was long. Not *nothing in this is an order*, because by the fourth strike the market lane was treating it as one, and by then the qualification would have been a lie about how much control he had.

He sent the true easy part. Four times. And Sabin Fell had told him at the fourth hour in the morning that a thing that comes out of a bell a long way off arrives thinner and rounder and more certain than it went in, and that if you send only the true easy part then what you have sent is a version and not a claim.

He had sent a version, and it was a true one, and it was four times.

---

What happened in the market lane of Kells between twenty past the eighth hour and half past it, in the rain, is in the watch gate book, in Jerm’s hand, in five lines, and it is the only document in this whole business that nobody has ever been able to improve on.

*The market lane cleared. Estimated one hundred and eighty persons in the lane at twenty-five minutes past the eighth hour, moving north and west. A carrier named Rutledge lost a handcart and load; the load was wool and salt for the winter and a cask; loss stated by the carrier as thirty-six pounds and not disputed. Watchman Cask at the lane mouth. Watchman Ardle at the north gate, where about seventy came in an hour from the market lane alone.*

And: *A Crown road hand, Perry, aged nineteen, of the gang of the fifteenth, was struck at about half past the eighth hour by the shoe of a handcart in the surge and her right wrist was broken. She had been standing at the corner of the market lane by the fish market with a book and a lamp, and the book was in her hand, and the book is wet and the entries for that hour in it are illegible.*

---

He stopped it himself.

He walked back into the market lane at about the fifth hour of the morning of the twentieth and the lane was not empty, because Kells is a market town and a market town does not stay emptied — it just sends everybody out into other lanes, so there were people in the fish market arcade and against the toll shed wall and forty of them asleep on the fish market floor with their coats over them. And he got up on the fish market step, in daylight, in front of about sixty people, and said:

“That was me. I sounded a bell in this lane at twenty past eight last night, four times. I made the bell. Nobody sent it and nobody asked me to and nobody in this ward knew I was going to.

“I have no right to have sounded it. I have said in this town for nine days that the man who decides things in a room is the reason three children went out of a lane, and I was in a lane last night with a bell in my hand and no keeper on either end of it, and I did not ask one person before I sounded it, and there is a girl of nineteen in this town with a broken wrist who was standing in this lane with a book because I wrote to her on the fifteenth day and asked her to be there and write down where my words reached, and she is nineteen and she is not yours, she is the Crown’s, and she was standing where I sent her.

“I am not going to stand here and say I was right. I was not right. And I am not going to call last night a false alarm, because there was water in the low lane and it was going to be and it came, and a carrier lost a cart and thirty-six pounds and a girl lost a wrist, and every word of that is my doing, and the true part is true and the damage is mine, and those two facts are not allowed to be in the same story in a way that lets anybody off.”

Nobody cheered. A man at the back said *whose fault*, and Tomas said *mine*, and then stood there for about a quarter of an hour while about sixty people asked him questions, and he answered every one of them in the same voice and gave no explanation that was not a fact and offered no comfort and made no promise.

Then he went and told the two keepers, before the ward, in that order, and it took an hour, and Cray sat down on the step of the ward board and said, “You are going to say their names.”

“Crane’s and Tamsin Rook’s,” Tomas said.

“Yes. And you are going to say it in the book and not in the lane, and you are going to say that if either of them had said yes there would have been a third answer at the mill end, and that with three answers the bell would have had a keeper on the far side of the market lane, and that the reason there was nobody there is that I asked the two people in this town who were most likely to refuse me, and they refused, and I went and rang it anyway.”

“Yes.”

“Write it in those words. Do not write *had they agreed*. Write *if either had taken a duty, there would have been a keeper at the mill end of the west road on the evening of the nineteenth day*, and then write my sentence after it, which is that they refused and I rang it, and that they told me so in writing and I have their writing and I have shown it to neither of them again since, which is also mine.”

He wrote it at the board at the seventh hour in four hands, his own and Cray’s and Jerm’s and, at the bottom, in her own writing, Tamsin Rook’s refusal of the nineteenth day, which he copied out entire and initialled as a copy, having asked her, that morning, whether he might.

And then he wrote a line under it that he has not taken out since, and it is the only line in that board’s book that is not a fact:

*Which means that my record of this whole business is that I have no warning for anybody out there, and I have not made one, and there is not a bell in Kells that says anything about a wagon, and nobody is going to write a sentence about that. And I am not going to be able to say it for long, and I know exactly why I cannot, and the reason is three lines long and it is the order I asked the four people in, and her name is at the top of it, and I had put her at the bottom two days before. She was owed the answer and not the opportunity. I have written that down because I have no way to put it right and I am not going to be allowed to forget it, and because the day I stop writing that kind of thing down is the day I start deciding things in lanes again.*

He cost it in the day-book in figures, and the figures are the ones from the gate book:

*What it cost on the nineteenth day at the market lane, the whole of it, and I am writing it at the seventh hour of the twentieth and I have not slept:*

*One carrier’s handcart and load, stated at thirty-six pounds and not disputed. One wrist, broken, in a girl of nineteen who is not of this ward and was standing where I asked her to stand. The market day’s takings of four stalls, given up. About one hundred and eighty persons put out of a lane in the rain in the dark in nine minutes, and about seventy of them through the north gate in an hour after that. The ward’s own practice damaged in a way I cannot measure, because a bell that was sounded four times in a lane in the dark is now a thing the ward knows about, and the next time there is one, there will be a part of the ward that waits to see whether it was four times or once.*

*And none of the reasons go under those figures, because the reasons are for me and the figures are for them.*

And then, in the plainest words he had:

*I was wrong about the water, in a checkable way. My sentence said the twentieth day, second to fifth hour, and the water came on the nineteenth at the sixth hour of the evening, and I was wrong by a day, and eight hours early at the near edge of it, on the face of a page that is now on a ward board with my name on it.*

*I was wrong about the cause, and the cause was the one I left out because I did not want a Crown office in a sentence about water. The cause is a cart across the low-turn culvert under a Crown works order a fortnight old, with eleven witnesses on the record from the ninth day, and the cause was known to me and was in the watch book and I put a drain in the claim instead, because the drain I could stand in and prove and the cart I could only point at.*

*And a bounded claim does not stay bounded because a man is careful. It stops being bounded because a metal answer travels further than the man who rang it, and I have written that sentence inside the claim where Sabin Fell told me to write it, and I read it, and I rang it four times anyway.*

---

Oren entered the whole of it in the register at the eighth hour of the evening of the nineteenth day, in the form of his own that carries hours and places and no names, and the nineteenth has twelve entries on it, and they are entered the way he said he would enter them, which is by place and by hour and by nothing else. Six of them are in the maker’s own yard, and five of those six are the maker striking a new bell on his own bench with a leather, an hour apart, and the sixth is the seventh hour, out of the wind, in the open. One is at the low drain gate and one at the ward board, at the seventh hour and some minutes. Four are in the market lane at about the eighth hour. The column for the person who sounded it says *none* twelve times. The register is going to the district at the close of the seventh day, which is the twenty-fourth, and there will be twelve lines in a Crown book in Fourmile, and five of them will be one man’s own bench with an hour between each of them on it, and four of them will be a bell sounded four times in the market lane of Kells at about the eighth hour of the nineteenth, and the watch does not know by whom.

He read Tomas the entry when he had written it and said, “I am not asking you, and I have thought about it for a day.”

“I would rather you did ask me.”

“You would, and that is the whole of why I am leaving it alone: a sentence asked for is an answer, and an answer is in a book, and then the district could have it out of my book, and it would be the only name on the return and it would be a Crown clerk’s word for the sound of a bell in a market lane, and you would be the man who sounded a bell in a market lane on the strength of a Crown register entry. No.” Oren shut it. “And I want you to hear the other half. Four entries. Places. Hours. That is the Crown’s whole of it and there is nobody in it. And on the twenty-fourth there will be a man in Fourmile with a page that says a bell went off four times in your market lane, and that man will want a name, and he will not get one from me, and he may get one from somebody else, and that is not a thing I can stop and it is the reason the fourth time you rang it four times instead of once.”

Nobody in this town knew yet that on the ferry road, six hundred yards beyond the end of anything Tomas Vale had any business with, a man with a cart and a petition and nine co-signatories had been standing in his own gate for an hour, telling people what he had heard.

Ivo Prentice had heard the bell. He had heard all four of it from six hundred yards, in the rain, through a house, and he had understood three sentences of it and had believed all four, and at about half past the eighth hour he had gone out on his own gate and called to two houses and then to four, and he had said what he had heard, and he had been careful, because Ivo Prentice was the most careful man in the Low Ward and he had a paper in his house with nine names on it.

And what he said, in his own gate, on the ferry road, at half past the eighth hour on the nineteenth of the month, was:

“There will be water above the flat stones at the foot of the low lane *at first light*, and a Crown wagon with the round behind it.”

Nobody had said *at first light*. Tomas Vale had not said it, and neither had Sabin Fell, and neither had Hollis Renn, and neither had Wenna Cray, and it is not a phrase that occurs anywhere in the sentence he wrote that morning in a yard, or in the words he spoke in the market lane in the dark, or in any of the four times he rang them.

*At first light* is on the Crown’s own notice for the twenty-first. It is the phrase the commission used on the first notice and the district repeated in the return, and it is in a Crown hand in a Crown building, and on the evening of the nineteenth it walked out of a Crown office in a man’s mouth on a Crown’s road and into a ward that had spent nine days refusing to say the word children, and it arrived there the way a sentence arrives at the end of a bell: whole, and small, and already confident.

And it was repeated on the ferry road four more times that night by people who believed they were telling their neighbours, and by the second telling it was no longer a thing a bell had said, and by the fourth it was a thing that was going to happen, and the two families whose cart was still standing across a culvert two streets below them were already packing for a cart they did not have.
