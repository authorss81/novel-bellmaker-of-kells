# Chapter 0006 — The Order Before the Bell

The notice on the public board had been pinned there for three days, but the rain had loosened one corner and the clerk had not thought to replace it. Tomas arrived at market court with Nell’s waxed copy of the household fragment in a packet and the fixed carrier’s measurements in another pocket. He had not brought the carrier. The casting-room door remained locked, and the route beneath the Bellhouse had been covered with a board weighted by an iron block.

Deputy Reeve Halden Crake stood beneath the court’s tiled roof with two Crown officers and the same thin clerk from the landing office. The clerk held a short ledger against his chest. A knot had tied itself in the string of his seal case.

“Vale,” Halden said. “You are making a public record.”

“I am asking for the date and route number to be written where people can see them.”

“The notice is already public.”

“The date is partly hidden by the fold. The route is written in a line small enough to miss.”

A crowd had gathered. Fish sellers, carters, and women carrying baskets stood beneath the arcade. They had heard about the fixed carrier. Some had come to see whether Tomas would make it ring again. Others had come to find out whether the Crown intended to take their children.

The clerk glanced at Halden. “The notice can be amended.”

“Amended by whom?” Tomas asked.

“By the office that issued it.”

“Name the office.”

“The Crown survey office.”

“Name the person who signed it.”

The clerk’s fingers tightened on the ledger. “That is not necessary for a public notice.”

“It is necessary if the notice can take a child.”

A murmur passed through the market. Halden lifted one hand. The gesture was small, but the crowd quieted.

“You are asking for a clerk to endanger his posting,” he said.

“I am asking him to write down what the posting already says.”

“Those are not the same.”

“They become the same when the first is hidden and the second is punished.”

Tomas did not raise his voice. He had learned that anger made people look for a side to stand on. He unfolded the public notice flat against the board, weighting the lower edge with his own knife.

The order read:

CROWN RENEWAL INSPECTION  
LOW WARD — WEST ROAD — ROUTE L-4  
DAWN, FIFTH DAY FROM POSTING

The paragraph beneath it authorized entry into dwellings and examination of household rolls. It named “unregistered minors” as public assets pending civic review. A final line permitted removal of any signal apparatus that obstructed the inspection.

Tomas pointed to the date without touching the seal. “When was it filed?”

The clerk looked at Halden.

“Answer the question,” Halden said.

“Three days before the carrier rang,” the clerk said. “It was entered in the survey store before first light.”

A man near the front swallowed. “Three days before?”

“The date is on the notice.”

“Does that mean the bell knew about it?”

“It means the bell sounded after the order was filed,” Tomas said. “It does not mean the bell knew anything.”

The man looked angry at the distinction. “If children are being taken, why does the explanation matter?”

“Because if we treat every sound as an order, we will obey the first frightened person who gives one. If we treat every paper as harmless, we will obey the first person who hides the danger.”

Someone called from the back, “Are the children safe?”

Tomas turned. The question came from a woman holding the hand of a girl too young to understand why the market had stopped.

“I don’t know,” he said. “The order makes a credible danger. It does not tell me what has already happened or what will happen. Families should make their own decisions from the evidence they can see.”

The woman looked as if she wanted a more useful answer. Tomas could not give her one.

Halden stepped between him and the board. “You are not the reeve.”

“No.”

“You have no authority to interpret a Crown order.”

“No. But I can ask that its date be visible.”

The clerk unhooked a small wooden board from beneath the court’s shelter. On it he wrote:

FILED: THREE DAYS BEFORE THE CARRIER SOUNDED  
ROUTE: L-4  
DAWN: FIFTH DAY FROM POSTING

The writing was plain. Tomas asked the clerk to include the seal impression. The clerk hesitated, then pressed the seal onto the board. The red wax left a broken circle around the Crown mark.

Halden watched. “You have made your exhibit.”

“I have made a record of a public date.”

“Public records can be copied.”

“So can sealed ones.”

The clerk looked between them. “If I sign the board, I put my name to the copy.”

“You do not have to sign it,” Tomas said. “You can leave your name off and let the board speak for itself.”

That was crueler than he intended. The clerk understood it. He took the charcoal stub and wrote his initials beneath the seal.

The Crown officers moved closer. One of them was broad through the shoulders and had the dull bronze badge of a survey guard. He looked at Tomas’s satchel.

“You are carrying documents from a private household,” the guard said.

“I am carrying a fragment given to me by its owner.”

“Under what authority?”

“The owner’s permission.”

“The household is under inspection.”

“The household is not here.”

The guard’s hand moved to his belt. Halden lifted two fingers and the hand stopped.

“Vale,” Halden said, “you have been careful all morning. Do not make the next part difficult.”

“I want access to the survey store.”

The clerk made a small sound. “The store is closed.”

“For the public?” Tomas asked.

“For now.”

“I want to see the filing record.”

“You have the date on the board.”

“I want the route number, the name of the filing clerk, and the time the order was entered.”

“The order is not a criminal charge. You do not have the right to demand its working papers.”

“I am not demanding them. I am asking whether the public notice matches the record.”

“The notice is the record.”

“Then let the public see the record.”

Halden stepped close enough that Tomas could smell wet wool and the iron of the seal. “The Crown does not need a bellmaker to decide what a public record means.”

“No. It needs one,” Tomas said. “That is the danger.”

The guard’s expression hardened. Halden’s face did not change, but Tomas saw him calculate the cost of a crowd gathering around a confrontation.

“Public court,” Halden said. “No one enters the survey store. The notice stands as posted. The clerk has recorded the date. Vale is dismissed from the board.”

“If I refuse the public record, will you invoke emergency custody?”

“I will consider it if the public danger becomes immediate. I will not call a disputed warning an order merely because you have made me answer for it in front of these people.”

“Consider it in public, then.”

“And leave the board.”

“And the inspection?”

“Will be considered at the proper hearing.”

“Which is when?”

“The order says dawn.”

“That is not a hearing.”

“Then the Crown will hold one before the inspection if the law requires it.”

The clerk looked down at the board. “Deputy, the order’s current status is—”

“Not for public discussion.”

Tomas caught the phrase. It was not an answer, but it was a shape. The inspection had a status, and the status was not yet written in the open.

He left the court before someone decided to search his coat. The route measurements crinkled against his chest. He had gained a date, a route number, and a public seal. He had not gained a name, a child, or a guarantee.

Before going toward the Low Ward, Tomas walked behind the market court. The notice board’s post had been set into a shallow stone channel, and the old bellwire ran beside it beneath a strip of dark pitch. He crouched where the channel met the wall and followed the copper by touch. A bright splice crossed the older line, stamped with the same survey triangle he had copied from the carrier. The mark was not proof of a connection, but it was proof that the Crown’s route had been opened and repaired near the public notice.

He left the splice in place and copied its position on the back of his hand. The route could explain how a warning moved. It could not tell him who had accepted the duty that made it worth moving.

The Low Ward lay beyond the market, where the houses leaned toward the water. Tomas found Nell at the corner of the west road. She had the original household fragment tied inside her shirt.

“Did you see the board?” she asked.

“Yes.”

“Did they say the children are being taken?”

“They said minors may be held as public assets. That is not the same as telling a parent what will happen to a child.”

“It is close enough.”

“Close is not proof. But it is not nothing.”

Nell looked toward the road. “Jory’s line is on that board.”

“I know.”

“Will you take the bell there?”

Tomas thought of the fixed carrier locked upstairs, the empty crown, the child’s handprint in wax. “Not with that carrier. It has no clapper, and I do not know who is driving its route.”

“Could you make another one?”

“Not today.”

“Why not?”

“Because I do not yet know the claim, and because making a useful sound without a free answer would be a faster way to do harm.”

Nell looked down the road. “You are good at not doing things.”

“Most of the time.”

The watch captain appeared at the market court arch. Oren Pike had seen the crowd and come to find out what had caused it. His gaze found Tomas, then the board, then the route number Tomas had copied into his notebook.

“Vale,” he said.

“Captain.”

“Come to the watch house.”

“Now?”

“Now.”

Oren’s expression held no welcome, but it held less accusation than the morning’s official tone. He turned toward the gate.

Tomas followed. Behind them, the clerk’s board remained in the market court, its seal and date exposed to anyone willing to read.

Oren waited until they were out of earshot. “The inspection came through a watch route,” he said. “My men were ordered not to question the rider. The order arrived with a Crown seal, but the local route was already marked as urgent.”

“Which route?”

“L-4.”

“Who ordered your men to stand down?”

“Deputy Crake.”

“Why would he do that?”

Oren’s mouth tightened. “Because he believed the order was lawful. Or because he believed the cost of disobeying it would fall on the town. I have not decided which.”

“Do you know who the local surveyor is?”

Oren looked toward the market. “That is the other problem. He is not on the roll. He was expected at the north gate yesterday. His service ticket was withdrawn this morning.”

“Where is he?”

“Missing.”

The word settled between them. A missing surveyor was not a ghost or a hidden answer. It was an absent person whose absence could make a warning easier to dismiss.

Tomas looked toward the north gate, where the fixed watch bell hung in its stone frame. “Did the route repeat after the carrier rang?”

Oren’s gaze sharpened. “You heard it?”

“Someone did.”

“I heard it once. I have not put that in a report.”

“Why not?”

“Because I do not yet know whether I heard a bell or the memory of one.”

It was the first time Tomas had heard Oren admit that his judgment might not be enough. It did not make them allies. It made the road ahead less certain.

Oren handed him a folded slip. “A copy of the route docket. The surveyor’s name is on it. The name is not on the public roll.”

Tomas took the paper. “I will need to compare it with the service marks.”

“Do that where the watch can witness it. No private searching.”

“Agreed.”

“And Vale—”

He stopped when a Crown courier came through the gate, carrying a narrow black case. The courier did not look at either of them. He went directly to Halden, who was waiting beside the survey store.

Oren watched him go. “When you find the route, do not make it speak for the town.”

“I am trying not to.”

“Good.” Oren’s face closed. “That is what I would have said before the mistake.”

The words did not ask forgiveness. They gave Tomas a task and a witness.

He folded the docket and walked toward the Bellhouse, leaving the market court to its divided answers.
