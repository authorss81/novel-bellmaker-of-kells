# Chapter 0024 — Two Men on the Gate

The two men came in before the watch changed and sat down without being asked, which was the first thing Oren noticed and the reason he did not trust the morning.

Ardle had his cap in his hands. Cask had not taken his off at all, and after a while Oren understood that this was a statement.

“You have not slept,” Oren said.

“No, Captain.”

“Whose decision was that?”

Ardle and Cask looked at each other, which is what men do when a question has more than one correct answer.

“Mine,” Cask said. “I would like it written that I would do it again and that I would like to know I did it right.”

“Then say what happened and I will tell you what I know, and I will not tell you that you did right, because I do not know whether you did, and because a captain who says *you did right* to a man at seven in the morning is buying something for himself.”

Cask put his cap down at last.

“Cart went at about twenty to five, and I say about because I have not got a clock on that gate and never have. It did not stop at the gate because I had the gate barred for the wagon, which I have no authority to do and did anyway, and it came through the gate and up to the axle in the ford beyond it, and the far horse took fright and I got hold of it. That is the whole of the trouble I had.” He rubbed his face. “There was a woman on the tailboard. Not on it. On it, sitting on the boards with her hands round a rail, and she was not the one we took out at 114 because the woman at 114 did not get on the wagon. That woman had no business on that wagon and she was on it, and I could see her, and I did not put my hand on her arm.”

“You had a warrant for a lawful removal of three children and a wagon going north.”

“I had a warrant for three children,” Cask said. “That was a woman of about forty and I do not know whose she was and I have not asked and I am telling you anyway.”

Oren wrote it down. He wrote it exactly, in Cask’s words, without softening and without the word *inattention* that he had been about to use.

Then he said, “That is the whole of what you have done and it is going in the book with your name on it, and I am not going to put the word *failed* in it either, because the moment I put a judgment in that book about either of you it stops being a record and becomes a disposition, and a disposition can be read back to you in a year by a man who was not here.”

Ardle said, “Captain, we did a lawful thing and three children went. That is what the book is going to say when it is read out. Would you rather it said something else?”

“No. I would rather it said the true thing, which is that two watchmen carried out a lawful order at the commission’s direction in open court before dawn, on my instruction, in the open, in front of a hundred and forty people, so that no man in this watch would have to do it in a lane in the dark; that they did it correctly; that three children were taken; and that I chose the hour, not them.”

“Then we are not to be blamed,” Ardle said.

“You are not to be blamed, and you are not to be praised either, and neither of those is a thing I can give you and I am sorry.” Oren closed the book. “There is nothing at the end of the service in which either of you is promoted, and there is nothing at the end of it in which either of you is dismissed, and I have no authority to do the first and no wish to do the second, and if the commission wants a man to blame it will have to come and take one off me.”

---

Jerm came in at the eighth hour with the gate book under his arm and a Crown return in his hand, and he put the return on the table face up, which he had never done with a Crown paper in three years.

“Read the second paragraph,” he said.

Oren read it.

*The ward having been notified at the north gate and having responded, the return of this round is certified as partial only, and the balance of the round is carried to the next posting.*

“Read the footnote under it,” Jerm said.

*Notification and response evidenced by the Kells watch gate book, tenth day, entries fourteen to nineteen.*

“That is my book,” Oren said.

“That is your hand.”

He turned the gate book round. It was a thick ledger, the watch’s own, in which every gate movement in this town had been entered for eleven years by four men in turn, and the entries for the tenth day were in his own writing, because the tenth day had been his own.

*Entry fourteen. Fourth hour and ten minutes. Surveyor and Crown party to north gate. Round opened.*
*Entry fifteen. Fourth hour and forty minutes. Crown wagon northbound through the gate and over the ford beyond it. One child, taken from a household at one hundred and fourteen. A woman rode on the tailboard and was not on the warrant.*
*Entry sixteen. Fifth hour and ten minutes. Crown wagon returned and out again. One further child, from a household at one hundred and twenty-one. Household at one hundred and nineteen answered the door and was marked.*
*Entry seventeen. Fifth hour and fifty minutes. Crown wagon returned and out again. One further child, from a household at one hundred and thirty-three, which gave no answer and no name. Crowd at the ford, thirty persons, no disorder, no injuries.*
*Entry eighteen. Sixth hour. Crown wagon northbound for the third time. Party returned on foot: two officers, one filing clerk, one surveyor, one case-man, plus unlisted.*
*Entry nineteen. Sixth hour and thirty minutes. Round closed by the surveyor.*

Oren read it four times. He had written it at the gate in the rain with a bad hand and it had taken him eleven minutes and it was, in every particular, an accurate record of a gate’s traffic on a wet morning.

“They have taken the words *notified* and *responded* out of my entries,” he said, “and they have found them in a book that never claimed to contain them.”

Jerm said, “Entry fifteen. I wrote that. The woman on the tailboard, not on the warrant. That is the woman Cask has just told you about, and it is the only piece of paper in this town that says there was a fourth person on that wagon, and the Crown’s return calls entry fifteen *notified*.”

“Yes.”

“Captain, I asked for something yesterday and I am going to ask for it again and I want it in the book whether you agree or not.”

“Say it.”

“I wrote the slates. I would not write the marks, and I did not, and that was the only thing I refused yesterday. But the Crown officers wrote the marks themselves, so my refusal cost the ward nothing, and I have been thinking about that all night, and what I have come to is that a record which names only the men who obey is not a record, it is a list of examples. So I want the book to name the men who obeyed and the man who refused, and I want the refuser’s refusal to be entered as a refusal and not as an omission, and I want the day’s conduct written so that a man could read it in ten years and know which of us stood where.”

Oren looked at him for a moment.

“That is a right thing to ask for and I am going to do it, and I want you to understand what it costs and I am not going to explain it away. If I name Ardle and Cask as men who carried out a lawful order, and I name you as the man who would not write a mark, then in ten years there are three names in a book and no way to tell a reader which of them I thought was right. And I do not know which of them I thought was right, and I have not got the standing to decide, and the best I can do is write the order each man was given and the thing each man did about it, and let the order stand in the front of the page where anybody can find it.”

“Do that,” Jerm said, “and I will hold the pen while you do it.”

“You will not hold the pen while I do it. You will read it back and if I have made a man sound better or worse than he was, you will say so, and I will change it.”

The black-case man came at the ninth hour and asked for an interview with the watch.

“I am not asking to interview the watch,” he said, correcting himself before Oren could. “I am asking to interview the two men who carried the seizure, in a private room, for the file. Not under caution. On the record, in the room, with you present.”

“And the man who would not write a mark?”

“If he wishes to be present.”

“And the crowd at the ford,” Oren said. “Thirty persons. And the household at one hundred and nineteen.”

“They may also be present.”

“Then you may interview the watch in this room, with the door open, with the book on the table, and with a clerk of this watch’s choosing to take down every question and every answer, and any man of the watch who wants to be present may be, and any man who does not may go to the yard and stay there and nobody will write his name down or hold it against him.”

The black-case man considered it.

“You are protecting them from me.”

“I am protecting the *record* from you,” Oren said. “There is a difference and it is the only one I have. If I interview a man of this watch in a back room, then there are two accounts, and one of them is in a file in the district town, and the other is in the head of a man who was in a back room. In ten years the file will be the account. So: open door, pen, book.”

The black-case man looked at the door, which was open.

“Agreed,” he said, “and I will put in the file that the watch captain required it open, so that the file is not quietly a better record than it would otherwise have been.”

That was fair, and Oren wrote it down as fair, and the interview took an hour and forty minutes, and it produced almost nothing, because there was almost nothing: a cart, a ford, a bar, a horse, a boy on a tailboard.

And at the eleventh hour, on his own, without being asked by anybody, Oren wrote the memorandum.

*MEMORANDUM, entered by Captain Oren Pike, Kells watch, this day, on my own hand and my own motion.*

*The Kells gate book is a record of gate traffic and observation. It is written by watchmen in the course of duty and it has no standing as a record of the consent, agreement, answer, or will of any household or any ward, and it never claimed any. Any paper of any office which cites the Kells gate book as evidence that a ward was notified or had responded is citing a document for a thing it does not contain, and I say so now, in advance of being asked, because I have spent one night understanding that a book of mine is being read by a man in the district town as a permission and that the reason he is able to do it is that I wrote eleven years of it in a clear hand.*

*I further enter that I have not read the gate book for the night of the Hush of Kells, and have not permitted any man of this watch to read it aloud, and that the reason is that a man who has read it will be tempted to use it, and that I have not the standing to be the man who decides that for this town.*

*I further enter that if the commission believes that the Kells watch has given an account of the ward’s will, that account is false, and that it is false in my hand, and that the false hand is mine.*

He read it back to Jerm and Ferrow and signed it, and then — because it was his own book and his own fault and he had ten years of habit in his handwriting — he did something he had never done.

He read it out in the market court at the noon hour, in the open, to about thirty people, and left the copy on the clerk’s table, and told Ferrow to give it to anybody who asked.

Then Deputy Crake found him at the top of the arcade steps.

“Pike.”

“Deputy.”

“I am going to say something and I would like it understood that I have thought about it for six hours.” Crake was not enjoying himself and was not pretending to. “There is a district appointment. Northern district. Watching a river crossing with a stone bridge and two arrests a month. It is a better appointment than this one, it is honest work, and it is four days’ ride, and the commission would approve it in a letter and you would be gone before the round is closed.”

“Thank you.”

“I am not doing it as a favour. I am doing it because in nine days you have made three records that are harder to file than anything I have received in a year, and because a captain who is going to keep making them is a man I will have to be able to remove, and I would rather remove you to a bridge than to a cell, and I would rather you went quietly.” He looked out over the arcade. “And I will tell you the other reason, because you would work it out and I would rather say it. If I move you, the ward reads it as me getting my own way. If I move you with a document, in front of people, on a morning, it reads as me not being able to move you. You have spent eleven days making me say things in the open. This is my one move and it is behind your back, which I have never done in my life and do not intend to do again.”

“Which is the reason to refuse it,” Oren said.

“Yes.”

They stood there for a moment.

“Then I will say the rest in the open, in the same room, at the same hour, in the same words, so that it is in your hand and not mine,” Oren said, “and you will put it in the file yourself, and I will read it out at the court at the second hour this afternoon with the ward standing in front of me while I do it.”

“That is not the same as a private conversation.”

“It is not. It is a worse one.”

“Considerably worse.”

“Considerably better at being true,” Oren said, “and I have spent twelve years finding out what that costs and I would like to be able to put one thing in the book today that I got right.”

Crake looked at him for a long moment, and then said, “Second hour. And if I answer it, you will let the answer stand.”

“I will let the answer stand in full, in your words, and read it out after mine.”

“Then the district office will hear about you by the end of the week,” Crake said, “and you will not like it, and I am sorry about that, and I am going to do it anyway, because you have made a record that a district office cannot ignore and I would rather it came from a bridge post than from a file.”

He went down the steps. Oren stood at the top of them and watched him go, and thought, with a clarity that surprised him, that the man was going to be more dangerous in four days than he had been in eleven, and that this was not a discovery about the deputy.

It was a discovery about what it costs a decent man to keep doing a thing he knows will be punished.

Tomas Vale came up the hill at the last hour with the custody receipt in his hand and the margin on it, and asked for the second copy of the L-4 file.

“No,” Oren said.

“You had it open in front of a Crown copyist nine nights ago. You told me to my face that it was in a drawer in your own watch house where you could read it.”

“It is. It is six sheets and a schedule and I have read them all twice and a third time this week.” He put his hand on the drawer. “The ninth hour, any morning you like, in this room, with Jerm at the table. You may read all six sheets for as long as you want them, and I will write down the hour and the sheets and that nothing was taken out of the drawer, and that note is the only thing I can give you and it is not much.”

“That is not what I asked for.”

“No. It is what I have instead of it.”

“Then say why, plainly, because I have had a day in which nobody tells me why anything.”

“Because it is Crown property,” Oren said, “and because the only copy of it in this town is the one the watch holds, and the watch does not make copies of Crown files for private parties. If I copied that file for you, then in a month there would be a copy in your yard and a copy in the ward and a copy in whatever building your sister is in if she is alive, and in a year there would be five, and then the Crown would file the *filing* of that file, and one day somebody in the district town would be able to say that the local watch had set about duplicating Crown papers, and that would be true, and it would be the end of the only thing I have left to do here.”

“So I may read it, and I may not keep it.”

“You may read it and you may not keep it, and I will note the hour and the sheets and that nothing was removed, and that note is mine and it is the only one that will be worth anything in a year.” He shut the drawer. “You want a piece of paper you can hold. I understand it. If I cannot do that for you, then what I can do is be the man who will read it to you in a room, and that is a worse thing, and you are entitled to say so.”
