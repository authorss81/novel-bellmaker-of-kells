# Chapter 0011 — The First Refusal

The two Crown survey guards came back at first light and took up their positions under the arcade as if they had never left. One of them wrote in a small book. The other watched the yard gate. Neither man had eaten yet, and the one with the book kept rubbing his thumb over the same line, the way a man rubs a coin he has been given for bad news.

Three blank witness slips lay on the workbench, cut from cheap paper by a clerk's knife. Tomas had laid them out before dawn because Oren had handed them over at midnight with the observation that a man who waits for the right moment to be given paper will decide the paper ought to have said something else.

He did not move them. He moved everything else.

The trial handbell sat on its bench under a folded cloth. He lifted the cloth, checked the crown, and set it back. The sliver of the L-4 posting was still wedged in the wooden frame where anyone could take it out with two fingers and read the route, the dawn, and the broken seal. The paper did not touch the rim. He had cut it that way on purpose.

The no-public-ringing order was still nailed to the door, and its terms were narrow: no public ringing in this workshop, no ringing of any bell connected to the north-gate route, and no movement of the fixed carrier, which was not here to move. The carrier itself was two streets away in the watch store, upright in its frame in a padded cradle, the retaining bolts released for transport, the red watch seal tied around the lower brace. Tomas had walked past the watch house at four in the morning and had not gone in. Knowing exactly where a thing was had become one of the ordinary duties of his week.

The problem on the bench was this: he had three slips and no lawful bell to put them near.

He put the candidates on the back of a wrapping sheet.

*TRIAL HANDBELL — bound, one accepted answer already, named in the order. Out.*

*LOOSE RELAY BELLS — under the same order, and the connected ones sit on a Crown line. Out.*

*THE NORTH-GATE WATCH BELL — ordinary, proper clapper, never touched by me. If I sound that, the Crown's own instrument carries my claim and the line under the wall carries it further. Out.*

He had a fourth line and did not want to write it.

*MARKET COURT CALL BELL — unbound iron, hand striker, kept on a rack in the court loft. Not on the route. The court owns it. The ushers ring it to call an assembly.*

That one was not ruled out. It was also not available, because a bell in the market court's loft belonged to the market court, and nobody there had been asked.

Sabin Fell came at midmorning with a covered basket and the expression of a man who had walked farther than he intended.

“A pear,” Sabin said before Tomas could speak. “That is what the guard wrote. If I had said tools, he would have had to open it.”

“You came into my yard to make a joke about a pear.”

“To stop you from asking the same question three times.”

He read the four lines twice, which Tomas appreciated, because Sabin did not reread anything he meant to dismiss.

“Four things you cannot use, and one you have not asked anyone to own,” he said. “That is a good morning. Sit down, because the next part is where men like us get clever.”

He turned the sheet over and drew a box around Tomas's question marks.

“Here is the rule. You set the claim down once. You read the same words to each person, in the same order. If you improve the words for the second person because the first one frightened you, you are not collecting answers. You are arranging agreement.”

“A question asked twice is a demand,” Tomas said.

“You have been paying attention. It is unsettling.” Sabin tapped the box. “The other half is worse. If you thank a man for an answer, you have taken it away and given it back decorated. Write what he said. If it is ugly, that is what you have.”

“And if two of them say the same thing?”

“Two answers that happen to match. Not a stronger answer. A common one.” He tapped again. “If the third says the opposite, you do not have one answer with a doubt attached. You have three answers. You may not keep the two and file the one.”

Oren came at the fourth hour with rain beginning in the western sky, his boots soaked to the ankle. He had walked over from the gate, which he did not often do.

“You have a list,” he said.

“A list of what I cannot ring. It is the more useful one.” Tomas pushed the sheet across. “The court bell is possible. The clerk will not answer for it. He says the answer is not his to give, which is the first honest sentence I have had from a Crown clerk in a week. He says that when somebody of standing accepts a duty for that bell, and brings him the words, he will answer, or he will record who refused.”

“That is a way of saying we cannot ring it yet.”

“It is a way of saying you may not ring it until somebody in that room has a duty to hear it.” Oren turned the sheet over. “Now the frame. Four parts, and nothing is a test until all four are written.”

He wrote on the back of a Crown requisition form, which Tomas would regret letting him use.

“**One.** The physical witness. Something a person can walk to and touch. If you cannot point at it, it is not a claim. It is a feeling with a bell attached.”

“**Two.** The keeper's duty. One person, named, with a specific shape. Not protect the town. Warn about the thing you have looked at, name the evidence and the gaps in it, and stop there.”

“**Three.** The three answers. Independent. Free. Each may say yes, no, later, or this much and not that. Each recorded in their own words.”

“**Four.** The carrier. Who strikes it, how many times, and what it is not permitted to add. If the last part is blank, the bell adds whatever the crowd expects, and the crowd will not be wrong about hearing it.”

“Where does my claim go?”

“In the second part. You are keeper of the words.” Oren tapped the paper. “The bell is a door. You are not the door.”

Something in that landed harder than it should have. Tomas thought of the trial handbell on its bench, and of how quickly the sense of having made a useful thing had become the sense of being the one entitled to use it.

“Why five people? You said three answers.”

“Because you do not know which three will answer, and a man asked only once can be told it is an honour.” Oren looked toward the gate. “I have three slips from last night and two more I took out of the watch store's book this morning, because three is a number you can be handed and five is a number you have to go looking for. I have five slips. I have not five answers. Write the words, Vale. Everything else is weather.”

Tamsin Rook came at the fifth hour with rain in her scarf and the recovered handbell in her coat, out of sight and out of anybody's hand. The guard with the book had already written her name on a second page with the word *courier* beside it, and Tomas understood that a person who arrives on a Crown schedule is difficult to treat as an intruder.

“You have guards on your door.”

“I have guards on my gate. The door is mine.”

“Same thing, in a town this week.” She looked at the three slips without touching them, then at the covered bench. “You have written a carrier list and no carrier. Then you have a test that does not exist, and the first thing that happens is that somebody rings something on their own and the crowd decides it was yours. A public tone is not the same size as the claim under it. That is all I came to say, and I will say it once.”

“Go on.”

“The number of people who can hear a sound is not the number of people who agreed to it. My handbell is authorized from the north gate to the river landing. The wax in its crown was laid over an older groove, so it repeats past the landing. Past the landing it is not a Low Ward signal. It is a thing that sounds like one, travelling into a village that never chose to be told anything.”

“Have you measured that?”

“I had a watchman at the landing listen while I rang at the gate. He heard it. I did not ring it again with anyone else listening, because I do not hand out my bell for that.” Her jaw moved. “There is a village above the landing. During the spring rise they acted on a repeated warning about a ford — four households and a cart up into the hills for two days. Nothing false was said. The ford was not going to flood that week. The warning described a rise that had already passed by the time it reached them.”

“So a true thing arrived false by being late and wide.”

“A true thing arrived as something that was not theirs.” She glanced at the guard with the book. “And there is a version of your warning going round at the landing that ends with *before dawn*. You never said it there. A boy said it on Saturday and the wind took most of it, and now every woman on that quay believes a Crown bell named a time.”

“I wrote it down as what a boy said.”

“Good. Keep it that way. A man who repeats a rumor to kill it has adopted it.”

The market court was half full by the sixth hour, more than Tomas had expected. Rain had driven people out of the low lanes and under the arcade, and the clerk's board with its filing date, its route number, and its broken seal stood where Tomas had asked for it yesterday with one corner still lifting.

Oren set a small table against the arcade rail and laid the frame face up. He did not sit. Tomas understood that a watch captain who sat would be presiding, and presiding was a claim he had no right to make.

Five people had agreed to come. He had asked them in the lane rather than at their doors, because a request made at a door has a witness standing in the doorway.

He read the words he had written that morning and rewritten twice.

“Here is what I can support. A Crown notice was filed three days before the fixed carrier sounded. It schedules a dawn renewal inspection on route L-4, and it names unregistered minors as public assets pending civic review. The carrier has no clapper. The route under this workshop was cut and rejoined with a bypass after it rang. I do not know who ordered the seizure, who removed the clapper, or who will answer for the danger. I will not tell any household what to do with its children, its goods, or its door.”

Then the question, the same words each time.

“Will you accept a duty to carry those words, as written, to people who ask you for them? You may accept less than all of it. You may ask for time. You may refuse. Your answer goes on a slip in your own words, and it stands for no one else.”

The first man accepted. The second asked whether the words would be read at the ward's own board or only at the market court, and Tomas said he did not know yet and would not promise, and the man said he would answer when he knew, which Oren wrote as *time asked* and not as a yes.

The third, a woman who sold salt fish on the landing, said she would carry the words to any house on her row that asked her, and added in the same breath that she was not going to be the one who tells a mother to move her child. Tomas said that was correct and would go in her line as she had said it.

The fourth accepted and asked for a copy, because he had heard the version with *before dawn* and wanted to know what had actually been said. Oren gave him the copy and entered the paper in the watch account, which was thin.

The fifth was Bevin Sarr, a net-maker with gray hands and a wet sleeve, who had come because his brother mended the market's grain nets and he did not want the ward to be the kind of place where a brother stopped working.

He read the question with one finger on the line, the way a man checks a fence for a gap.

Then he said no.

“No,” Bevin Sarr said. “I will not answer that.”

The arcade quieted by a degree Tomas felt in his teeth. Behind him the guard with the book stopped for a moment and then wrote faster, which was the right thing to do and the wrong feeling to watch.

“Why?” Tomas said, and heard the wrong question in his own voice half a second after it was out. He had meant to ask. What came out had the shape of an objection.

“You want to know so you can answer it for me. That is not an insult. It is what you would do. You would find a way to write my no so it counted as a yes with a note attached, and then you would put the note in a drawer and use the yes.”

Oren's hand stayed flat on the table. “Say more or stop there. Both are allowed.”

“My name is on the ward's list. Not a renewal list. The other one, the one the clerk keeps for the Crown, with house numbers and who is inside. I have been on it since my wife died and I did not pay the entry fee. If I put my name on a slip that says a public answer was given for this ward, the slip goes into the same office as the list. Once it is in that office I cannot take it out, I cannot correct it, and nobody in this arcade will read it again.”

“So write it and keep it. Do not file it. I will hold it.”

“You will hold it until you need it.” Sarr looked at the three blanks. “The paper is not the harm. The office is. A slip in a drawer is safe. A slip in a file is a thing other men read out loud in a room you are not standing in.”

Tomas felt the answer rise in him, the one that said *I would protect it* and *I would be the one who decides where it lives*. He heard how much of it was about him.

He tapped the table twice with his knuckle, and then stopped tapping.

“Captain,” he said. “Write what he said.”

Oren wrote it in the man's own words and did not compress them, and turned the sheet so Sarr could read it.

“Sign it if you want,” he said. “Not if you don't.”

Sarr read it. He did not sign. “Leave it unsigned. Then it is a record that a man declined, and not a record that a man signed something.”

“As you like.”

“I will say one more thing and then go home before this arcade floods.” Sarr looked at the table. “You have three answers if the salt woman counts, and one man waiting for an answer. That is enough for whatever you are going to do. But a slip in a file is only what it is in a file. So it is for me. So it is for everyone in the Low Ward who is not in this arcade.”

He rolled his sleeve down and went out into the rain.

Oren did not look up. “That is a refusal. It is not sabotage and it is not cowardice. Write it as the fourth line, in his words, and write that the test went on without him.”

“I would have argued with him.”

“You did argue with him. He gave you a better argument and you accepted it, and it cost you a witness.” Oren capped the ink. “That is the whole trade, and I am not going to pretend it is clean.”

The four who remained had not left, and one of the four was a man who had told them he would answer when he knew where the words would be read. The salt woman spoke first.

“Not the Crown order,” she said. “We have had that read, and feared it, and slept badly on it. Stand up in that court and read us a paper about a seal, and half of them will hear a clerk being clever, because that is what clerks do to us.”

“Then tell me what to name instead.”

“The water,” said an older man at the back with a rope-scarred forearm. “I am Wenna Cray's uncle and I will not pretend I speak for her. The water is up two marks on the sill at the low gate since yesterday. My cellar took it. Take my hand to the stone and feel what I feel. That is not a seal. It is a thing that will be in my house tonight whether your bell is lawful or not.”

“The west road takes the low section first,” said another. “It took water there in the spring rise, and this rain is coming back, and the bridge keeper is one man with one pair of boots.”

“You want the test to ask a real question,” Oren said. “Not only what the Crown did. What the river is doing.”

“Yes,” Tomas said.

The charcoal stopped. He had wanted a claim about the inspection, because the inspection was what frightened him and because a claim about a Crown order could be supported with paper he could hold in his hand. A claim about water would have to be made at a river gate, in the rain, in front of people who would know within a minute whether he was lying.

He set it down anyway, in the second part, where the keeper's duty lived, and he did not yet have the sentence.

The clerk came out with his ledger under his arm and the knot in his seal string pulled tight again.

“Captain, I will answer your note now, since you have brought me a room with people in it.” He read the frame, which he had not been invited to read and read anyway, because a clerk who does not read the page is no use to anyone. “The court bell is unbound iron. Rope, hand striker, no line of any kind under it, and the usher can prove it better than I can. I will not lend it for words nobody in the room has accepted. When a keeper of standing accepts a duty for that bell, and brings me the words, I will answer, or I will record who refused.”

“And if nobody accepts a duty for it?”

“Then you have no bell, Vale, and the town keeps its fear and you keep your four parts. I will write in the court book that the request was made and by whom, because a request that is refused is still a thing that happened.”

He went inside. The door shut. The rain came on properly.

Tomas put all five into the watch envelope separately — three acceptances, the request for time, and the refusal — with a sheet between each, so that no clerk could lift the top and read a town.

Then he wrote at the top of the header, in the space where a claim should have been, four words he had not put down before:

WHAT THE WATER OWES US

Oren looked at it. “That is not a claim.”

“It is the name of the next question. You said everything else is weather.”

“I said that about witnesses.”

“I have a frame, a refusal, and a demand I did not make. I have no bell, no carter, and a clerk writing down that I asked.” He folded the header into his coat, where the paper was already warm against his chest. “If the water comes up before the rescheduled dawn, I will have spent today building a structure with nothing at the center of it.”

Oren walked him to the gate. The guards wrote the hour. The watch bell gave its ordinary tired note as the shift changed, and Tomas did not flinch, which was a small victory of a kind he could not afford.

“The man who refused today will be asked again at the public test,” Oren said. “He will probably refuse again. Put that in your frame now, so that when it happens you have to look at it.”

Tomas stood in the wet a moment, the envelope of five separate slips against his chest. Below him a woman was carrying salt fish along a row of doors, telling three households the exact words a bellmaker had read at the market court, in the order he had read them, and saying nothing at all about the two men who had not agreed to carry them. Somewhere a man with gray hands was under his own roof, unrepresented, because the office that held his name was the office he feared more than the river.

He walked home to open the shop, and the day-book had more names in the lost column than in the paid one.
