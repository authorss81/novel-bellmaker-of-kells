# Chapter 0017 — What the Water Owes

Tomas had told himself for three days that the sentence would arrive when the water gave him something to be accurate about. On the seventh morning the water gave him three things by six o'clock, and he wrote the claim on the back of the withdrawal procedure with his hand shaking from cold rather than from anything else.

The rain had stopped in the night and started again in the grey hour before dawn, which was worse, because it let people go out and think the day was improving.

The claim read:

*THE WATER STANDS ABOVE THE SECOND MARK AT THE LOW GATE. THE CROWN ROAD GANG'S CART IS ACROSS THE CULVERT AT THE LOW TURN AND HAS BEEN SINCE YESTERDAY. I HAVE MEASURED AN UNDERCUT AT THE NORTH END OF THE WEST ROAD'S LOW SECTION, AT THE OUTER EDGE, ONE SPAN AND A HALF FROM THE NORTHERN WALL. I CANNOT TELL YOU WHEN THE WATER WILL CREST. I CANNOT TELL YOU WHETHER THE LOW SECTION WILL HOLD. I WILL NOT TELL YOU WHAT TO DO WITH ANYTHING YOU OWN.*

He read it aloud to the empty yard. It was eleven sentences long, and Sabin would have called it a paragraph instead of a bell's worth, and Sabin would have been right, and Tomas wrote it down anyway because every time he shortened it he left out the part where he admitted he did not know, and the part where he admitted he did not know was the only reason a person could stand behind the rest of it.

He took the survey rod, a chalk, a line, and his own boot, and left the bench cold.

The low turn of the west road was under nine inches of moving water and had been since the middle of the night. A Crown road gang had been working a culvert there for a fortnight, and the culvert was the reason the Low Ward did not flood every spring. Their foreman was standing in the shallows with a hip full of paper when Tomas got there.

“Cart's across it,” the foreman said, before he was asked. “One stone and half a load of gravel, to stop the works washing out. It comes off when the reeve's men come with a barrow to stand the frames in, and they have not come, because the deputy has other business and the Crown has a hearing.”

“How long has it been across?”

“Since yesterday noon. I put it there. I signed for it.” He said it without embarrassment, which Tomas found harder than a lie. “It holds the works. Take it off without me and the frames go down and there is a hole in that road you could lose a cart in, and then this becomes a road nobody can use at all instead of a road that is under water this week.”

“Somebody has to write that down.”

“Write it down. It is in my book. It is in the reeve's book. It is in the Crown book, if the Crown writes its own books, which in my experience it does not.” The foreman looked at the water coming down the road. “Are you the bellmaker? The one the guards are standing over?”

“I am.”

“Then you can tell them the road is not the Crown's fault for being under. It is the river's. The river does not read the Crown's letters.”

The undercut was where Tomas said it would be, because the rod said so and the chalk mark from the spring rise was four inches lower and eighteen inches out. He put the rod in at one span and a half from the northern wall, felt it go through, and pulled it up with a foot of brown water in the hollow of it. He wrote the depth and the time and put a chalk cross on the wall so that another man could find the same place in an hour without being told twice.

Then he walked the low section both ways, which took longer than the measuring, and found two more things: a fish fence that had been pulled out of its sockets and a boundary stone that had moved in the night. He wrote all three down in a hand that was getting worse.

At the low gate, Hollis Renn was standing at his sill with the oilskin rolled and a look on his face that Tomas had not seen before and did not like.

“You have not rung,” Tomas said.

“I have not read the mark yet. Six o'clock is for reading, not for guessing.”

“You said you would read at six.”

“Six this evening. It is half past one in the afternoon, and if I have not read it with my own hand in the hour before, then six this evening is the hour, and a man who rings at one o'clock on a hunch is the boy that shut this market eleven years ago.”

“I have had your man from the watch at my gate since a quarter past five with a bar, because the last time the water came up in a hurry my windlass did not move and I found out afterwards that the pawl was slipping and there was gravel packed behind the sill like mortar.” Renn nodded at a watchman standing in the road with a long iron bar over his shoulder, who was not Jerm. “So here is what happened, and here is what I am not going to do.”

“Tell me.”

“Jam.” Renn crouched and put his hand into the gap where the oak met the stone. The watchman levered the bar and the gate came up a finger, ground like a mill, and stopped. Renn pulled out a double handful of gravel and clay that had been packed into the slot behind the sill by the river itself. “Forty years, and I have cleared that slot twice. I clear it when I clear it, and I have not cleared it since the spring, because there is a hard frost under it and a man does not want to be on his knees at that sill in February for a thing that may not matter.”

“It matters today.”

“It matters today.” He wiped his hands. “So I will tell you the two things I will and will not do, and then you can go and be a bellmaker somewhere else. I will not carry a sentence about the west road. I keep a gate and a mark. You have a road out there with an undercut in it and a Crown cart in a culvert, and I have no standing to tell anybody anything about a road, and the last keeper I knew who carried words past the thing he kept was my own father, and it took a market day and three families out of their bread to teach me.”

“And the second thing.”

“The second thing is that nothing comes off this sill until the bar has been under it and the windlass has been put round twice with the weight on. After that I am a man with a gate again, and I will say what the board says and not one word beyond it.” He stood up with some effort. “You can come to the court at dusk if you like. What I say there will be shorter than yours, and the gate will still go up.”

Tomas looked at the packed gravel in Renn's hands and at the man with the bar.

“How long to clear it properly?”

“Two men and three hours if the frost lets go of it. One man and most of the day if it does not.”

“Will you let the watch send you a second man?”

Renn considered it, and it was visibly a question about pride rather than about the river.

“One,” he said. “And he carries gravel and he does not stand in my gate and tell me about the Crown.”

Jerm came up the lane at the seventh hour with the second man and a shovel, and Renn put him to work without another word. Tomas left them at it and went up the west road to find Wyman Dace.

He found the bridge keeper halfway up, standing in a doorway out of the rain with a slate under his arm and his coat buttoned wrong.

“I said before evening,” Dace said. “It is not evening.”

“It is not. I am early, and I am sorry. I am here because the water is over the low turn and I have measured an undercut at the north end, and I have a claim written out that I think you should hear before somebody else says it to you badly.”

Dace came out into it and read the slate.

He read it slowly, twice, and his mouth moved on the two sentences Tomas had put in about what he could not tell them.

“You have put in the parts you do not know,” Dace said.

“Yes.”

“Nobody puts those in.”

“They are the only parts of it I can stand behind.”

The bridge keeper looked at him for a moment with something that was not quite respect and not quite amusement. “Then here is my answer, and I will give it to you before the court and not only at the court. I will walk the west road from the northern wall to the bridge, above the low section, and I will report what I find to the people I find on it, in the words you read me, and I will add two things of my own. I will say whether the north end has moved again since your chalk. And I will say that I have not been on the low section, because it is under, and a man who has not been on a thing should not be reported as standing on it.”

“That is more than I asked for.”

“It is less than I can afford, and it is what I can afford.” He took the slate. “And I will do it at the second hour after noon, when the light is best, and not at eleven, because at eleven I will be tempted to hurry and hurry is how men fall in.”

He carried the slate off up the hill toward the low ward, and Tomas let him go, and stood in the road in the rain with the particular feeling of a man who has been given less help than he asked for and has had to work out that it is enough.

Wenna Cray was at the bread stall with her own slate, and she had been waiting for him since the sixth hour, which he understood she had done on purpose.

“Read it to me as you read it to the others,” she said, “and do not leave out the part about the road, because I have already decided what I am going to say about it.”

He read it.

She listened with her head down and her hands flat on the slate.

“Your cart at the culvert is in there twice,” she said. “Once in the water line and once in the part where the road gang stands about swearing at the weather. I want my sentence to carry that. Not as a conclusion. As a fact a man put his hand on.”

“It is a fact a man can put a hand on. The foreman signed for it.”

“Then I will carry it with that in it, and I will carry it to the ward, and if anybody asks me whether the Crown caused the water I will say what I can support: that the Crown's own gang put a stone across the drain on a Crown order and that the water came up behind it. I will not say the Crown ordered the flood. I will not say it is somebody's fault. I will say the cart is there and the water came behind the cart, and I will say it in front of witnesses, and if a man wants to shout at the Crown about it he can shout at the Crown about a cart he has touched with his own hand.”

That was an answer with a correction in it, and Tomas understood as she said it that a person who attached a condition to a warning was not being difficult. She was being a second keeper.

“Can you say it in the ward?” he asked. “Out loud, to people who have not asked?”

“I will say it at the ward board and to the people who come to the board, and to the two doors I have carried things to for nine years. I will not go up past the landing. I will not shout it in the lane. And I will not say that anyone has been told to do anything, because you have not told anybody, and if anybody comes to me and says the bellmaker ordered them out of their cellars, I will tell them that is not what was said, and I will be the one who says it, and I will be in trouble for it, and I am telling you that now so that you know the cost before you use me.”

He wrote the cost in the day-book on the spot, in her own sentence, because she had told him to know it before he used her and the least he could do was record that he had been told.

By the eleventh hour the ward had begun to move without anybody ringing anything. Crane's fish were already on the high boards from the day before, so the first family to go was a family with a churn and a pig, who put both on a handcart and went up to the millrace hard standing and came back wet to the knees. The second family took nothing, and said so, and would not be moved. An old man with a barrow of nothing at all stood in the lane for an hour deciding, and then went and sat with his sister, which was a decision of a kind, and a good one, and not one Tomas would have chosen.

At the second hour after noon Wyman Dace walked the west road and did the work he had agreed to do, and came back with a slate half full.

The undercut had moved another two inches and had taken the fish fence with it. The boundary stone had turned. And at the span where the rod had gone through, the water was eight inches deeper than it had been at six in the morning.

“It will go in the night,” Dace said. “Not at noon. It is coming down off the ridge behind the low ward and the ground is already full. I am not a river man and I am not going to tell you when. I will tell you that by dark the north end is a bad place to be, and that the bridge itself is sound and I have looked at the two piers and the third stone, and that the water at the bridge is a foot and a half and rising and I would not put a loaded cart over it after dark.”

He read his report to nine people on the lane, in Tomas's words, with the two additions of his own, and the additions were the part that made it his.

Afterwards Tomas sat down on the low wall by the gate, and found that the day had taken something out of him that he could not name and would not have believed an hour before. The lane was crowded — the ward had come out of its houses to stand in the water and look at the water, which is what people do when there is nothing else to look at — and the sound of thirty people talking over running water put a pressure behind his right ear that had nothing to do with a bell and everything to do with being in the middle of a thing he could not hold. He stood up to get away from it and his balance went, and he sat down again on the wet stones before it took him.

“Vale,” Jerm said, from somewhere above him. “Stay where you are. That is an order and I am the man who can give it.”

“I am all right.”

“You are grey and you have been holding that rod for six hours.” The watchman crouched. “I have seen this in a bellmaker before. Your ears will be singing and the market will sound like it is under the floor. It passes. Do not stand up in a hurry and do not be tall.”

It passed, mostly, by the middle of the afternoon. Tomas sat with his back to a dry wall and wrote up what he had measured, and let two children who wanted to watch the water argue about it in a doorway above him.

At the sixth hour Renn read his mark.

He went down on one knee in front of the sill, which he had done perhaps a thousand times, and put two fingers in the water and held them against the stone for the length of a slow count, and stood up.

“Third mark,” he said. “Above it by a finger. The gate is clear and it turns. I am ringing, and I am saying four words, and there is nothing else.”

He took the windlass bar and lifted. The gate came up out of the slot with a grinding that people on the lane stopped talking to hear, and the low water of the Low Ward's drainage went out through it in a brown tongue, and Renn stood with his shoulder against the bar and shouted four words over the noise.

“The water is above the third mark.”

He did not say the road. He did not say the cart, or the undercut, or the Crown, or a single thing about anybody's house. He rang, and the sound went along the row and stopped, and about a dozen people heard it, and it was the truth and it was not enough, and Tomas watched three families look at each other and start to move anyway because they had seen the water and did not need a bell to be frightened.

It took another two hours for the gate to make a real difference, and by then the low lane was a canal, and the four houses at the bottom of the ward had water in their ground floors to the height of a table, and the chandler had lost the rest of his flour.

At dusk the Crown officer who had written in his book at the market court found Bevin Sarr standing at the low turn with two other men and a crowbar, arguing about a stone cart.

“You are not lifting that,” the officer said.

“I am not touching it,” Sarr said. “I am standing here asking you to, in front of eleven people, because your gang put it there and your deputy is three streets away and he has not come, and the water is up to the door sills of four houses. I am not doing anything. I am asking.”

The officer looked at the water, and at the eleven people, and at the road gang's foreman standing in it with his hip full of paper.

“The works order holds the cart until the frames are stood,” the officer said. “I cannot release a works order at a road junction in a flood. If you lift that stone, the frames go down, and then you have a hole, and then this is a Crown failure in a public record instead of a Crown obstruction in a mud bank.”

“That is an answer,” Sarr said, “and I will say that you gave it to me in front of these eleven, and I will say that you said the word *Crown* twice and the word *cart* once, and I will say it tomorrow at the court, and you may write it down now while your hand is warm.”

The officer wrote it down.

At dusk Tomas walked back up the hill with the rod over his shoulder and the day's work in his head, and the count in his day-book, which was the part he would have to publish.

Four households moved goods. Two waited and lost a day. One old man moved himself to a sister and called it a decision. Four houses at the foot of the ward took water. The chandler lost his flour. The millrace lost a morning's grinding. The market lane was shut, so six stalls lost the day's trade, and the market court would have to pay them out of a fund that had three weeks in it. One household's pig was drowned in a cart and the man who owned it stood at the water's edge and would not be comforted by anybody's sentence, including Tomas's.

And the three answers he had been given that day were these, and none of them was the same as another.

Wyman Dace would walk the west road and report in Tomas's words with two of his own added.

Wenna Cray would carry the claim only with the cart at the culvert stated in it, and only as far as the ward board and two doors, and only with the cost of saying it named in advance.

Hollis Renn would carry nothing of anybody's, and would ring four words at his own gate about his own mark, and would not be moved to do more.

Tomas stood in the Bellhouse yard in the dark with the trial bell under its cloth and the day's three answers on the counter, and understood two things in the dark that he had not understood in the light.

The first was that none of the three had said yes. Dace had said yes to a road. Cray had said yes to a sentence with a correction in it and a boundary round it. Renn had said no to both of them and yes to a windlass. A count of three had been assembled out of three men who would each have been astonished to hear that they had agreed with each other.

The second was that he had no business assuming the same three would be in the room. Cray had already said she would only speak where she chose. Renn had said he was a gate and not a court. And a man who builds a public signal on the assumption that yesterday's keepers will turn up tomorrow has built a signal on his own convenience, and the Crown does exactly that every season and calls it a roll.

He had the claim, and he would have the carrier the moment the clerk brought him the words. What he did not have was any idea how to make a bell carry three positions at once without quietly turning them into one, and no assurance at all that the three positions would be the ones he had collected. This was not a problem a maker could solve with better metal, and the town would not be doing him the favor of waiting while he found it out.
