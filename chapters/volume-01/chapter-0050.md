# Chapter 0050 — Before Dawn

The four days between the twenty-eighth and the thirty-third are not written in the order they happened and they are all true, and Tomas Vale wrote them up in one sitting in a room over the market court on the thirty-second day, in a page of paragraphs, with the hours against each, and it is the last page of his day-book that anybody will read in Kells, and it is the reason he was in that room.

**The twenty-ninth.** He was taken down the north road to Fourmile at about the eleventh hour of the night in a Crown cart with the oiled linen on his knees, and there is a Crown district building on that road with a lock on the door and a copyist in it, and he was put in a room there and given a copy of his own arrest record, made by the watch captain of Kells in the watch captain’s own hand, and it is the only copy in the world and he has read it about forty times. **The thirtieth.** Absalom Teage, Mrs Wilde, Louisa Tace, Wenna Cray, Ivo Prentice and Gib Hask were before the district, and Mrs Wilde was released on the thirtieth, a day before the rest of them, because the district could not hold a household of record upon a line in a witness column for a second day longer without a witness, and Surveyor Collis refused to certify her line again, in a room, in a district building, in a real town, and the reason she gave was written down and is in her return. **The thirty-first.** Five people were released without charge, all of them together, in about nine minutes, on a single sheet, on the ground that the district records that no act of violence was committed by any person in the ward of Kells upon any Crown officer in that ward, and that the bells of the ward are not accounted for by any person, and that where a person is not charged with an offence there is nothing to detain them on. **He was released on the thirty-first at about the tenth hour and was in Kells by the second day of the month at the second hour, and there is no condition on his release and no finding against him and no charge, and the sheet that released him is in his coat, and it is in Deputy Reeve Crake’s own hand, and that is the whole of the Crown’s contribution to the last four days of this month.**

**Crake was relieved of the Kells commission on the thirtieth** and the reason is on a form in a building in Fourmile, and the reason is nine words long and the nine words are: *That the officer recorded disorder and co-operation on one paper.* He had put the two lines of a two-line form side by side and filed a district finding and a commission man’s opinion on the same sheet, and the district had wanted the first line and had got the second, and a man who does that is a man who has decided the office is wrong, and an office does not keep a man like that in a ward of four hundred and eleven. He was moved to a works office on the western road and it is not a punishment anybody in Kells will ever know about, because nothing about it was published, and Tomas Vale found it out on the thirty-second day from a Crown carter who had heard it at Fourmile, and wrote it in the day-book in one line, and underlined it, and did not write anything about what he thought of it.

**The Crown cart came off the low-turn culvert on the thirty-first** at about the eleventh hour, a month after the works order for its removal, and Gib Hask was at the low end of the turn for most of that month with six men and a bar, and the last of the ninety feet of backfill went back in on the thirty-second under a Crown works order signed by a foreman who had been signing them for nineteen years, and Brann’s men put it in and did not compact it and the district did not check. **Foreman Brann wrote in the gang’s book on the thirty-second, in his own hand, the thing he had said on the fifteenth of this month he would find out**: that the ground part of the plan that came up with the works order of the eighth was not drawn by the road office, and did not come off a road sheet, and did not come off a survey, and came up with the works order in a folded third sheet in a hand that is not a road hand and not Brann’s and not Perry’s, and that the sheet says at the foot *from the district* in a different ink again, and that Brann does not know what else is on it and cannot ask, because the sheet is Crown property and has gone to Fourmile. The gang’s book is the only record in Kells of what happened in the low lane on the fifteenth of this month. **The entries for the nineteenth are water-damaged and illegible and remain so, and the word *illegible* is in them in Brann’s own hand with his initials, and nobody has ever been punished for it.**

**On the thirtieth, Nell Fenn spent it.** She wrote to the district renewal office at Fourmile, in her own hand, with the hour on it, and she sent it by the north post and it went on a rider’s bag, and the question was in the form that four hundred and eleven people have spent a month learning to put, and it was this: that the schedule of responses for route L-4 carries nine lines in the response column reading *not returned* and six reading *accompanying*, and that both are notes about persons who were not in a house when the office decided what was in it, and that any person in this ward may therefore ask, at the district office, of any one of those fifteen lines by number: whose non-return is noted, whose accompanying is noted, and on what date the note was made, and that the answer is to be written in the officer’s own name and given to the person who asked. She had made the count on the twelfth day of this month in a lane with a charcoal and had told two people and had kept it for nineteen days. She told Mercy Slee on the evening of the thirtieth that she had spent it and that she had written the date on the twenty-third in her own book for a day after the twenty-eighth, and Mercy Slee asked her whether she could account for the spending of it, and Nell Fenn said: *No. And I have been trying since the fourth hour and I cannot, and I want it written down that I have been carrying a thing for nineteen days and I have got about four hours of use out of it and I do not know what the other fifteen days were for, and I am not going to tell anybody they were discipline.*

**The district replied on the thirty-second.** It is two paragraphs. The first is the correct answer to a correct question: that the office will answer, in writing, in the officer’s own name, any question put by a person concerning a line on that schedule, and that the office has no objection and has never had an objection to a person asking about his own line, and that the office regrets that it has in the past described the whole of a schedule as non-response. The second paragraph is this: *That the respondent of the thirty-first is a person of the age of sixteen, of the household of line twenty-two, and the office notes that line twenty-two is one of the fifteen lines to which the question refers, and the office notes that a person may put a question about any line and that the office does not propose to inquire into why a person has asked about a line in particular.*

So there is a Crown document in Fourmile that says that a girl of sixteen on line twenty-two asked about her own line and that the Crown is not asking why. It is the most careful piece of writing the district has produced this month, and it is the only one that has frightened anybody, and it is on the ward board at Kells with a drawing pin through a drawing pin, because the pinned schedule is still on that board, and nobody has taken it down, and the Crown’s own paper is pinned beside it.

Nobody in Kells has an answer to the question about the fifteen lines yet. The district has said it will answer, line by line, on request, and there are fifteen lines, and the ward has not yet asked, because a household has to ask about its own line and no household on any of the fifteen has come to the board, and that is on the thirty-third day of the month and it is not a thing Tomas Vale can help with and he has written that in his day-book in four words, which are: *Nobody has come. Not my business.*

And the ward is smaller. Nine households left on the twenty-eighth and have not all come back, and five of the six who were taken are back in their houses and one is not and nobody has said why, and the Cafferys and the Oakes have not come back, and there is a Crown notice still nailed to that board that says the practice of the ward board and the market court originated in the Vale Bellhouse and that the commission will consider the standing of an originating workshop, and the shop has been sealed for five days and nobody has taken that notice down.

---

He was in the watch house at about the third hour of the morning of the thirty-third with his coat on, because Captain Pike had sent for him at the second hour with a note in his own hand, four words long, and the four words were: *Custody review. Bring nothing.*

*Custody review.* Not a hearing. Not an inquiry. Not a piece of the district’s practice with a fifth schedule behind it. A **custody review**, which is the ordinary name for the ordinary thing an office does when an object has been in a place that the office has since closed, and which happens four times a year in a district of this size, and which is in the ward for the first time in its history on the morning of the thirty-third day of this month.

The reason it happened now, and Oren Pike had it in writing and read it out in the watch house with the lamp on, is the fourth line of the district’s own return of the twenty-fifth day, and the line that says: *That the register of bells for this ward will be read at the district renewal office at Fourmile, and that the officer of that office responsible for the reading of returns upon route L-4 is District Officer Marten Cope, and that any person having matter touching the register may address him at that office.*

District Officer Marten Cope wrote to the watch of Kells on the thirty-second, and it is four lines long, and it is the only letter that has come into Kells this month from an office that was not angry:

*That the district has read the register of the seventeenth to the twenty-fourth days, which was returned to this office on the twenty-seventh day, and that the name column of the page for the nineteenth day is filled with the word* none *twelve times, in a clerk’s hand, each entry initialled.*

*That the district asked for the names and the watch declined, and the district noted the refusal, and the district has now had some days with a page of twelve bells and no names on it, and has discovered that it cannot answer a question it did not know it was asking, which is: whether any bell in this ward is joined to any line, wire or ground, and if so by what.*

*That there is in the watch store of this ward an object which is Crown property, being a fixed stripped survey carrier in its mounting frame, and that the joint receipt on its file expired at the fourth hour of the tenth day of this month, and that there has been no custody review, and that a district cannot leave an instrument of the Crown in a watch store in a ward under its own declaration for a fortnight and then send a rider with a spade.*

*And that the district therefore requires a custody review, at the watch house of Kells, at the third hour of the morning of the thirty-third day of this month, and requires that the object be produced, and requires that the watch produce any other instrument in this ward whose manner of working is recorded, and that if there is none, the district will enter that there is none.*

Oren put the letter down on the table.

“He has given me a review,” he said, “and he has given me the first honest reason anybody in this county has had for wanting the object touched, and it is the reason I asked you for on the third day of this month.”

“Do you remember the reason.”

“You asked me what I wanted and I told you that I had arranged in the first week for the courier’s handbell to be put beside the Crown carrier and read, and that I had wanted it done before the twelfth day, and that you would not let me do it then because the carrier’s receipt was Crown property and the joint receipt had four days to run.” Oren was not looking at him. “It is the last chance and it is the only reason anybody in this town will ever turn that frame round and look at the inside of it, and the reason is a clerk in Fourmile who has been asked a question he cannot answer.”

---

They came at the third hour: Officer Rudd, who was still in the commission, and Surveyor Collis, who had been recalled from the district by a rider and had arrived on the twenty-ninth and had been waiting two days with the professional patience of a woman who has read a good deal and cannot prove any of it, and Ferrow the market court clerk, who was there because Ferrow is always there, and Tomas Vale, and Jerm, and — at the last, in the rain, with a lamp and a cloth and a bag — Tamsin Rook.

And a Crown copyist with a book.

Nobody rang anything. It is on the page, in Jerm’s hand, with the hour, that no bell was sounded at the custody review of the thirty-third of this month, and that the question of whether the courier’s handbell is joined to any line had been answered by a man reading the wear on it, and that the question of whether the fixed carrier is joined to any line had been answered by a Crown surveyor and a Crown file and a document that had been in Fourmile since the first week of the month.

Tamsin Rook unwrapped her bell herself and set it on the cloth, which is what she did on the sixth day of this month in this same yard’s yard, and Tomas Vale read the wear from a distance and did not touch it, and that was the arrangement and she made it in about four seconds and nobody argued with her.

What the reading showed is this.

The bell is hand-sized, no wider than her palm across the crown, and it is her own and it is stolen and it was stolen off a route. The wax in the crown of it was laid over an older signal by the man who paid for it, and the wax is that man’s business and has been since before this month, and it is a fact about metal and is not evidence that the Low Ward agreed to anything. **The other alteration is in the metal.** The crown of that bell has been re-cut. Somebody took a handbell that was made to carry a certain distance and took a chisel to the inside of the crown and took the wall down about a sixteenth of an inch all the way round, which raises the note and widens it and makes the bell carry further than the man who made it intended, and there is a file mark on the inside of the crown at four points where the work stopped and the tool was turned, and the mark is a **riffle file**, and a riffle file is not a common file and is in the case of every working bellwright in this county and about nine other counties, and the file is cut the same way in all of them.

And the fixed stripped survey carrier is in its frame, in the cradle, with the red watch seal on the lower brace and the retaining bolts still in the loose state they have been in since the tenth day of this month, and it is waist-high and it has no clapper, and its lip is warped, and a child-sized wax handprint is in the crown of it, and across a coupling that has been cut nearly through there is a thin copper jumper, and the jumper is a Crown issue repair and the district’s own file has the service ticket that says so, in the district’s own hand, and it says the clapper was removed and the carrier disconnected after a warning was filed, and the route held for maintenance review, and no keeper answer recorded.

And that ticket also says, in the second line, the thing that has been on a table in this ward since the second week of the month: *answered in office, L-4 ward roll, per local practice.* And the office mark on that line is **OFF. 411**, and it is the same mark as on the schedule, and on the culvert works order, and on the inspection order, and it is a mark on four papers in four hands, and it is a district renewal desk and not a man, and there is no person in it, and there is not going to be one this month.

“Both of these,” Collis said, “at two removes from a Crown pattern, were altered by a person who had a bench and a set of files and access to a Crown pattern. One of them was made to carry further. One of them was made to carry nothing. Both alterations are in the metal, and both are ordinary work, and both leave no name, and the file mark on the courier’s bell is the same cut as the file mark on the survey carrier’s crown, and I have compared them.”

She had compared them, and she had brought the carrier’s crown out of the frame on a bar, with two Crown men and the copyist, at about the fourth hour of the morning, and the seal was broken and re-made in front of four witnesses, and it is all entered.

“Then the comparison shows a technique,” Rudd said.

“The comparison shows a technique,” Collis said, “and a bench, and a trade, and a Crown pattern, and about a year between the two. It shows a person. It does not show me which person, and I have not been asked to find out, and I could not find out from a bell in a cradle.”

She wrote the district’s finding into her own return on the morning of the thirty-third and the district filed it on the same day, and it is the last document of this month, and it is the only one that is entirely favourable to everybody, and it cost the Crown nothing:

*That neither the fixed stripped survey carrier in the watch store of Kells nor any other instrument produced at this review is joined to any line, wire or ground; that the fixed carrier is a mounted object with a jumper across a cut coupling, and is joined to nothing; that the finding of the district of the twenty-eighth day, that the bells of this ward are not accounted for by any person, is withdrawn; that the register of the watch of the seventeenth to the twenty-fourth days is produced and is in the district’s own hand, and is a record of bells and hours and no names, and the district makes no finding against any person upon it; and that the district records that the watch declined the question put to it on the twenty-fourth day and that the district has now had the answer and that the answer is in the watch’s own book, and that the district makes no finding against the watch.*

Then the release.

There were three claimants to the fixed stripped survey carrier and there is a Crown file with a receipt on it that expired at the fourth hour of the tenth day of this month. The Crown could not take it back, because the district had closed the survey store in Kells in the second week of the month and the object’s holding place was the watch store of a ward under a declaration, and a district does not put a Crown instrument in a locked building it has declared unsafe. The watch could not keep it, because the watch store is Crown property and the ward is sealed and the watch has no building and no wall and no forge and a bell in a cradle is a thing a watch captain cannot answer for in a room in a year.

There was one other holder on the face of the file, and he was standing in the room.

“Vale,” Oren Pike said. “I am releasing to you the object in that frame, in that frame, in that cradle, in the state it is in, under a receipt, with four things entered, and I am going to read them out and you are going to say no to any of them you do not agree to and then you are going to say yes to the rest, and that is a transaction and not a gift, and I have had eleven years to learn the difference and I am not going to be a man who hands a bell to a friend.”

He read them out.

“**One: that the object is released as the same object, in its mounting frame, and the retaining bolts are released only for transport and are to be made fast again at every place the frame is set down.** It is not to be rung. It has no clapper and it is not to be furnished with one by any person, and if a clapper is put in it, the person who puts it in has broken the receipt.

“**Two: that the frame goes into its reinforced padded cradle for the carriage and stays in it, and that a frame out of its cradle on a road is a frame broken.**

“**Three: that the object is produced at the request of the district, or of the watch, or of the market court, and at no other time and for no other purpose, and that a request may be made by any person and not only by an officer.**

“**And four: that the maker is not the keeper of it, and has no claim in it, and cannot certify it, and that the Bellwrights’ Measure has found on the twenty-sixth day that its origin cannot be traced, and that the man who holds it is the man who was asked to hold it and is not the man who made it and is not a man who may say what it means.**”

Tomas Vale said no to nothing and yes to all four, and then he made the clerk add a fifth, which took four minutes and which is the only thing he asked for in the whole of the last four days.

“**Five: that this receipt does not make the holder a keeper of any route, and that no answer given by any person to any signal carried on or by this object may be entered by anybody, in any book, as the answer of a household of this ward; and that the holder has no standing to speak for any household in Kells or in any ward; and that the ward’s records are the ward’s.**”

Ferrow read it back and Rudd initialled it and the copyist entered it and Collis witnessed it, and Captain Oren Pike signed it, in his own hand, at about the fifth hour of the morning of the thirty-third day of this month, and gave it to a bellmaker, and that is the last official act of Oren Pike’s eleven years in the chair of the watch of Kells.

Tamsin Rook’s arrangement was on the counter in a hand that was not his and not hers.

Mercy Slee had written it on the evening of the thirty-first at a hundred and nineteen with a slate, and she had given a copy to each of them separately, in a different room, in different words, and neither of them had seen the other read it, and it is four lines long and it has no preamble and it is on a slate because she said paper was Crown property this month and a slate is not.

*That she travels with him, on her own account, as far as he goes, and that she may stop at any hour without giving a reason and nothing happens to her for it, and that he does not decide when she stops and is not asked to.*

*That her route is hers and her bell is hers, and the four terms of the thirteenth day of this month stand and are hers and are not varied by him, and that no bell of his carries her and no bell of hers carries his.*

*That neither of them uses the other’s road without asking first, and that an answer may be no, and that the person who asked may not ask again that day. Which is a leash on both of them, and she wrote the word* leash *on it herself, and underlined it, and said to each of them in turn, separately, in the same words: I have put a leash on you and on him and I am not going to pretend I did not, and if either of you takes yours off in the first week I will know, because I have had a house on a Crown return for a month and I know what it looks like when a person stops answering.*

*And that nothing in this is a promise and nothing in it can be enforced by anybody, and that it is written down because a thing that is only in two people’s mouths is a rumour, and because I have watched this ward for twenty-one days find out what happens to a sentence that somebody has to remember.*

That is the whole of it, and neither of them wrote it first, and the person who wrote it is a woman of about forty with a boy of nine who is on no roll and who has not asked for anything and has not been thanked.

He read it on the thirty-second day in a room over the market court and she read it on the thirty-first at a hundred and nineteen, and on the morning of the thirty-third, in a lane, in the rain, at about the fifth hour, walking up past the washhouse turn, she said out loud the thing that is not on the slate, to nobody in particular and to him:

“A warning does not require me to obey you.”

“I know.”

“I have not said that to you in twenty-one days and I want it said now, before there is a road and a frame and a thing to carry, so that it is a thing said in a lane in the rain and not a thing found out about in a lane. I will carry my own bell on my own road for my own reasons and I will not carry yours, and when you ring a thing in a town with no keeper in it I will not be obliged to move, and if a child is in front of me I will do what I do, and you will not be told about it, and that is not a punishment and it is not a discipline and it is the arrangement, and I would like you to say it back to me in your own words so that I know it has gone in.”

“It has gone in.”

“Say it.”

“A warning does not require you to obey me. I will tell you what I know and I will not tell you what to do about it, and I will not ask you, and I will not be able to tell you where it was heard.”

“Right,” said Tamsin Rook, and shouldered her bag. “Then we will go before the light, because the light is a thing that makes a road visible and I do not want a road visible.”

And then she stopped in the lane, ten yards further on, under the eave of the washhouse turn where the rain does not get in, and took a folded leaf of paper out of the inside of her coat where it had been for nine days, and gave it to him.

It was the request. It was a leaf off the back of a day-book with a fold down the middle, and it had a heading and three numbered lines and a witness’s name at the bottom in Jerm’s hand with the hour of the twenty-fourth against it, and it had been folded in four and carried in a coat through a district building at Fourmile and back to this lane and it was soft at the folds and it had never been opened on the side that mattered.

“There is your paper,” she said, “and there is one line on the outside of it in my hand and you may read it and you will not ask me for anything else, and if you ask me for anything else I will go up this towpath on my own and you will find out where I went in about a week.”

He turned it over. There was one line on the outside of the fold, and it was in her own hand, and it said:

*No. And I am not going to give you a reason, and you are not going to have one, and you wrote that down yourself on the twenty-fourth and I have held you to it for nine days, and I want it in your book that you did not press me once.*

“That is all of it,” Tamsin Rook said. “That is the whole of my answer and I gave it inside forty minutes of you going up the hill and I did not tell you, and I have not told you for nine days on purpose, because you would have planned a morning with it. You said on the twenty-fourth that you could not know in advance and that you were not going to find out from anybody but me, and I have found out how that feels to be on this side of it, and I did not enjoy it, and I would not have missed one hour of it, and there is a bell in this county that knows exactly how that feels and it is too bright and it repeats in a corner.”

He wrote it down in his day-book standing at the top of the towpath in the rain, with the hour against it, and Jerm initialled it as the witness to the handing over, and the leaf is in the day-book and a copy is in the gate book of the watch of Kells, and both of them have the hour on them, and the ward’s book has the same fact in three hands because Mercy Slee entered it without being asked, which is what she does.

“Then what is this,” he said, holding up the slate in a lane in the rain. “Because this is not a no.”

“No it is not,” she said. “That slate is not about your bell and it is not about your warning and it is not about your route and it is not about me on your account. That slate is two people who have been leashed in this town for a month and who are going to be leashed a good deal further. It is a different document and it is mine and Mercy Slee wrote it and neither of us wrote it first and I have read it and you have read it and we are both carrying a copy.

“I said no to the paper in your coat and yes to the slate in my bag, on the same day, within an hour of each other, and neither answer cost the other one anything, and that is the first time in a month that two things happened in this ward that were not the same thing. I would like somebody to write that down eventually, and I am not going to write it down myself, and if you write it down you will do it in four words and they will be wrong.”

---

He got the frame out of the watch store at about half past five and it went down the lane on a barrow with Jerm on the front of it, and the padded cradle was on it, and the red watch seal was cut at the store door in front of four witnesses and the receipt says so.

The Vale Bellhouse sign hangs crooked over a locked door. It has hung crooked since the eleventh day of this month, when a Crown officer and a lad with a ladder had put it up level and a man from the Bellwrights’ Measure, who had no business there and had come on his own account, had stood in the lane and looked at it for a while and said it was crooked and would come down in the wind, and the Crown officer had asked whether a licensed man had any business in this lane, and the licensed man had said no and had not left, and the officer had not made him, and that is a story four people in this lane tell and none of them know all of it.

The wax on the door is the district’s wax with the district’s number on it, and the watch’s mark is under the wax, and nobody may take it off.

Tomas Vale put the barrow down on the towpath at the top of the lane at about twenty to six in the morning, and the millrace was making its noise, and the water was out of the low gate, and the gate had come up on the twenty-eighth and had stayed up, and a Crown cart was not across the culvert any more, and a child of about nine was walking down the lane to the landing with a bag in his hand because a Crown document said he was going to Fourmile on the Friday and it was the Friday, and nobody in Kells had anything to say to him about it, and he went down the lane at his own pace and did not look at a barrow with a bell in a frame on it.

He wrote the last four entries in his day-book standing at the top of the towpath, and they are the last thing in it, and there are four of them and they are the whole of what he takes out of this town.

*Thirty-third day. Left before dawn, with the fixed stripped survey carrier in its mounting frame, bolts made fast for transport, the frame in its reinforced padded cradle, and a receipt in four clauses and a fifth. I have no metal of my own left, no shop, no licence in a workshop, no bell I may ring, and one object in a cradle that I did not make and cannot trace and am not the keeper of and may not speak for. I did not make it and I am carrying it and I want that written in the order it happened.*

*What I have asked. I have asked this ward for a piece of its own iron and got it. I have asked four people for a duty and two said yes and two said no and I did not go looking for a fifth. I have asked a woman for something in writing in front of a witness and it was refused in advance in terms I wrote down myself, and I did not press, and the answer is hers and is not on a page I keep.*

*What I got. One child of nine stayed in his own house this week because his father filled in a Crown box in his own hand six days before he heard anything. Thirty-one children off a landing in forty minutes. Nine households moved, twenty-one stayed, eleven children in eight places, and four doors said no, and four columns, and nobody merged them, and none of that was me. A woman of sixty-five got a surveyor by putting her hand up in a room. A carter’s question at nineteen doors. A woman with a slate who had a count for nineteen days and spent it in four.*

*And a question I am carrying out of this town and cannot answer, and I am setting it down in the words I have and not in better words.*

And the question is this, and it is the last page of the day-book of a bellmaker of Kells, and it is the question this month ends on and does not answer:

*There is a row under the last rule of a Crown schedule, on a sheet pinned to a ward board through a drawing pin through a drawing pin since the ninth day of this month, and the first field is L-4, and the persons field is empty, and the response column carries a mark that four papers in four hands call OFF. 411, and the row is dated nine days before the carrier rang, which is six days before the inspection order it answers, and I have had it in front of a hundred and forty people in a market court this month and nobody in the district has ever said what it is for.*

*A file in a district office said that a bell was answered in office, and the L-4 ward roll, per local practice, and there is no person in a room in that line, and a filing clerk told a watch captain on the ninth day of this month that he has been merging divided answers into single ones for eleven years, and that a ward that cannot make up its mind is read as a ward that will not answer, and he said it in a room with the door shut and would not write it down, and I did not write it down either, and it is the only sentence anybody has said to me this month that I have wanted to keep.*

*So: the answers on the schedule were not the households’ answers. They were entered in an office, from a mark, under a rule, nine days before the round they describe, and then a page came back into a ward and a man read it as the answer of a town, and three children went up the steps of a wagon on the tenth of this month and a child of ten went up two steps of a wagon on the twenty-first, and every single one of those was correct, and every single one of those was lawful, and not one person in this county has been found who decided to do it.*

*There was a person. There has to have been a person, or a room where a person sat and chose the office mark and chose the date and chose the rule that the mark stands for the answer, and that person or that room is not in any of the four books in this town, and is not a mark, and is not a district renewal desk, and is somebody in Sennet, and they are not finished, because OFF. 411 is a mark on a line and a mark is made by a hand, and the hand is not in the file, and it may be a hand that thinks it is doing what it is doing.*

*And the other half, which I have carried for three weeks and cannot put down, and which is worse: nine lines on that schedule say* not returned *and six say* accompanying, and every household a child was taken from in this ward is on one of those fifteen lines, and not one of the other marked doors is, and a girl of sixteen has spent that count, and a district in Fourmile has answered in two days that it will answer, line by line, in an officer’s own hand, and has also written down that a person of sixteen at line twenty-two asked a question about her own line and that the office does not propose to ask why.*

*So the names are on the schedule. Not the children. The names. And the question is not who wrote OFF. 411 on a rule nine days early. The question is where a name goes when an office has entered it in a column called* accompanying, *and has nobody in the house, and has the word in a book, and there is a building in Sennet where the Crown’s own answers are kept.*

*Who taught a Crown office to turn one clerk’s answer into a whole settlement’s consent — and where are the names being sent when they are registered?*

*Two questions. I have had them for three weeks and I cannot answer either one and I am carrying them out of this town with an object in a cradle that I cannot ring, and a ward board with a pin in it, and a woman on a towpath who does not have to obey me, and a child of nine going to the landing with a bag.*

He shut the day-book and put it inside his coat, and started down the towpath.

The towpath runs north out of Kells along the quiet side of the water, and the first four miles of it are in this county and the fifth is not, and there is a village at about the second mile called Ocklebridge that is a river village of about two hundred persons and that has a landing stage and a smithy and a woman who keeps a bell for the water and who has not heard of the district’s answer of the twenty-fifth day.

The bell in the cradle came off the barrow at about the sixth hour and was not rung, because it has no clapper and it may not be furnished with one, and the bolts were made fast and the frame went down in the wet grass beside the water with the cradle under it and the day began.

And at about the seventh hour, at the top of the towpath where the lane leaves the water and goes up to the Kells road, Tamsin Rook stopped, and took the bell out of her bag, and looked at the river for a while, and then went back about sixty yards to a gate where a path comes down off the hill to the water, where there was a woman standing with a barrow.

Nobody had asked her.

That is the last true thing in this month’s book and it is written here in a way that a person may check. No person in this town in this whole month asked Tamsin Rook for a reason at any hour after the twenty-eighth day. Not Captain Pike, who had asked her for four on the twenty-first in public and was the last man in Kells to ask her anything. Not the board, which had a copy of the slate and a person who could say what the words meant. Not a Crown officer, who had a form in a satchel and four items on it. Not a girl of sixteen with a slate and a count. And not a bellmaker with a day-book and a question in it and a reason of his own for wanting to know, who did not ask, and wrote down in the yard on the twenty-eighth that he would not.

She rang it once.

It was a small bell, hand-sized, no wider than her palm across the crown, with wax in the crown laid over an older signal and the wall of the crown cut a sixteenth of an inch thin all the way round by somebody who had a riffle file and access to a Crown pattern, and it had not been rung in a public road since the fourth day of the month by the woman who owns it, and it went out over the water to about two hundred yards, and she could not hear it, because the note has been gone out of her left ear since the fourth day of this month and has not come back and is not coming back, and she is partly deaf for the rest of her life and has said so in a lane in front of a hundred people and priced it herself.

And she put the bell in her bag and sat down on the parapet of the low parapet at the top of the towpath, above the water, in the wet grass, in the rain that had not stopped since the twenty-eighth, and she waited.

She waited for a long time. Long enough that a man walking a barrow past her said *cold for it* and she said *it is*, and he did not ask what she was waiting for, because he was going to work.

The answer came at about twenty past seven, from the north, from the far side of the water and about a mile out, and it was a bell that is not hers and is not his and is in a village of about two hundred persons that has not yet heard of the district’s answer of the twenty-fifth day, and it rang four times, unhurried, the way a person rings a thing in a village at seven in the morning because somebody at a landing has asked them to and has named what they want repeated, which is *come down to the water*, and there is a woman at a village landing who has heard four bells in a market lane in the dark once this month from a man on a ferry road and has told the story of it four times and added four words to it that nobody said, and has a right to be slow about a bell.

Tamsin Rook did not ring again. She did not stand up and wave and she did not go and find out and she did not ask the man with the barrow what he thought, and when Tomas Vale came down the path with a frame in a cradle and stopped about ten feet short of her, she said, without turning round:

“That was Ocklebridge and that is a woman called Marta Vell who keeps the water bell and who is about sixty and who has rung it every morning for thirty years and who does not know there is a district.”

“Does she know about the order?”

“She knows there is an order. Everybody who uses a river knows there is an order. She does not know that her bell has been entered on a schedule in a building in Fourmile as a bell of this ward with no keeper against it, and she is not going to be told by me at seven in the morning on a towpath, and she is going to hear about it from whoever tells her, and that is her business and not mine.”

And then she stood up, and shouldered her bag, and looked at him, and gave him the only sentence she gave him that morning and it is the last sentence of the month:

“Do not tell me where to go.”

“I was not going to.”

“You were thinking about it,” Tamsin Rook said, “and I could see it, and I have been able to see it since about the eighth of this month, and I want it written down somewhere that you did it again this morning and that I said no before you asked and that no is still the answer and will be the answer on the second day of next month and on the first, and you may write that on the slate if you like, and Mercy will keep it, and it will be on a slate in a shop in Kells and not in either of our heads, and that is the whole of what a woman with one working ear and no bell of her own can ask for.”

And they went on up the towpath in the rain, the two of them, with a bell in a frame in a cradle on a barrow that neither of them could ring, past the washing stones and the low turn, and nobody at the top of the lane turned out, because it was before the light and the light had not come up over the western ridge yet, and behind them the Vale Bellhouse sign hung crooked over a locked door, and the wax on the door had a district number on it, and the watch captain’s mark was under the wax, and nobody in the world could take it off.
