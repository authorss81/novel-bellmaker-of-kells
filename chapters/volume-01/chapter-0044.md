# Chapter 0044 — The Name in the Margin

He found her at the parapet of the low drain gate at about the seventh hour of the evening, sitting where she had sat on the thirteenth day with the bell in her lap inside her bag, and the millrace making its noise on one side and the water coming out through the gate on the other, and the sky over the western ridge the colour it goes about this time when the light is going out of the high country and not yet into the water.

She had heard him coming. He knew she had, because she did not turn round, and because he had been walking down a stone path for four minutes and had said nothing at all and she is a woman who has spent eleven years knowing what is coming down a path at her.

“Four days,” Tomas said.

“You said four days and you meant four days, and it is the seventh hour now, and the twenty-fourth is the twenty-fourth, and I have been sitting here since the fourth hour because there is nothing on the towpath today and I have not had a paid run since the ninth.” She moved her bag along the parapet to make room and did not say anything else about it, which is a thing she does instead of saying something else.

He sat down. The stone was cold and wet on the top and dry underneath and he had a piece of chalk in his coat for marking water and he did not need it tonight.

“There are two things and the first one is about the nineteenth and I have never said it to you.”

“You have never said it to me in the days since the twenty-first, when you stood in a lane with a bell half a mile off and did not move,” Tamsin said. “That is different from never having said it to me. Go on.”

“I rang a bell four times in a market lane at twenty past eight on the nineteenth and I did not ask two keepers, I did not ask the ward, I did not ask the woman whose box it was, and I did not ask you, and it went further than I have any right to say it goes, and a carrier lost thirty-six pounds and a girl of nineteen has a wrist broken who was standing where I had written to her and asked her to stand. It is mine. I have said it in the lane and at the board and in my own book and I am saying it to you first because you are the one person in this town who has been inside the reach of it and everybody else has only heard about it.”

“How many times did you ring it?”

“Four.”

She turned her head then, and not far.

“I was at this gate,” she said, “at about the eighth hour, on the parapet, with my feet off the stones because the sill is wet. And I felt it come up through four feet of stone and I felt it arrive in the gate and I felt it in my two hands on the stone. And I heard the strike. I do not hear the note any more, you know that, and I have said it in a lane in front of a hundred people and I am not going to make you be careful with me about it. I heard a strike. I felt it in the gate. And I could not tell you how many times, because I cannot hear it, and because the note is the part that hangs on and the strike is the part that does not, and I have been carrying a bell for eleven years on the part that hangs on.”

“You could have felt four.”

“I could have felt four if I had been watching for four,” she said. “I was not watching for four. I was sitting on a wet parapet with nothing to do for the fifth day, and a bell I cannot hear went off in this ward, and the only part of it that reached me was the part that arrives through stone, and that part is the same for one strike and for four, and if you want to know the truth about it, I have been sitting here for an hour working out whether it was one or four, and I have decided that it does not matter, and I would like it recorded that I decided that.”

“It matters that you could not tell.”

“Yes,” she said. “That matters. That is the part I have been holding since the nineteenth and I have not had anywhere to put it, because there is nobody in this ward I can say it to without it becoming a thing about a courier who cannot hear, and I said the rest of it in a lane on the twenty-first on purpose, and I have not had anybody come up the towpath since and say one word about it, and you are the first, and you are not going to, and I am going to say thank you for that and it will be the only time I say it.”

They sat there while the water went out through the gate.

---

“Now the other thing,” he said, “and I am going to say it the way I would say it in a book, and you are going to tell me when I have got it wrong.

“I do not know what a bell is for. That is not true in the way it sounds. I know what a bell is for in a yard and on a bench. What I do not know is what it is for at four hundred yards when the person who rang it is standing in the middle of the lane with his hand on a leather and has no way of knowing whether the woman on a wet parapet has felt it once or four times, or whether a carter on the ferry road has heard it and understood three sentences of it and put four words of his own in the middle and gone inside.”

“You have just described your bell.”

“I have described what happened on the nineteenth.”

“No,” Tamsin said. “Listen to yourself. You have not described what happened on the nineteenth. You have described what you cannot do. And that is the first time in a month you have come to me with the second one instead of the first, and I want to mark it, and I want you to know that I have noticed it and that it is not forgiveness and it is not a settle, it is a mark, and I am putting it in my own book and you are not getting to read it.”

“Fine.”

“No, it is not fine, and you have come to the towpath to ask me for something and you have started with the thing that makes it hard for me to refuse you, and I do not know whether you did that on purpose or whether you have simply always been like this, and either way I am going to finish the sentence you did not finish.”

He waited.

“A signal that goes further than its keeper can govern,” Tamsin said, “is not a fault in the signal. It is the whole of what a signal is. A bellmaker’s bell is a mouth and it goes out of the mouth and it does not come back. A courier’s bell is a mouth and it goes out of the mouth and it does not come back. I have spent eleven years learning where my mouth stops and I could not find the edge from inside it, and I found that out on the thirteenth of this month by standing in my own reach with a slate and a man beside me, and the answer was that there is no edge, it thins, and a thing that thins instead of stopping cannot be governed by the person who is speaking.

“And you have just told me that on the Friday night, six hundred yards off, a man I have never met repeated four times what he thought he heard, and added four words that were in a Crown notice in a Crown building, and has been repeating it for five days, and that the four words are still in this ward and you cannot get them out, and that you rang four times in the dark to a lane full of people and could not stop it going further than you meant.

“That is my hearing. That is exactly my hearing. Mine went in a left ear over nine days at the start of this month and yours went out of a bell on the Friday night, and neither of us can hear where it went, and the two of us together have made a map of precisely the part of this that cannot be seen from either end.” She took her hands off the stone. “So here is what I am not going to do, and I want it clear that I have thought about it for two days and not five minutes. I am not going to fix your bell. I have no ear for it, no ear left, and there is nothing in the trade that puts an ear back. And you are not going to fix my hearing, and I am not going to let you try, and if you tell me in the next four days that you have looked into it I will go up the towpath and keep walking.”

“I have not looked into it.”

“I know you have not. That is why I said it in that order.” She turned the bag on the parapet so the strap was under her hand. “You rang a true thing four times into a lane and it went to a man on a road six hundred yards off and he made a version. I rang nothing at all and it went to a man on a road six hundred yards off and he made a version, and mine was a *true* sentence about my own rules that I said in a market court with a clerk present, and he made a version of it, and the version is still in this ward. Two bells, or one bell and no bell, and the same result, and neither of us is in a position to do anything about the other one’s end of it.

“So we go on.”

---

“You are going to ask me something,” Tamsin said, “and you have had a sheet of paper in your inside pocket for about a quarter of an hour and you have not taken it out, and I want to tell you that I have watched you decide to take it out four times and go back to the sentence about the reach, and that I would rather have the sheet.”

Tomas took it out. It was a leaf off the back of the day-book with a fold down the middle and it had a heading and three numbered lines and a place at the bottom for a witness’s name, and the heading was in his own hand and read: *Ask, and the answer, and the hour.*

“I am not going to read it to you,” he said. “I am going to give it to you, and Jerm is at the top of the towpath with a lamp, and I am going to ask him to put his name on the bottom of it as the witness to the asking and to the hour, and then you are going to take it, and you are not going to answer it tonight, and I am not going to ask you again, and if you answer it on the twenty-seventh I will take the answer you give and if you answer it on the twenty-eighth morning I will take that too, and I will not ask a second time in a lane or in a yard or in the middle of a night.”

She took the leaf and did not open it.

“Say the three lines to me and not the paper.”

He said them.

“One. The answer may be no. Two. No reason is required and none will be asked of you, and if you give one I will write it down and I will not argue with it and I will not use it. Three. I cannot know in advance which it will be and I have not asked you and I am not going to, and that is not a courtesy, it is the arrangement you made on the thirteenth day and I wrote it in my own book and I am not going to be the man who asks you to break it so that I can plan a morning.”

“And the request itself is where?”

“On the other side of the fold. It is a sheet, and it is in my own words, and I have not improved it since I wrote it at the fourth hour, and you may read all of it, and you may strike any part of it, and if you strike a part then that part is not asked.”

“What is in it?”

“Not on the towpath,” Tomas said. “That is not me being clever. It is that if I read the request to you in words, then the words become mine and are in the air, and you can accept or refuse the version of it you have just heard, and I want you to be refusing or accepting the sheet.”

Tamsin Rook put the leaf inside her jacket, against the bag, and did not open it there, and looked at the gate and the millrace for a while.

“There is a Crown man with a satchel at the second house past the toll shed,” she said. “He has a form and he is doing a second round because two have not been taken, and I have watched him go past twice this week, and I know what the form is because a woman at the washhouse read it out a couple of days ago and has been talking about it since, which means the whole of the towpath knows there is a box on a Crown sheet with a space for every child in a house in it and a space for who the child goes to. And I have been sitting on this parapet for five days working out what I am going to do about that, and I had decided that I was going to do nothing, because doing nothing is my trade and because I have no standing and because I have a bell I cannot hear and about nine coppers a month and a woman at 133 who has put her own name in a column and does not need me.”

“Right.”

“And I have not decided anything now,” she said. “I have got a folded piece of paper in my jacket and I am going to go up this towpath and I am going to go to my own room, and I am not going to read it tonight, and I will answer it when I answer it.”

“Jerm is up there.”

“I know Jerm is up there. That is why you brought it down here and not up there, and I am not going to say so to a man while I am annoyed with him.” She stood up and shouldered the bag. “Give me the lamp a minute.”

He gave her the lamp and she went up the first eleven steps of the towpath and stopped, and came back down four of them.

“The first line of your request is a copy of the first line of the thing I said on the parapet on the thirteenth,” she said. “*I will answer a request and refuse a request.* Yes. And I want you to know that I read that leaf in about a second and a half and I have not read the other side of it, and that I have not decided, and that I am telling you both of those things out loud, on a towpath, at night, because I have decided that if I do not say them out loud I will spend three days deciding whether to say them, and I have never in my life been able to do anything on a towpath in the dark except tell the truth, and that is the only skill I have left and I am not going to waste it being dignified.”

“I did not think you were being dignified.”

“You are going to be, for about a week,” she said, “and I am not going to watch it.”

She went up the towpath. She did not come back down it.

---

Jerm was at the gate with the lamp and the book, and his own name was on the bottom of the leaf already, and he wrote the hour against it at which the request was handed over, and read back what Tomas had said, and Tomas said it was fair.

“Will you be wanting the answer written,” Jerm said. “When she gives it.”

“No. She will give it however she gives it and I will write down how she gave it. Do not ask her anything when she gives it to you, and do not be standing near her when she does.”

“That is the whole of my part in this,” Jerm said, “and I have had it eleven times this month.”

“Yes.”

“It is a good part,” Jerm said, and shut the book.

---

Tomas went back up the hill at about the ninth hour with an empty inside pocket and the receipt of a custody he had held for fourteen days.

The yard door had the no-ringing notice and the Crown’s wax and Oren’s watch mark on it, and none of the three would come off without the other two. He unlocked it from the inside, which nobody had ever forbidden him to do, and shut it behind him, and put the lamp on the bench, and took out the one sheet of paper he had carried for two weeks and had not once looked at, because there was nothing in it that he needed.

It is a Crown custody receipt for his trial handbell, made out at about twenty-five minutes to four in the morning on the tenth of this month, when a handcart and an oiled linen and a boy of nine came down the Bellhouse lane between two watchmen.

It has his name and the copyist’s and a number and the condition of the object, and it is a good receipt. It records the ash frame, the oiled linen, the paper sliver lifted out and packed separately, and it is signed by the Crown copyist and by the watch, and Tomas Vale’s hand is not on it as a surrender but as a maker, at the bottom, in the place where a maker’s hand goes.

And in the margin, in his own ink, he had written a correction that is eleven words long, and under the correction the Crown copyist had put his initial, and Tomas had explained the initial in his own book on the tenth day in about a hundred words and had never once improved on them.

The margin said: *Object received bound. No answer recorded at taking. Maker has not certified the binding.*

And the initial was a single letter, and it was not a name, and there was no way on the page to tell whose it was, and it was not in the same hand as the margin, and Tomas had never once asked who wrote it and had never looked for the answer on purpose.

And Tomas Vale, alone in his father’s yard with a lamp, read it again the way he had read it fourteen times before, and said out loud, to nobody, in a yard with three seals on the door:

“That is not agreement. That is a man saying *I have seen this* and going back to his desk. There is no difference between that and a man who agrees except that one of them has to come back to the door.”

He put the sheet away and got out the plain carrier off its frame and set it in the middle of the yard, out of the wind, more than eight feet from the ash-pit wall, and looked at it for a while without touching it.

Six and a half inches at the mouth. Too bright. It repeats within eight feet of a wall and it goes further than he wants and he cannot make it not go. There is no wax in the crown of it and no groove dressed in the crown, and there is a bar of the ward’s own drain grating in the waist of it, cut twice with a file in three places so that a stranger could find it in ten years with a lamp, and there is a mark on it that says VALE KELLS and the nineteenth of this month and nothing else.

It has one claim in its frame and the claim is about water, and the claim is the only one it has ever carried, and on the twenty-seventh it is going to have to stop carrying it.

He put his hand on the headstock and took it off again and left it in the middle of the yard and went and shut the door, because it had begun to spit rain on the ash-pit wall.

And standing in the dark inside his own workshop, with a sheet of paper in a drawer that asked a question he was not going to be told the answer to, and a receipt in a book that had a name in a margin that was not agreement, and four days of silence that had ended on a towpath at the seventh hour, he heard — or did not hear, and he could not have said afterwards which of the two it was, and neither could anybody else have said for him — a sound, once, off the river, somewhere below the millrace, out in the dark, where there was no route and no gate and no door, and which was not loud, and which was not a bell, and which stopped.

He went out into the yard and stood at the shut door with his hand flat on the boards and listened for a long time.

There was the water at the low gate, and the millrace, and the wind coming off the ridge, and a dog, four streets off, and nothing else.
