# Chapter 0048 — First Light

It began at the low end of the turn at about the fourth hour of the morning, in the dark, with a crowbar, and nobody had been asked and nobody was going to be talked out of it.

Gib Hask’s six men took the backfill out of the Crown renewal at the low-turn culvert between the second hour and the fourth, in about ninety feet, with a bar and a spade and a coil of rope, in the dark, in the rain that had not stopped since the evening, and they put it on the ward side of the lane in a heap, and they did not take any of it away, because Gib Hask is not a thief and has never been a thief and has said so three times in three rooms in this month.

He came to Tomas Vale’s gate at about a quarter past four, in the rain, with the bar in his hand and his coat off, and he did not ask for anything, and he said:

“I have opened ninety feet of your Crown trench above the ward’s own drain and I have done it at night without a paper and I would do it again in an hour. Here is why and it is not a speech. On the fifteenth day a Crown foreman moved his mark two yards because a bellmaker told him the ground was wrong, and everybody in this ward has spent a fortnight being pleased about it. And on the fifteenth day, at the same hour, four houses down this lane stood in water to a table because the same trench is bridged over the same drain at four points, and nobody has done one thing about it, because the thing that would have to be done is illegal and the only men in this ward who could do it are six of mine with a bar, and I am one of them, and I have said so to a court and been entered in a book.”

“You have cut a Crown works order at night.”

“I have cut a Crown *backfill* at night, which is dirt,” Hask said. “I have not touched a wire. I have not touched a bar of their metal. There is a difference and a Crown man will not see it and I do not need him to.”

He shifted the crowbar.

“Do not tell me it is a good week for a thing like this. I know what week it is. I want you to understand that I have not done it for the round and I have not done it for the children and I have not done it because a bellmaker made a bell. I have done it because Renn’s gate has not come up since the seventh day of this month and there is rain on it and the water comes up this lane to the flats and the flats are where the people are. That is all of it. That is a thing I said in a court on the fourteenth day and got a book entry for, and the book entry has not moved a stone in a fortnight, and I have run out of other ways.”

Tomas stood in the rain at his own gate with no answer, and had one that was worse than none, which was that Hask was right and the answer to it was a bar.

“When Renn’s gate comes up this morning, men will go down that lane to look at it,” Tomas said, “because that is what men do.”

“They will.”

“And there is going to be a Crown officer in this ward today who is going to be given a piece of paper about whether there is disorder in it, and that paper has two lines on it, and the second line is about co-operation, and forty men standing at a flood gate at first light is going to help somebody fill it in.”

“Write that down,” Gib Hask said. “Write it in your book with the hour, and put my name against it, and I will not unsay a word of what I have said and I will not be sorry for the ninety feet of dirt, and if a Crown man wants a man to be sorry about the ninety feet of dirt he can come and find me at the low turn this evening and I will help him take it out again and put it back and we can do it all day.”

Renn’s gate came up at about ten past five. It came up hard, on the first pull, with the water already over the flat stones and a foot of it in the lane behind, and Hollis Renn stood in it to the thigh with a lamp and a spade and a boy of thirteen he had got out of a house on the flats at two in the morning, and he worked the gate for fifty minutes, and it came up, and the water went out of the lane in about eleven minutes in a shape that a Crown surveyor could have drawn and that about sixty people watched.

Tomas Vale was in the ward during all of it. He was not at the low turn and he was not at the gate and he was in his own yard, and he wrote down the hour and the fact and the name of the man and the ninety feet, and he wrote *cost to the ward* in the day-book and then crossed the word out and wrote *cost to the ward, borne by Gib Hask, who has asked for it to be entered against his own name and who is entitled to have it entered exactly.*

---

At about half past four he took the plain carrier off its frame in the middle of the yard, out of the wind, more than eight feet from the ash-pit wall, and put it on the bench, and did the thing he had come out of bed to do.

The bell has no wax in its crown and no groove dressed in its crown. There is nothing in the metal but the metal and a bar of the ward’s own drain grating set into the waist and cut twice with a file in three places. The claim is not in the bell. The claim has never been in the bell. The claim is in four places in this town and every one of them is a piece of paper, and he had made that decision on the nineteenth day without knowing he was making it, and it is the only decision in this whole business that he is still certain was right.

He took the water claim off the frame first. The ward board’s copy came down off the board on the twenty-third day, in front of nine people, and Mrs Wilde had watched it come down. Hollis Renn’s oilskin board at the low gate had a line on it that had been there since the nineteenth, and the line came off with a wet cloth, and the two of them went into the Bellhouse yard, and he read them out in the dark to a keeper and a watchman and a woman with a slate, and he put them in the fire in his own yard, in the ash-pit, because a claim that has been withdrawn in three books is not a claim any more and is not a relic and is not a witness, and a paper you keep is a paper somebody finds in nine years.

Renn said one thing about it, at the low gate, in the rain, with the lamp.

“That is not about water.”

“No.”

“My sentence says the words said at the gate are the words the keeper himself can support, and nothing is added in the ward, and nothing is added above the landing.”

“Yes.”

“Your bell reaches a mile past the toll shed and you wrote that on the sheet and I read it and I said yes to a bell and not to a mile.” He put the spade down and stood on the sill. “I am going to sound it. I am not going to add a word of mine, and if a man on the landing stage says that I told him to go, I will tell him to his face that I did not, and I will do it in front of whoever is standing there. Do you understand that I am agreeing to be contradicted by my own bell and I cannot stop it and I am agreeing to it anyway, at half past four in the morning, in the rain, with my gate due up inside forty minutes?”

“Yes,” Tomas said. “That is the whole of what is being asked of you and it is the whole of what I am able to promise, which is nothing.”

---

Wenna Cray’s withdrawal came down the lane on foot at about a quarter to six, in the rain, in a coat over her night things, with a slate, and she had written it before she came and she had not come to find out what he was going to do first, which was the correct order and Tomas noted it in the book afterward as the difference between a withdrawal and an act.

*Twenty-eighth day, the fifth hour. Wenna Cray withdrew from the bell of the Vale Bellhouse, in her own hand, in her own words, before the bell was sounded and without knowing whether it was to be sounded. The ground of it is entered by her and is hers. Captain Pike records this as a withdrawal, and records that it is not a silence, and that no consequence of any kind follows to her or to her trade, and that the bell will be sounded at about twenty minutes to the sixth hour with one keeper and not three, and that the count of one is entered here and is a count of one.*

Her ground was four lines and it is the correctest thing anybody has said in this ward this month.

*Because my four clauses were the ward board and two doors and I named the two doors, and this is not that. And because a Crown officer with a spade stood in that yard yesterday at the fourth hour and asked me which bell in it was the one bound to a claim, and I said I would not tell him, and I did not tell him, and he went away and got what he wanted out of a book an hour later and did not need me, and I have understood from that that a keeper’s duty is not a wall. It is a thing you say out loud in a room, and if the room is not a room, it is only a woman being difficult in a yard.*

*And I am not withdrawing out of fright and I want that in the book, because I was on the board for nine days being the careful one and I have a right to the other half of the reputation. I am withdrawing because I have read the words and I cannot stand behind all of them, and a duty I cannot stand behind is not a duty, it is a signature, and I have watched two signatures this month in a Crown book turn into a wagon.*

Nell Fenn read it into the ward’s book in three hands and Oren Pike read it into the watch book and the copyist who was at the board at that hour — because the Crown’s officer for the twenty-eighth was already at the board at half past five, as he had said he would be — read it into his own book and gave Nell Fenn a piece of carbon paper for it, which she has still got.

And Tomas Vale stood in his own yard with a count of one.

He had said on the nineteenth day that the count was a count of two and that he was not going to go and look for a fifth. There was no fifth. There was a count of one. He did not go up the hill.

---

He rang at about twenty minutes to the sixth hour, in the middle of the yard, out of the wind, with the leather, once, and he said the words each time and the words are these, and they went on the board afterwards in Mercy Slee’s hand and in Jerm’s hand and in the copyist’s hand, and the copyist had them copied out fair by six o’clock and they were in a satchel going to Fourmile by eight, and what the Crown did with them is the whole of the second half of this day.

*Here is what is known. A Crown officer of this commission is to be at this ward at first light today with a full return and a list of what he is to bring. The district has required, of every household, an answer in the house’s own hand to the question whether this ward is going to take its children out of it, and has said it will certify that answer, and has said in print that a certified answer is a return. About forty doors have put something in that box. Where a household has, there is now a Crown document with a child’s name in it and the name of the person he is to go to, in the house’s own hand, and a Crown office that holds a document with a name in it will come to the door written on it. That is the whole of what I know.*

*I do not know which door. I do not know the hour of the day. I do not know that anything will be taken. If nothing is taken, this sentence is wrong, and I will say so at the board myself, in daylight, in front of whoever comes.*

*This is a warning. It is not an order. There is nothing in it that tells you what to do, and I have no right to tell you and have not tried.*

*It is one bell and it is a barred bell. The Bellwrights’ Measure found on the twenty-sixth day that this bell’s origin cannot be certified, and that I may not ring it in a public place in this county, and I am ringing it, and that is a finding I have made on myself in front of about forty people and I would like it written that I made it and that nobody made it for me.*

*The keeper is Hollis Renn at the low gate. There is one keeper and there are not three, and the count of one is entered in the ward’s book this morning in three hands. My own words are the words and I have not added a word of his, and he has told me he will not add one of his, and the two of us are answerable for that between us and for nothing else.*

*I am Tomas Vale, maker, of this yard, and this is the only bell I have ever made, and it is too bright and it repeats within eight feet of a wall and I cannot make it stop and I am not going to try to. It will be heard further than I want. I do not know where it is heard. I do not know who hears it and I will not be told what anybody did about it.*

Jerm held the book and wrote the hour, and Tomas asked him to write one more line under it, and Jerm wrote it.

*Twenty-eighth day, the fifth hour. Sounded once in the yard of the Vale Bellhouse, in a public place, by a bell barred on the twenty-sixth day by the Bellwrights’ Measure. Entered by Jerm, watchman, holder of the book. The maker asked that the fact of the bar be written beside the hour and I have written it.*

---

At the north gate, at the sixth hour, Watchman Jerm sounded the millrace bridge bell.

It was on the gate bracket, having come off the north wall of the Bellhouse yard at about the seventh hour the night before, in the rain, on the strength of a grant made in a lane by a watch captain in his own hand, with a keeper named and a limit written and the Crown’s wax on the Bellhouse door broken that same afternoon at the Crown’s own request and entered in two books.

Jerm pulled the rope twice, slowly, with a long fall between, and the note of it went out over a town that was already awake and did what a Crown note does when it is struck on a Crown instrument at a Crown gate on the morning of a Crown attendance.

And the two sounds were about ninety seconds apart and there is a woman at one hundred and nineteen who has said, in a lane, in the last hour of that morning, that she heard both of them and that she could not tell which was the worse news, and that the Crown’s was the one that told her what to do.

---

They came in on the west road at about twenty past six, in the first grey, and there is a bar and a chain at the head of the mill narrows and a man with a lamp who went on all night and who was Foreman Crane’s own man and not a watchman, and it stopped two carts and it did not stop anything with a wheel track wider than a handcart, and it was not enough, and the three sentences that half-closed that road on the sixteenth day are on a slate in a market court clerk’s hand and in a covered box on the ward board and they were all true and none of them was a wall.

A Crown wagon came up to the mill narrows at about the seventh hour, on a morning when the Crown’s own cart was still sitting across the culvert at the low turn under a works order that was twenty-six days old, and it stopped, and the driver got down, and the man with the lamp said, as a man with a lamp says a thing, that he was sorry and that he could not move it, and the wagon went over the bank.

It took the top four of the cut steps out, the ones Alis Marrow’s mother goes down four times a day to the mill, and it went out over the water on the other side and came back onto the road thirty feet on with both shafts down, and got itself together, and went on up the west road into Kells at about a quarter past seven in the morning, in front of about eleven people who had come out to watch a road be shut.

Alis Marrow was one of the eleven. She watched about nine feet of her mother’s steps go into the water and she did not make a sound that anybody heard, and she went up the hill afterwards and told Wenna Cray, in about nine words, and Cray wrote it in the ward’s book with the hour, and Cray’s own withdrawal of a bell was on the same page, three lines apart, and she did not look at the two of them together and said so to a man who asked her to.

---

And the ward did what a ward does, which is that it did four different things, and there is a page in the ward’s book with four columns and no headings at the top of it and every household’s own hand in the right one, and Oren Pike read all four headings into the market court book at the tenth hour with the hour against it, and nobody merged them, and there is a Crown return in Fourmile about the twenty-eighth that gives the four figures and does not give the four headings, because the Crown’s man could not find a box for them.

**First column: households that moved.** Nine, and of the nine, four went up the hill to relations and two went to Fourmile in a hired cart and three went out the west road on foot before the wagon got to the top of it, walking up the bank where a wagon had been, which is a thing nobody had told them to do.

**Second column: households that stayed.** Twenty-one. They stayed and they said so, and eleven of them put a person in the door and the rest did not.

**Third column: households that hid.** Not eleven households. A household does not hide. A neighbour’s cellar, a loft, a wood, a field, a boat at the upper mooring — eleven children in eight places, put there by other people, in about two hours, mostly by people who had not spoken to each other since the eighth day.

**Fourth column: households that refused.** Four doors, at which somebody said no to a Crown officer in daylight, and the fourth of them is set down in full here because it is the one the morning is about and because Tomas Vale was not in it and was four streets away.

House 114 is Absalom Teage, a carter, who filled in the Crown’s second supplementary return on the twenty-second day in his own hand and in his own words and with four lines in the reason field and nobody in the room, and in which he wrote that his household is removing a person under the age of seventeen from the ward before the twenty-eighth day, and to whom, and by what means, and the person is his boy of nine, and the boy went to Fourmile on the twenty-sixth, in a cart, to his aunt’s door.

And that Crown officer at one hundred and fourteen had a list on the twenty-eighth at the eighth hour, and the list had the boy’s name on it, and the Crown district office in Fourmile also had a return on file, made out in a carter’s own hand on the twenty-second day, with a Crown stamp on it, saying that the person named in it had been delivered to Fourmile on the twenty-sixth.

Both of those are true and only one of them is a fact about the world.

He put it in his own hands. The officer read from the list. The officer said the name. The officer had a form and the form had a line on it.

And there were about thirty people in that lane and Tomas Vale was four streets off in a market court with a slate, and did not know any of it, and heard the whole of it at the ninth hour from Mrs Corris, in about eleven words, standing in a doorway, and did not improve them.

And Absalom Teage said: “Then you have two pieces of paper and they do not agree, and I am going to tell you why they do not agree, and I am going to tell you in front of the lane, and I am not going to shout, and you may write it down or you may not, and if you do not write it down then you are going to be a man who took a child out of a house on a list when there is a cart coming up the hill this morning to take him.”

He put the return in the officer’s hands. It was the Crown’s own form, folded in four, and it was soft at the folds and it had been in a satchel since the twenty-second and it had been to Fourmile and come back, and the line in it was in his own hand and it said: *The persons under the age of seventeen resident in this household: one. Removed before the twenty-eighth: yes. To: my sister, in Fourmile, by cart, on the twenty-sixth, the boy to be delivered at her door. Reason, in our own words: because there is a box on your paper and if the box is empty they will fill it themselves, and we have put in it the truth, and the truth is that he is going on the twenty-sixth, and you may write that down and put the hour to it.*

“Your return says the boy was delivered on the twenty-sixth,” the officer said, reading it twice. “He was not delivered on the twenty-sixth.”

“He was not,” Teage said. “I will tell you what happened and it is nothing and there is nothing in it that anybody in this lane needs to be frightened of. My brother-in-law was to bring a cart from Fourmile on the morning of the twenty-sixth, and he did not bring it, and the boy has been in this house since the twenty-second and slept in this house last night and had his breakfast in this house this morning, and I have not left this house since the morning of the twenty-sixth because I have been standing in front of it with a list of my own. And there is a cart on the west road at this moment with my sister in it and it will be at the top of this lane before the middle of the day, and you can stand in the lane and watch it, and so can about forty other people.”

The officer read the return a third time.

“Then your return is not true.”

“My return is exactly true,” Teage said. “It says he goes on the twenty-sixth. He goes on the twenty-sixth. He did not go on the twenty-sixth until the twenty-sixth had been and gone, because the cart did not come, and the form you sent me has no field in it for *the cart did not come*, and that is the fault of the form and not of the man, and I am not going to be the man who lies in a Crown box to make your morning easier, and if you want my son off this step you will have to do it on a piece of paper that says you have done it, in front of forty people, and put the hour to it.”

The officer was about twenty-six and he had a lamp and he was not going to enjoy the rest of the morning whatever he did with it.

“I am not taking him,” he said. “I am writing this down. Household of one hundred and fourteen produces its own return of the twenty-second day, in which the person named is stated to have been delivered. The person is produced and is in the house. The district holds a return and this commission holds a list and they do not agree. No removal made. And I am going to write that the household said the cart did not come, because that is what the household said.”

“Put the hour.”

“— the eighth hour and twenty minutes.”

“And put that you came to my door with a list and I gave you the paper, because in a month one of us is going to be told the other thing happened, and it will not be you that has to prove it.”

The copyist wrote it. It went in the round’s book in the Crown’s own hand with the hour and it was read out at the market court on this morning, and it is in Collis’s return, and it is the only line in any Crown document from this month that says the Crown arrived at a door and the household produced the Crown’s own answer and the Crown’s own answer did not hold, and the reason that line exists is that a carter filled in a Crown’s box in his own hand on the twenty-second day of the month, with no officer in the room, and nobody in this ward treated that as betrayal, and it was entered at the board as *first column, and the number is one*, and nobody who read it said a word about it.

And that is the whole of it, and it is a return and a hand and a carter and a door and a cart that did not come, and the bell had nothing to do with it. The bell’s whole part in it was that a true sentence arrived at about twenty minutes to five in the morning about a Crown office that comes to a door written on a document, and it arrived before the wagon, and the carter had put his boy’s name and a day of the month on that document six days before he ever heard a bell, and what he got in return was a young man with a lamp who read his own handwriting twice and then would not be the man who contradicted it in a lane. Nobody in Kells may say the bell did that. **A bell cannot get a Crown officer to leave a door alone, and the reason this boy is in his kitchen is that a cart did not come up a hill from Fourmile on a morning in this month, and that is a fact about carts, and a district in a real town has no instrument for it at all.**

---

Oren Pike held the north gate from the sixth hour to the ninth, which is two hours longer than a watch captain needs to hold a gate on a morning when nothing is coming through it, and then went into the market court and sat down at the side with his cap under his arm, and he did not put two men in a lane at any hour of that morning, and no person asked him to.

Nell Fenn got thirty-one children off the river landing at about half past six in the morning, which she did not do alone and was not asked to do, and the way it was done was that she had a slate with a list on it of the households on the route that have somebody under seventeen in them, which she has had since the fourteenth day, and she stood at the top of the landing steps at half past five with Mercy Slee and read out twenty-two house numbers, and about nine adults came up the steps, and thirty-one children went off that landing by the mill end and up the hill inside forty minutes, on foot, in the rain, led by whoever was nearest.

Nobody rang anything at the landing. There was nothing to ring it with. The Crown had two officers on the landing stage at the seventh hour and the stage is a stone’s throw above the low drain gate, which is the end of a courier’s route and has been since the ninth day of the month, and the crown of the situation on that stage at seven in the morning was that there was no bell on it that anybody was allowed to sound, and there had not been for three weeks, and on the morning it would have mattered most there was still not.

Tamsin Rook rang once.

Foreman Crane came to her at a quarter past five, at the top of the lane, in the rain, and asked her, in his own words, which is a thing he has never done in front of anybody, and he said:

“Come to the landing door and ring it once and say nothing, and I will stand at the door and say the words in my own voice to whoever opens, and I want it in your own book that I asked you and named what I wanted repeated, which is *come to this door*, and no more than that, and that I have not asked you for anything else and I know what the answer to the rest of it is and it is no.”

She walked the route. From the north gate down through the Low Ward to the low drain gate, in the rain, in the dark, at the pace of a woman who is checking, with the bell in her hand and not in her bag, which she had not done on a public road since the fourth day of the month. She rang it once, at the landing door, because a man at a door had asked her to and had named what he wanted repeated, and then she stood at the door while he said his own words in his own voice to about fourteen people, which were: *There is a Crown officer on the landing stage and I have shut the stage gate and I have got the key in my pocket, and I am not telling you what to do and I have no more idea than you do, and if anybody comes to this door before eight o’clock I will be here, and I am not saying run.*

And she did not repeat it. She did not ring a second time. She carried the bell the length of the route and did not ring it past the low drain gate, because the route ends there and the flood bell takes up the local signal and has since the ninth day of this month.

And she did not hear it. She cannot hear the note and has not been able to since the fourth day. She rang a bell at a door in the rain in the dark and the hand that held the hammer went numb and the bell did what it does and she was told by nobody, and did not ask, and put it in her bag at the drain gate and walked home up the towpath.

She said one sentence about it to Tomas Vale at about the ninth hour, at the board, in front of nobody she chose and did not care about, and it was this:

“I rang it once and I could not hear it and I did not ask four people afterwards what they thought had happened, and that is the first honest piece of courier work I have done in a month, and I have nobody to thank for it, because there is nobody in this ward who is going to tell a woman who has been standing at the top of this lane since the eighth day of this month that she was right to ask.”

---

At the ninth hour Crown Officer Ambrose Rudd came to the ward board of Kells as he was instructed to, in a good coat that had been rained on, with the watch attending him and about two hundred and forty people in the lane, and he produced the four things on the board, in the order they were written, and read each of them out entire, and he did not sit down.

He read the full return for the renewal of the twenty-eighth, entire, at a board, in a lane, to a ward that had spent a month arguing that it was never going to be shown one. He read the headings, and there are nine columns on it, and the ninth is *Witnessed by*, and the column is filled in six lines this morning, and five of the six are names in a household’s own hand, and the sixth is a name entered in the margin of a line at one hundred and thirty-three on the afternoon of the twentieth day of this month by a Crown clerk in his own name and initialled, and a clerk from the district said the word *six* out loud and then said it again.

He read the two series of supplementary returns delivered to the ward, with the house numbers of those who had returned them. Three in the first series. Eleven in the second, by the time of the reading, of which nine had put a person under the age of seventeen and a destination, and of the nine, one household was Absalom Teage’s and had produced its own paper at its own door an hour before, and Rudd read that fact out in the lane as well, because he had it in the round’s book in a Crown hand with the hour against it, and because he is a man who reads the thing that is against him first.

And then he read the third of the four things, which is the authority of the commission to ascertain what works of metal are in the ground of the yard of the Vale Bellhouse, with such works to be opened in the presence of the ward and entered in three books.

He read that one out and then stopped, and looked at his own page, and then at the lane, and said:

“Captain. That authority was discharged yesterday afternoon at the fourth hour, in daylight, with the ward in the lane, by Surveyor Collis, on her own initiative and without a spade of the district’s, and the finding is in the copyist’s book and the copyist’s book went out of Kells at the eleventh hour last night. The floor was opened. The line was found. The bearing was taken and read out in front of about forty people. I have been instructed to produce a thing that was spent yesterday, and I am producing it, and I would like it noticed by everybody standing in this lane that a district office in a real town in a real building sent a schedule to this ward for work that had already been done in the open by a Crown surveyor who was not asked to.”

He held the paper up for a second, and then folded it and put it inside his coat.

“I am not going to pretend I enjoy it. I am going to tell you the truth about the fourth thing, because I have spent a fortnight being a man in this ward who says true things when it costs him and I am not going to stop now that it is expensive.

“The fourth thing is the full return. That is what I am for. Not the surveyor, not the finding, not the notice. I have got a form and I have got a return and I have got six lines in a column, and I am going to read them out in a public room this morning because I have said in this lane for eight days that a Crown commission that refuses to offer is a commission that has lied to somebody, and a commission that offers a form and then reads it out in the same morning is not lying. It is something much worse. It is exactly what it said it was, at the time it said it.”

The market court sat at the ninth hour with the Crown’s table under the arcade because the watch had compelled that counter on the ninth day of this month and nobody had ever withdrawn the compulsion, and it is the only thing in Kells that happened on the ninth of this month without anybody arguing about it.

And the Crown’s paper came out of the district’s satchel at about twenty past nine, and the copyist read it out in a flat voice, and it is on a page in Fourmile, and Oren Pike read it afterwards and entered it in the watch book entire, and it is the reason the Crown did not leave with nothing:

*That at about twenty minutes to five in the morning of the twenty-eighth day a bell was sounded in the ward of Kells from a workshop under a declaration of this commission, and that the bell is one of which the Bellwrights’ Measure has certified on the twenty-sixth day that its origin cannot be traced, and that the same bell is entered in the register of the watch of this ward on the nineteenth day four times in the market lane, at about the eighth hour of the evening, with no keeper named, upon an occasion when the ward of Kells was disturbed and a carrier lost a handcart and load stated at thirty-six pounds and a Crown road hand had a wrist broken.*

*That at about the sixth hour of the same morning a second bell was sounded at the north gate of this ward, being a Crown instrument, under a written condition made by the watch captain of this ward and entered by him in his own hand in a public place in the evening of the twenty-seventh day, upon an application made by this commission in the commission’s own name, the condition being made in answer to a form which the watch had been sent on the twenty-fourth day requiring the names of persons sounding bells and which the watch declined in its own hand and in public, and the district’s finding is that the second bell was sounded under a condition the Crown applied for.*

*And that the district finds, upon this and upon the register of the watch, that the bells of this ward are not accounted for by any person.*

“Are those your bell’s words, Mr Vale.”

“They are.”

“Entered by the soundest, at the ward board, in a hand you recognise, and copied out fair by a copyist of this district. Do you dispute them.”

“No.”

The clerk gave him a pen and Tomas Vale wrote, in full, in front of about two hundred and forty people, and it went in the market court book in three hands and in the ward’s book in two and in his own day-book in the yard at night, and it is the shortest thing he has ever written in public:

*I wrote those words, in those words, at about twenty minutes to five in the morning, in my own yard, out of the wind, once. I was asked the same thing yesterday and I said the same thing. I wrote them knowing the Bellwrights’ Measure had barred the bell on the twenty-sixth and I rang it anyway, and the bar and the hour are both in the record and I have asked for them to be written beside each other.*

*I was also told, at the fifth hour of the same morning, through the watch, that the district had required an answer whether any bell in this ward is bound to a claim and by whom, and that the watch had declined to answer and entered that it declined. The last sentence of the paper the officer has just read out is the district’s answer to that refusal. I have read it. I do not dispute one word of it. And I want it read again, because it is the sentence in which I am used as a Crown witness, and I want it read in a full room.*

The clerk read it again. Then a woman at the back said, quite loudly, *Read it again.* And about forty people said it again, and the district’s copyist — who was about forty, and had a cough, and had put a bearing in a finding on the previous evening and told a bellmaker about it at a gate — looked up, and then read it a third time because two hundred and forty people told him to, in a full market court, in the rain, with the hour against it.

And Deputy Reeve Crake’s two-line form, the one that had been sitting in a district office since the seventeenth day of this month, was answered on the afternoon of this day in the words he said in a lane on the twenty-first with a child on a wagon behind him, and he filed it exactly as he said he would file it, and nobody in Kells saw it until the twenty-ninth:

*First line: is there disorder. Second line: what was the state of the ward’s co-operation. Answer: the ward co-operated. The ward co-operated in a document, in three columns, in a public room, for twenty-one days, and co-operated on the morning of the twenty-eighth by moving nine households, hiding eleven children through other people’s doors, and standing at a flood gate, and the commission is of the view that the district should record that no act of violence was committed by any person in this ward upon any Crown officer in this ward, and should record further that the ward’s bells are unaccounted for, which is the other line on this form, and is a different question, and is not the same as disorder, and I have put the two on the same piece of paper because the office has given me one piece of paper and I have been a Crown officer for nineteen years and I am not going to pretend I did not.*

The operation on the twenty-eighth of this month was disrupted and it was not erased, and the Crown was still in the ward at the twelfth hour, and the Crown had its own paper, and in about four days that paper was going into a real building in a real town with a real date on it, and there was not one thing in it that a Crown officer had to be ashamed of, and every single line of it was true.
