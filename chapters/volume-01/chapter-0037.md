# Chapter 0037 — A Plain Bell

Sabin Fell taught rules for a living and had never once in fifty years supplied a design, and Tomas had known that since he was nineteen, and had gone down the hill on the nineteenth morning anyway, with a slate, because the only man in Kells who had cast more bells than his father was sixty-one and would not help and would not stop him either.

“What I have,” Tomas said, “is no metal.”

“You have half a barrel in the casting room with your father’s hearth in the bottom of it,” Sabin said, “which was never on an inventory because an inventory counts objects, and that has been the argument for four hundred years, and it is a good one, and it is the reason you are about to do a foolish thing in a yard with three seals on the door.”

“It is enough for one small bell.”

“It is enough for one small bell and not for two, and you know that, and you have not come to me about metal.” Sabin took the slate and looked at it upside down, the way he had always done, and then turned it round, which was a thing he did not do. “You have come to me about a frame.”

“The four parts.”

“The four parts. Say them.”

“Physical witness matter that another person could find. A living keeper with a specific accepted duty, and the duty has to be written down and has to be withdrawable. An informed answer from a person who can refuse. And a carrier that may not add anything.” He stopped. “And three answers from three people who understand it and can withdraw.”

“Four parts and three answers, and in nine days of this month you have had a town of four hundred and eleven and a document with forty-one lines on it and not one bell you can sound, and you have come to me on a morning to ask me what to make.” Sabin handed the slate back. “I am not going to tell you. Listen to why, because the why is the thing you have not learned, and I have watched you for nineteen days and you have learned everything else.”

He put the slate down on the bench and put his own hand flat on it.

“If I give you a design, then in four years there will be a bell in this district that came out of my head, and when it is copied — and it will be copied, they always are, that is the whole of the trade — then what has been copied is me, and I will be standing in a room in Sennet with a guild on my side of the argument and a bell on yours, and I will not be able to say that I gave you a shape and you poured the metal. Men have been ruined in this trade for less than that and I have seen two of it and one of them was me.” He took his hand off the slate. “So: no design. Rules, as many as you can stand up.”

“All right.”

“Now sit down, because I have four and you have never had four at once, and the fourth is the one I have not given anybody in this county in twenty years, and I am going to give it to you because you are going to get it wrong otherwise and get it wrong in a way that will be expensive for people who are not you.”

---

“Tell me the four men you would ask,” Sabin said.

“Two of them,” Tomas said. “Renn at the low gate, and Wenna Cray at the ward board. Both of them have a written duty already and both of them can withdraw from it in one sentence and neither of them is a person who says yes to be agreeable.”

“Crane?”

“Crane will not. On the sixteenth he gave me a sentence about a lane and about being handed a decision, and it was a good sentence, and it is not the sentence he is going to say to me now, and I am not going to pretend to a man that it is. A foreman cannot hold two duties on one morning and be trusted for either, and he told me he was right about that before I thought of asking him, and that is the part of it that has nothing to do with me.”

“The courier.”

Tomas did not answer for a moment, and that was the answer, and Sabin watched him not answer with the expression of a man watching a fuse.

“Right,” Sabin said. “Here it is, and it is one sentence and it took me twenty years and a bell I never cast.

“**Take the person most likely to refuse, and ask them first.**”

Tomas looked up.

“That is backwards,” he said. “If you take the person most likely to refuse and ask them first, and they refuse, you have lost the only answer you could have had. You have got two of three and no time. The rule you are giving me is a rule for throwing the carrier away.”

“It is a rule about the order of the asking, not the number of the answers, and you have not understood it, and you will go on not understanding it for about a year and then you will.” Sabin leaned forward. “Why do you want a third answer?”

“Because a public signal takes three.”

“No it does not. A public signal takes three, and that is the watch captain’s frame and it is a good frame, and it has nothing to do with what I have just said.” He put a finger on the bench. “Why do you want a third answer, Tomas. Not why the rule says three. Why *you* want three.”

“Because a thing that one person says is a thing a person said. A thing three people say is a thing a ward—”

“A thing a ward can be *made* to have said,” Sabin said. “That is why three, and the watch captain knows it and you do not, and I will not argue with him about it. Here is the other half, and it is the half that costs. The first answer you take is a claim. Every answer after the first is a claim about your first answer. It does not become true by being repeated. It becomes harder to deny. And a man who has three keepers is a man who has three people he can be *surprised* by, and a man who has been surprised once will not ring a bell again for a year, and will tell the story of the surprise instead, and the story will be that the bell was wrong, and he will be believed by everybody who was frightened, and the bell will be finished.”

“So I take fewer.”

“You take *better*. And the way to take better is to go to the person whose answer is the most dangerous to you, and get it first, while there is still time to be frightened by it and not to argue with it.” He sat back. “A carrier carried by three people who are all certain is a carrier that has had its teeth taken out on the way to the market. The carrier is not the bell. The carrier is the disagreement, and the disagreement has to be somebody’s, and it should be the person most likely to give it to you and the least willing to.”

Tomas sat on the stool in the corner of a workshop with a slate in his hand and worked through the four people in this town he might ask, in order, and Sabin watched him do it and said nothing, which is the worst thing Sabin ever did to him.

Hollis Renn would say yes and mean it and could withdraw in one sentence. Foreman Crane would refuse and would be right. Ansel Stroud was a Crown clerk, and a Crown clerk cannot be asked, and the clerk is the one with the ink on his fingers, and the ink is the argument. Wenna Cray would say yes, and Cray had withdrawn once in this town’s hearing and would say yes with a condition four foot long.

And there was a fifth, and she was at the end of the list, and he had put her at the end of the list in about the time it takes to write a name.

“Vale,” Sabin said.

“I put her last.”

“You put her last,” Sabin said, “because you want her, and a man who wants an answer puts the person he wants at the end so that he has time to get everything else in first and can go to her having already had a day. And she will know. She is a courier; knowing the order of a list is her whole trade. And when you ask her, in whatever way you ask her, she will understand exactly what you did, and she will refuse you, and the refusal will be correct, and you will spend a year thinking it was about the route and it will have been about the list.”

Tomas put the slate down on the bench, face up, where his father had kept a note of every bell he had ever poured.

“Then I put her first,” he said.

“No,” Sabin said. “You do not get to fix it by deciding to be brave in front of me. You get to fix it by telling her what you did, before you ask her anything, and by asking her in that order and not in a better one, and by entering the order in your book, because the order is the whole of what happened and a man who cannot say the order of his own asking has not asked, he has collected.”

He got up and picked his coat up off the stool.

“Two more things and then I am going, because I have a licence in my own name to look after and the Crown have started counting licenses in this county.”

“Two.”

“Your witness matter has to be findable.” He said it as though it were nothing, and it was not nothing. “A piece of metal in a bell that no living person could locate in ten years is a relic and not a witness, and a relic is a thing people kneel at, and I have watched men build careers on kneeling. So the bar out of the drain grating at the low lane: take it, and have it identified, and cut it so that the cut is findable, and write down which of the three gratings it came from and how many bars that grating has, and get two people to write their names under it, and then when somebody comes in ten years and says *what is this* there is an answer that is not you talking.”

“Agreed.”

“And the carrier may not add anything, and you have not thought about what that costs.” He was at the door. “A bell is a mouth. Anything you put in a bell comes out of it, and a thing that comes out of a bell a long way off arrives thinner and rounder and more certain than it went in, and the thinning is not the bell’s fault, it is distance’s, and by the time a sentence has gone a mile it is the same sentence with everything difficult taken out of it. So when you write what goes in, put the difficulty in deliberately. Put in the part that is not known. Put in your own name at the top and your own fault at the bottom, and put the hour, and put *I do not know when this will happen*, and let that travel too, because the hard part is the part that is true, and if you send only the true easy part out of a bell then what you have sent is a version and not a claim, and a version is how this whole business went wrong and you will be able to say so to a Crown clerk in ten years, and today you cannot.”

He went up the hill, and Tomas stood in the yard with the four rules and the fact that he had got them all from a man who would not give him a bell, and the no-ringing notice on the door, and the Crown’s wax, and Oren’s mark, and none of the three would come off without the other two.

---

He asked Oren for the metal on the nineteenth morning, at the watch house, in writing, and he wrote it out longhand, and Oren read it and did not argue for two minutes.

“You want to cast a bell.”

“I want to cast one small bell in my own yard from my own metal, and I want to strike it on my own bench with a leather and a hammer to tune it, and that is all I want, and the no-ringing order is a public order and I am not breaking it and I will not ring it in a lane or in a yard with anybody in it but me.”

“I am not going to let you strike it on the bench.” Oren said it flatly. “Vale, the yard door has my mark on it. I have a Crown seal and a district notice on this same object and if I let a bell be sounded in that yard then in a fortnight there will be a Crown officer in here with a report about an undeclared sounding, and I will be the man who authorised it, and I have already been the man who authorised one thing this month.”

“You are not.” Tomas put the paper on the table. “Look. It says a bench test with a leather and a hammer, not more than once an hour, not in the lane, and I will write the hour in a book and you will read the book whenever you like. And you will not be there and you do not have to hear it, because I am asking you for the paper and not for the evening.”

Oren read it twice and then did what Tomas had correctly predicted he would do, which was to make it narrower.

“Once an hour is twice an hour. Once. And if the leather goes through I will hear it from the lane, and if I hear it I will come up, and I will not stop you ringing it, but I will stand in the yard, and a watch captain standing in a yard is a thing two people remember. Jerm will have the book. And I am entering this myself, in my own hand, and the Crown is going to have a copy in a week, because the Crown is going to have everything this week.” He signed it and gave it back. “You are asking me to write down that I permitted a man who is under a Crown declaration to cast a bell. Do you understand that this is the page I am going to have to defend in a room?”

“Yes,” Tomas said. “And I am asking anyway, and the asking is the whole of what I have got, and I have not got a bell, and on the twenty-first there is a round.”

“That is true and it is not a reason.” But Oren signed it, and pushed it across, and then said, “Vale. One thing, and then I have a gate to open. When you tell me the bell exists, I put it in the register. Hours and places, no names. And the register goes to the district at the close of the seventh day, which is the twenty-fourth, and there will be a line in it that says a bell was made in the Vale Bellhouse yard on the nineteenth day by Tomas Vale, and a Crown man in Fourmile will read that line and he will not need to do anything about it, because the line will already be in a book, in his own office, filed by me. Do you understand what you are making? You are making a document about yourself that nobody will have to ask for.”

“I understand.”

“Right,” Oren said. “Then cast it. I am not going to pretend to be a man who thinks you should not.”

---

He got the bar out of the grating on the nineteenth morning, at the ninth hour, in daylight, in front of about thirty people, and it is the only thing he did this month that he is proud of in a way that does not make him feel good.

He asked. He asked the ward board for a piece of the ward’s own iron, and he said why, and he said what the bar was going into and what the bell was going to say, and he read the words of the bell out, and then he asked for the bar.

*Here is the sentence. On the twentieth day, between the second hour and the fifth hour, there will be water above the flat stones at the foot of the low lane. The ward’s own drain is bridged by the Crown renewal cut that was made on the fifteenth day, and the gate at the low drain has not come up since the seventh, and the reason it has not come up is in the watch book in Renn’s own words and it is not a Crown work. I do not know when it will come. If it does not come, nobody has been harmed, and this sentence is wrong, and I will say so myself at the board in daylight. Nothing in this is an order and nothing in it is a decision. I made the bell and I can sound it and nobody else can.*

Somebody at the back asked what it cost them, and Tomas said it plainly: *It costs the ward a piece of iron out of its own drain, and it costs the ward the fact that a bell now exists in this yard with the ward’s metal in it, and the Crown will have that in a register by the twenty-fourth, and there is a line in a Crown book from the twenty-fourth that says this ward gave a bell a piece of its drain. That is what it costs and I am telling you before you decide and not after.*

The board gave it. It was a bar off the lowest of three gratings, and it was rusted in the middle and sound at the ends, and Mercy Slee, who was the co-holder of the ward’s page and had been made that in public four days earlier, wrote in the ward’s book that the ward gave it, in her hand, and the date, and the words of the bell, and the whole of the words, and the cost as he had stated it. And two more people put their names under hers, and the grate was mended before dark with a length of chain by four of Gib Hask’s men without being asked, and Hask did not come to the board and did not vote and sent word that the grate was his to keep and he would keep it.

Renn was at the board and heard the words with everybody else, and said that a duty on a bell that had not been cast was not a duty but a promise about a thing, and that he would answer it when there was a bell to answer about, and that he wanted it done in front of the board and not in a lane.

Cray said she would answer it too, and asked him one question, which was whether the two doors she named were hers to name, and when he said yes she said *not those two then*, and named two others, and he wrote them down without reading them back to her, on purpose, and she made him read them back, and he read them back twice.

*And the cost, entered, on the same page, in the same hand: the half-barrel has metal in it for one bell and not for two, and if I pour this one it is empty, and I can cast no second bell out of this yard’s own metal this year, and if I want another one I have to buy metal, and if I buy metal from a Crown dealer then this bell has a date and a place of purchase in a Crown document, and if I do not, I have no bell, and I have one.*

He cast it on the nineteenth day, out of the half-barrel, and it took him five hours and it was the first metal he had poured since the day before the wax went on the door, and the smoke went up the chimney and about nine people in the lane looked up at the smoke and one of them was a Crown road hand living in the lodging and came to the door and asked what it was, and Tomas said *metal*, and the road hand wrote it in the gang’s book, because that is what road hands do now. He went and looked at the barrel after it and there was nothing in it but the dross and the scale on the bottom, and he shut the lid, and the half-barrel was empty from that hour and would be empty until somebody bought metal.

It came out well. That was the trouble with it.

It came out six and a half inches at the mouth and it came out bright, and when he had taken the skin off it and set it on the bench and struck it once with a leather, the note went out of the yard.

He sat down on the bench.

It was not loud. He was not a man who heard bells at a distance and had never claimed to be, and he had said in a document with a Crown deputy’s signature on it three days ago that he had no instrument, and this was the third day of not having one. What he had was a note that left the yard and did not come back, which is not the same thing as a distance and is the only thing a bellmaker in a yard with three seals on the door has ever been able to know about one, and he wrote *the note leaves and does not return* in the day-book and underlined it, and underlined it again, because he had learned in nineteen days that a thing a bell does not tell you is not a thing to write in as though it did.

He tried it twice more, an hour apart, as the paper said, and got the same answer twice, and then did not try it a third time, because the third time is when a man starts tuning to what he cannot hear and a bell tuned to what he cannot hear is a bell tuned to nothing in the room.

Then he put a hand on the wall.

That was the other thing and it took him another two hours to understand. He had set the bell in the corner by the ash-pit wall to get it out of the wind, and the wall came back at him. Not the sound. The *note*, a fifth of a second after the strike, quieter and rounder, the same words again in the same voice.

A bell near hard stone does not stop at the stone. It goes into the stone and comes out of it a moment later, and a thing that comes out twice is not a claim any more. It is a claim and a copy of the claim, and a copy is how a sentence stops belonging to the person who said it.

He took the bell out of the corner and set it on the frame in the open, in the middle of the yard, and struck it once, and there was one of it, and he wrote that down too.

Then he wrote the whole of it up as a condition, the way he had written up every bell his father had ever poured, in the ordinary way, with the date and the weight and the metal and the note and the faults. He began it in the afternoon and finished it at the fourth hour, and there is a line in the middle of it that was not true when he started writing and was true when he stopped, and he has left it where it fell, because that is the difference between a condition and a story.

*Bell made the nineteenth day, in the yard, of the shop’s own metal, out of the last of a half-barrel that came out of my father’s hearth and was never on an inventory. Six and a half inches at the mouth. Ash stock, leather-bound headstock, iron clapper, new throughout. Maker’s mark, VALE KELLS, and the date, and nothing else. Weight at the mouth, nine pound two ounce, and at the shoulder, thirteen pound and some, and the difference is mine and I know what it is. No wax of any kind in the crown. No groove dressed in the crown. Nothing in the metal but the metal, and one piece of matter that is not metal, being one bar out of the drain grating at the low lane at the foot of the ward, which the ward gave me at its own board at the ninth hour of this morning, and which is set into the waist inside and which I have cut twice with a file in three places, so that anybody may find it in ten years with a lamp.*

*Condition of the carrier. It is too bright. It goes further than I want it to go and I have not been able to reduce that and I have stopped trying, because a man who tunes a bell to stop at a distance is tuning it to a lie about how far it goes. It repeats in a corner and I have recorded that, and it must not be sounded within eight feet of the ash-pit wall or any wall.*

---

He asked four people, one at a time, with the same words, and he had intended to ask three, and the order on the page is not the order he had written in his own book two days before, and he copied the new order onto the old page and initialled the alteration in the margin, which is the only way he knows how to do that kind of thing.

The first was Tamsin Rook, and she was asked first because a man of sixty-one had told him at first light that morning that the person most likely to refuse has to be asked while there is still time to be frightened by it instead of arguing with it, and because Tomas had put her at the end of his own list for two days out of pure wanting, and had then told her so, in writing, with the hour on it, before he asked her anything at all.

“You put me first,” she said.

“I put you first. I had put you fourth. I wrote it in my book, in three lines, with the hour, and I have read it to you before I asked you anything, and if you want to read it yourself, it is on the page, and it says I had put you fourth because I wanted you, and that is the whole of the reason.”

“Read me the whole of it.”

He read the three lines. She took the book and read them again herself.

“Ask me,” she said.

“Tamsin Rook. Will you take a keeper’s duty on this bell for a warning about water in the low lane, and will you carry it on the low gate, and may I put your name in the book as one of the three answers?”

“No,” she said, and she said it at once, and she said it in the voice she had said at the drain gate on the fourteenth, and then she told him why, which is a thing her own fourth term does not require her to do and which she has never done for anybody.

“Because a bell that is in a claim cannot be a bell that is only a call to come to a door, and my four terms are the only thing I have left that is mine, and if I take a public duty on your bell then the day somebody wants my bell they do not have to argue with me about the route at all. They have my name in your book next to a claim about water, and the route is a thing I gave away for nothing in a bad afternoon, and the man who gave it away is the man who rang it in the first place. No. And I want it written that I said no before I gave a reason, and not after, because the second of those is a different kind of no and you will use it.”

“It is written.”

“Give me the pen,” she said, and wrote it, and signed it, and gave it back, and then she did the thing that made the whole of the morning worth it.

“You have two people coming who will say yes,” she said. “Renn will say yes, because a man who has refused a bell once is a man nobody asks twice, and Cray will say yes, because she has been waiting two days for somebody to hand her a door with a name on it and nobody has. And both of them will draw down to two hundred yards and no further, because both of them are careful people and that is what careful people do. And your bell is too bright and you know it is too bright and you have written that down, and you are going to carry a warning about a drain two hundred yards and a half mile, and on a lane that goes to a landing, and there is a man on that lane with a Crown cart who is waiting to be proved right about Crown lines, and everything you are about to do is going to arrive at that man before you know it has.”

“I know,” Tomas said.

“You do not, and I have been the courier at the far end of a reach for eleven years and you have been the maker at the near end of a bench, and those are two different countries and neither of us is the worse one.” She shouldered her bag. “Write the limit on the page. Write it in the claim and let the difficulty travel too, because you did not hear me and Sabin Fell did, and Sabin Fell has never once rung a bell in a lane in his life and he got that sentence out of a kitchen at first light this morning.”

He wrote the limit on the page. He wrote it inside the words of the claim, so that the difficulty would travel:

*And the water will be heard to a quarter of a mile and further, and further than a lane, and I cannot make it stop and I am not going to try, and the far end of it will not be my end of it, and anybody who hears this and fills in the rest of the sentence for himself is doing me the courtesy of a mistake and I cannot prevent it and I am not going to be able to prevent it and I am writing it down anyway.*

The second was Wenna Cray, who said yes in a sentence with four clauses in it and then made him read all four of them out loud twice before she signed, and then wrote it on the board in her own words: *I will sound this at the ward board and at two doors I name, and no further, and I have put the two doors on this line and the two doors are not going to be the doors anybody wants.* The third was Hollis Renn, who said yes in eleven words and then wrote his own sentence on his oilskin board and made Tomas read that too, twice, and did not give a reason, because his own term does not require one, and the sentence was: *I will sound this at my gate if it is sounded at the Bellhouse, and only if it is, and only once, and I may take myself off this sentence at any hour without giving a reason and nothing happens to me for it.*

And the fourth was Foreman Crane, who refused in about a minute, standing up, in his own landing, in the words he had already given Tomas on the sixteenth, and who wrote his refusal out at Tomas’s request and signed it, and it is on the page because Crane said it should be.

So: four asked, two accepted, two refused, and the count is a count of two, and Tomas Vale did not go up the hill to find a fifth, and did not go back to Sabin Fell, and did not go to the wheelwright, and did not ask Oren, and did not ask anybody for a whole afternoon whether two was all right, and the only person he told about the count was Tamsin Rook, because she had told him what it would be before he knew it.

He rang it at the fifth hour of the afternoon, in the middle of the yard, out of the wind, and it went out over Kells.

He did not know where it was heard. Nobody came to the door. Nobody knocked. He was in the yard and Tomas Vale, who is a competent ordinary bellmaker with no power of any kind and no perception of any kind, had exactly one thing and could not use it, and did not try to.

Two people heard it that he did not know about, and both of them were more than a quarter of a mile off and neither of them knew who had sent it.
