# Chapter 0043 — The District

The district’s horse came into Kells at the second hour of the morning of the twenty-fourth with a form on it, and Captain Pike read the form standing in the yard of the watch house with the gate shut, and then came out and shut the gate behind him again and sent Watchman Ardle to the board to ask whether the district had ever in the history of the county sent a form and got an answer back on the same horse.

The answer was that it had, twice, both times within the week, and that Watchman Cask said it was three times and had been on the second one.

The form was four lines and a schedule. It was headed: *TO THE WATCH CAPTAIN OF KELLS. FOR SEVEN DAYS COMMENCING WITH THE TWENTY-FIRST DAY OF THIS MONTH, being days already elapsed: a record of every bell sounded within the ward of Kells, with the hour, the place, and the name of the person who sounded it.*

*Commencing with the twenty-first day. A bell sounded on a day now past is within this return.*

Oren Pike read it four times, and the fourth time he read the second line of it aloud to a watch house that had three men in it, and then he put it on the table and said, without any particular weight, that he had been expecting a second form since the seventeenth and had not expected that one.

“So it is the same paper with a day changed in it,” Tomas said.

“It is a different paper,” Oren said, “and that is the whole of the trick and there is nothing clever in it at all. Mine was a request. It asked for the hour and the place and the name, it said nothing about must, it named no penalty and no office, and I gave two thirds of it and refused the third, and I was quite right about the words on it and I was also, if I am honest for one second, waiting for exactly this. This one commences with a day that is three days gone. It wants the names of the people who rang bells on the twenty-first, the twenty-second, the twenty-third, and the twenty-fourth, and by the time anybody has written the first of them down the first of those days is a thing that cannot be asked about, and a man who has to answer about the twenty-fourth will read the twenty-first and get it right for the wrong reason.”

“And you are not going to answer the twenty-first either.”

“No.”

---

He wrote it out in the watch house in the plainest sentences he had, and he did it with Jerm holding the book and Cask standing at his shoulder, and it took him until the fifth hour and he read every word of it aloud twice before he signed it and it is the longest thing he has ever put in the book.

*Twenty-fourth day. A district form was delivered at the second hour requiring, for seven days commencing with the twenty-first, the hour, the place and the name of the person sounding every bell in this ward, including bells already sounded.*

*I accept the hours. I accept the places. I accept every bell, joined or unjoined, held by any person. I decline the names, as I declined them on the seventeenth, and for the same reason and now with a second reason set out below.*

*The first reason is unchanged. A register of the names of persons who sound bells, made by a Crown office in a ward in the month of a round, is a list of persons who may be removed from a route. The district has already declined to require a keeper list and has recorded that it requires none, and this form requires the same field by a different route, and the route is that the days are in the past.*

*The second reason is mine and is entered because I have had three days to think about it and because a captain who withholds from his own district is a captain with a private file, and I would rather be a captain with a bad public record than a good private one.*

*Set out: the register for the seventeenth to the twenty-fourth goes to the district at the close of this day as the form requires. It contains, for the nineteenth, twelve entries. Six are in the maker’s own yard at the top of the lane. One is at the low drain gate. One is at the ward board. Four are in the market lane at about the eighth hour of the evening. The column for the person who sounded it is filled twelve times with the single word none, in the clerk’s own hand, each initialled, and no name is in that register and no name will be.*

*And I record here, in my own hand, before I sign this, that I know who sounded the four in the market lane, and I have not said it in any book, and I have not said it in any room, and I am not going to say it now, and that the reason is not a protection of the man and is that the register is the district’s document and that a keeper’s name in it would be the only name in a document of twelve lines, and a document with one name in it is a document about that name.*

*I am the watch captain of this ward. I signed the form of the seventeenth. I have a return in the district file with eleven names of this ward’s persons in it and my own name at the head of the covering page. I complied with a lawful seizure on the tenth and I entered why. I am recording all of it here in one place on one day so that if a man in Fourmile ever wants to say that this watch has withheld from its district, he will find it on this page and not in a story he has assembled himself.*

*No name is entered. I am signing this and the register and the two are the whole of the return, and I will read both out in open court with the room full.*

“Read that last part back to me,” Cask said.

“You have it in front of you.”

“I have read it,” Cask said, “and I want you to know that you have just put your own neck in a Crown file for the second time in three weeks, and that on the seventeenth you told me you were not a man being kind in this room, and I am recording that I understood that, and I am recording that you have now done a thing you did not have to do, and I do not know what to do with either of those.”

“Neither do I,” Oren said. “Sign the book.”

Jerm signed the book. Cask signed the book. Oren Pike signed it, and put the hour against it, and did not read the second reason out again.

---

At the seventh hour Captain Pike walked the register down to the north gate himself, in a leather wallet, with Jerm behind him and a Crown rider on a bay horse waiting in the road with the horse’s head turned.

The rider was about thirty-five and had a district hand and had come in with the morning form and had spent five hours in Kells on a matter to do with a culvert, and he took the wallet and untied it on the parapet of the gate, in the lane, in daylight, with about nine people standing there because a man untying a Crown wallet at a gate in this ward is a thing people stand and look at.

He read it. He was not in a hurry about it. He turned the pages at the right places because he had done this before, and he got to the nineteenth and stopped, and stood on the gate step with his finger on the page for a while.

“Four in the market lane,” he said.

“Four in the market lane.”

“At about the eighth hour.”

“At about the eighth hour of the evening.”

“*None*,” the rider said, reading it. He read the word and then he read the next one, and the next, and he went back to the top of the page and read all twelve of them, out loud, standing in a lane with the ward able to hear it, which Oren Pike had permitted on purpose and had entered in the book before he came down to the gate.

“That is four bells in a market lane and no keeper,” the rider said. “And six in a yard at the top of this hill and no keeper. And one at a gate and one at a board. Twelve bells and twelve times *none*.”

“Twelve bells and twelve times *none*,” Oren said, “and I have signed that the word is mine and not the clerk’s, and that the clerk has initialled each one, and that if the district wants the name it may have the name from me in a room, and there is no name in the wallet.”

“I am not asking you for the name,” the rider said. “I am not permitted to ask you for the name. I have read the form of the seventeenth and the form of the twenty-fourth and the amendment in your district’s own hand and I have a very good idea what I am standing in the middle of.” He retied the wallet. “I will tell you what is going to happen, because it is my job to carry it and I would rather carry it having said it. It goes to Fourmile this afternoon. The district renewal office will read it. Somebody in that office is going to read four bells in a market lane and twelve times the word *none*, and he is going to want a name, and he is not going to be able to get one out of that page, and he is going to go up a chain of about four people until he reaches a man who can ask you for it in a different hand, and that man will send for a form, and the form will not be a request.”

“I know,” Oren said. “Send it and I will answer it in the same shape as this one, and you may put that in the book.”

“That is not a comfort to me,” the rider said, and got on his horse.

Ardle watched him go up the north road and then said, to no one in particular, “He is the first Crown man in a month who has read one of our pages out loud in a lane on purpose.”

“He is a man who has read a page and then had to stand in a lane with nine of this ward’s people and work out what to say about it,” Oren said. “Enter that too, and enter the hour, and enter that I permitted him to read it here and not in the watch house, and why.”

---

And then the thing that had been standing behind the whole day came out of Oren Pike at about the ninth hour, in his own room, with the door shut, to a bellmaker who had come down the hill to give him a hand with a thing that did not need hands.

“I have been doing arithmetic again,” he said, “and this time I have finished it, and I do not like the answer, and you are the only person in this town who is going to be told it before it is in a book, because you are going to be in the book.”

“Go on.”

“There are four papers.” Oren put them out on the table, face up, and squared them, which is a thing he does. “The seventeenth: a list of the ward’s signal keepers, and of all persons in the ward having public standing in matters of signalling, with names. Due the seventh hour. The same day, at the seventh hour of the evening: a record of every bell sounded in the ward for seven days, with the hour, the place and the name of the person who sounded it. Yesterday, at the second hour: the same record, for seven days commencing with a day already past. And yesterday afternoon, at the ward board, a district officer, in a room, in daylight, asking whether a list exists of the households that intend to remove a child from this ward before the coming renewal.”

He put his finger on the first one.

“That one wants names of duties. That one wants names of persons who have spoken. That one wants names of persons who sound bells. That one wants names of households. Four documents. Four different offices to send them, four different hands to write them, four days in a month, and every single one of them ends in the word *names*, and every single one of them arrived in this ward in the same two weeks, and And I would have told you, in any ordinary year of this trade, that a district does not send four requests in a fortnight by accident.”

“There has not been a year of this trade when a district sends four requests in a fortnight by accident,” Tomas said.

“There has not been a year of this trade when a district sends four requests in a fortnight by accident,” Oren agreed. “And here is the part I have been not-saying since the seventeenth, so listen to it. I have spent nine days teaching four hundred and eleven people to speak in public with dates on their words. I did that. That is not a Crown thing and it did not come from Fourmile; I put it here with my own hand and Ferrow read it back and the ward acted on it. And every act of it is a sentence, and every sentence has a date, and so when this district asked me on the seventeenth for the voices of this ward I was able to hand them over in a morning without breaking a single oath.

“Not one name of those eleven was forced out of anybody. Every one of them stood up in a room and said a thing out loud and was heard. I did not take them. I took their words, which had already been taken by the room, and I put the room’s day next to their names and sent it to Fourmile in the afternoon and got it back in the evening with a new heading on it.

“So the first of the four papers, I supplied. The second, I supplied; I built the form, I wrote *none* twelve times in a clerk’s hand and initialled each, and I put the whole thing in a leather wallet and sent it up the north road. The third, I supplied; I refused it, and a refusal that is read in an office is still a document from this watch with this watch’s reasons on it. And the fourth was supplied by a girl of sixteen and a woman of about forty in a room, and I entered the word *none* in three hands under it and initialled it myself.”

He looked at the four papers.

“I have not lost one of them. Every request for a name in this ward in the last fortnight has been answered out of my own building, by my own hand, and I did it in the open on purpose, because the whole of my method is that a thing said in a room is a thing that arrives in a file alone and I would rather it arrived with a room attached to it.”

“Oren.”

“I know what it looks like from your side of that table. I have spent nine days teaching a town to be legible and I have built a town out of names without ever once entering a ward, and the reason I can do it is that I did the making.” He squared the papers again, which was the third time. “That is the whole of a watch captain’s arithmetic this month. It took me three weeks and a list of eleven.”

He put the second reason from the morning on the table in front of Tomas and let him read it.

“There,” he said. “That is what I did this morning, and I want you to understand that I could have left it out. Nothing in that form required me to say that I know who rang the four. I entered it because a captain who holds back from his own district has a private file, and a private file is a thing that only one man can be trusted with, and I have spent this month finding out what happens when four hundred and eleven people have to trust one man. So I put my own arithmetic in the record, in my own hand, with the hours, and it names nobody, and it can be used against me, and it is the only honest way I could find to hand a district four pages of myself instead of one line.”

Oren sat back.

“If I had kept that back,” he said, “then this watch would hold a register with twelve lines in it and a private knowledge of four of them, and the district would have the register, and somewhere in a building in Fourmile there would be a man with a reason to come and ask me a question in a room with no witnesses in it. I have had that conversation once. I know how it goes.”

Tomas read the paragraph twice and put it down and said, “You have just made yourself the only Crown officer in this county who can be cross-examined on his own month.”

“Yes,” Oren said. “I have. And I would like to go and stand at a gate now and think about it, and I would like you to come and stand at a gate with me, and I am not going to talk about it any more today.”

---

The district’s return came in at the eighth hour of the evening, on the district’s own horse, in the district’s own hand, and it is the third time in a fortnight that a piece of paper has come out of that office and back into this watch house in the same day, and Oren read it standing.

It was short. It was, in the whole, five lines, and three of them were about the register, and the district had not read the register, because the register was on a bay horse somewhere between the toll shed and Fourmile with a rider who had read it aloud on a gate step in front of nine people.

*Received, the twentieth-fourth day, the watch’s statement of the twenty-fourth.*

*That the register for the seventeenth to the twenty-fourth is received, or upon receipt will be received, and that the district notes the watch’s statement that the name of the person sounding a bell is not entered, the word* none *being entered twelve times in the column provided.*

*That the district requires the names, and requires them for the seven days from the twenty-first, and does not accept the watch’s reasons, and does not accept them now, and the office reserves the right to require them again on a form that shall* must.

*That the register of bells for this ward will be read at the district renewal office at Fourmile, and that the officer of that office responsible for the reading of returns upon route L-4 is* **District Officer Marten Cope**, *and that any person having matter touching the register may address him at that office, and that the office is at the Fourmile renewal office and not at the district commission.*

*And that the office of the district clerk of the northern sub-district signs the return, and that the officer named above is not the district.*

“That is the sentence,” Oren said. “Read the fourth one again.”

He read it again.

“Marten Cope,” he said. “District renewal officer. Fourmile. Named before the paper got here. That office has written down the name of the man who will read the register, on a return dated the twenty-fourth, and it has been drafted and stamped and signed and put on a horse, and the register is on a different horse behind us on the same road, and that office has known since before midday today that this register was coming in and had not seen a line of it.”

He put the paper down on the table and squared it, and then did not square it again.

“Officer Cope,” he said, out loud, to the watch house, with Jerm at the table and Cask in the doorway and Tomas Vale standing by the lamp. “That is the name. Write it down, Jerm, and put the hour against it, and put in the book that I read it out in this room and that I entered it.”

“Do you know him?” Tomas said.

“No. I know the office. I have never in eleven years written to a man in it and I have never had a reason to, and I have had four hundred Crown returns off that office over eleven years and not one of them has ever had a name in the signature that I could put on a person, and I told you on the fifteenth that it was a district renewal desk and not a man.” Oren looked at the fifth line of the return. “And there is a name in it now. It has been a desk for eleven years and there is a name in it, and I have to say this, and I am saying it because it is true and because you will find it out in about a week otherwise and I would rather you heard it from me in a room: I am not relieved. I have been sitting opposite *a desk* for eleven years and I have said the words *a desk and not a man* in this room to two different people, and both times I said it as though it were a comfort, and it is not a comfort, and I have only just worked out that it never was.”

Jerm wrote the name and the hour and read it back, and Oren signed under it, and then he said the last thing of the day, standing at the door with his cap in his hand, to a bellmaker who had come down a hill to hold a gate with him and had been made to sit through four pages of a watch captain’s arithmetic instead.

“Marten Cope, of the district renewal office at Fourmile, will read a register in which there is no name, and will want one, and will go up a chain until he reaches a man who can come here and ask me a question in a room with nobody in it. And when that man comes, I am going to be in this chair, and I am going to say no, and I am going to write down that I said no, and that is the whole of what I have left and I am spending it.”

He put his cap on.

“Twenty-two days,” he said. “I would not go back to the eighth.”
