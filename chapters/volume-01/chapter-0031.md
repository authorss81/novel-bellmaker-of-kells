# Chapter 0031 — The Lane at the Bottom of the Ward

The peg went in at twenty past the seventh hour, and Tomas Vale was eating a crust of bread he had bought from a woman who was not sorry to sell it to him, and he watched the whole thing from the low wall on the ward side without moving for long enough to finish it.

The line came off a reel. It was a cord, not a chain, and it was pegged at both ends and chalked between, and the chalk marks crossed the lane a few feet above the washhouse step, and the two men working it had come to the low end and were standing with their backs to the ward the way men do when they have been told the ward is not their business.

Above the lane the ward was awake. A shutter came up. Then another. By the eighth hour there were nine people standing at the lane mouths on the ward side and none of them had come down.

Below the lane it was different. The river end of it was open ground with the millrace on the right and the old washing stones on the left, and at the low end of it, where the lane turns for the landing, Gib Hask was standing with his back against the wall of his own outhouse and a crowbar across his knees and six men along the turn, and one of the six had a spade and one of the six had a coil of rope, and none of the six were doing anything at all, which was the most frightening way for six men to be standing.

The children came at the eighth hour, the way they came every day. Eleven of them, from the ward side, down the chalk the line was run across, and then along the lane to the landing. A girl of about nine went in front. The lane was nine feet wide and the day was dry and the copper was under it somewhere.

Tomas put the bread crust in his pocket and came down.

---

The foreman was a heavy man with a Crown issue spade and a Crown issue bar and forearms that had been forearms for thirty years, and his name was Brann, and he had signed for the cart at the low-turn culvert on a works order a fortnight old, and he was standing over the peg with his thumb on the cord and looking at the chalk the way a man looks at a thing he has been handed and does not own.

“You cannot peg there,” Tomas said.

He said it at an ordinary volume, in the middle of the lane, where the chalk ran, and there were nine people at the ward mouths and six men at the low end and eleven children coming down, so that the whole of it was heard by about thirty people, which he had decided in the first second was the correct volume and the only one available.

Brann turned round.

“Because?”

“Because that is not ground.” Tomas came up beside the peg and put the flat of his boot on the chalk line and moved it a foot and then put it back, which was the only piece of showing he had that did not involve touching Crown property. “Look at the colour. That line of paler stuff going across the lane at an angle, and it does not go straight because nobody ever dug it straight. That is a filled trench. Sixty years ago somebody opened a service cut along there for the washhouse run and filled it in with rubble and lime and the offcuts of a bell, and the fill has settled about an inch under the lane, so the lane is a lid.”

“A lid,” Brann said.

“A trench has got a crown on it, and the crown is the rubble that nobody bothered to take out, and it is the strongest part of it for about four inches and then it is nothing at all. You are going to dig a renewal trench through the crown of it this week, in the wet, and the crown is going to come down into your new trench like a lid off a pot, and the men standing on it at the time will be standing on nothing, and there will be four reams of Crown order on the whole business and no injury entered anywhere, because the Crown will have a plan.”

Brann did not answer. He looked at the ground. He had the spade butt on his heel and he pressed it down, once, the way a man presses when he is checking a thing he already believes and would like to be wrong about. It went in easily for a foot and then stopped.

“Aye,” he said. “There’s a stone.”

“There’s a stone,” Tomas agreed. “And there’s a good deal more of it than one.”

Somebody at the ward mouth said something and it was not loud enough to hear. The girl of nine went down the lane past all of it without looking at anything, because a child in a lane with a cord across it looks at the ground, not the men.

Brann took his hand off the cord.

“Now,” he said. “You are the maker up the hill.”

“Vale.”

“I know who you are. Every man on this gang knows who you are, because the foreman had it read out to us on the first morning so that nobody would be surprised when the yard door has a Crown seal on it.” He had not said it bitterly. He had said it the way a man states the weather. “How do you know that line is a cut?”

“Two ways. The first is that I can see it, and the second is that my father’s bench-book has a line in it in his own hand about the washhouse run, and the corner of the washhouse is at that mark and not at this one, and the difference between the two is about a yard and a half, and the chalk is a yard and a half out.” He put two fingers on the cord. “I am not doing anything clever and I am not reading anything. I have been in this ward forty years and there is a trench under half of it and I know where most of them are.”

“Then you move the line.”

“I am not moving your line. I am telling you where the ground is and what will happen if you cut it, and it is your peg and your order and your foreman’s hand on it.” Tomas took his hands out of his pockets, deliberately, so that nobody in the lane would have to think about what they were for. “Move it a couple of yards if you want. It is your decision and it should be in your own hand and it should be in your own book, and I will stand here and read what you write and say whether it is fair.”

Hask came off the wall.

He came the whole length of the turn, and the six men came with him, and the crowbar came with him, and he did not hurry, because a man who has been waiting four days does not hurry.

“Vale,” he said. “That is the fourth day you have said something useful in a public place and let somebody else do it.”

“I have said four useful things in a public place,” Tomas said.

“You have said one,” Hask said, “on the fourth day, and you said it standing on a wall eating bread, and there is not one man in this ward who could have said it except you, and the Crown had you in a room in this town on the second day with a wax on your door, and they have had you ever since.” He shifted the crowbar. “That is not a complaint. It is a fact, and it is the whole of my argument, and I have been making it in three rooms for four days and getting a book entry for it. The ward has a man who can read the ground and that man is on a Crown inventory.”

Nobody spoke. The children were gone past the turn and the lane was clear again, in the way a lane is clear, which is not the same as empty, and it was a dry morning with the water out and the millrace making its ordinary noise, and the whole of the thing had been decided in about four minutes by a man with a spade, which was how most of the ground in the world gets decided.

---

Brann moved it.

He did it himself. He pulled the peg, walked the chalk two yards down the lane toward the ward side, set the peg, put the cord back, and then said to the youngest of the gang — nineteen, thin, in a Crown smock three sizes too big for her, with a satchel and a book in it — to write it down.

Perry wrote it down. She wrote the hour, which was nine minutes past the ninth. She wrote the reason, which was that the first mark was over a filled service cut, and then she stopped with her pen on the page and looked up at Tomas, who was standing three feet away with his hands in his pockets like a man at a funeral.

“Put down what he said,” she said. “Word for word. Because in a week somebody in Fourmile is going to ask me why the line moved and I am nineteen and I have a book and not a reason.”

“You want my words.”

“I want the reason in a mouth that is not mine,” Perry said. “That is the whole difference between a note and a fact, sir.”

So Tomas said it again, slowly, in about nine sentences, and she wrote it, and then she read it back to him, and he said it was fair, and Brann took the book and looked at it and put his own initials against her initials and the hour, and then he said:

“That will be my hand as well as hers.”

“I know,” Tomas said. “I saw you initial it.”

“You did not know I would. You stood there and let a girl of nineteen put a Crown foreman’s name on a page because she asked to, and if you had offered, I should have had to think about it.” He shut the book. “Here is the other thing, and I will say it because you have been accurate with me and I am tired of this lane. That mark came off a plan. It came off a plan the office sent up on the eighth day with the works order and a schedule of the ground, and I have had the plan in a satchel since the eighth day and I have not looked at the ground part of it, because I have dug nineteen years and I do not read ground plans. I will find out who drew it. And when I do, I will write it in this book, because I am not going to be the man who found it out and kept it.”

He took the reel and the two men took the bar and the spade and they went up the lane to set out the next section.

---

Tomas sat down on the low wall in the sun and wrote up what he had said and why, in the day-book, in his own hand, with the hour on it, because a day-book entry without an hour is a mood and a day-book entry with an hour is a record.

*Ninth hour, fifteenth day. Told a Crown road gang in the low lane that their first mark stood over a filled service cut, the washhouse run, and that a renewal trench dug through the crown of an old cut will drop the crown and put a man under it. Told them so in the lane and in front of about thirty people and I did not touch their line and did not ask them to stop and did not invite them. Foreman Brann moved the mark two yards toward the ward side at nine minutes past the ninth, on his own hand, and initialled the change with the youngest of the gang, a girl of nineteen called Perry, who asked me to say it again so that the reason would be in a mouth that was not hers. Entered: the first mark came off a plan sent up with the works order on the eighth day. Foreman Brann says he will find out who drew the ground part of that plan and will write it in the gang’s book.*

*I did not ask to be thanked and I was not thanked and I am not owed it. What I asked for was two yards and a record and the foreman’s own hand, and I got all three, and I want it written that this is the first thing I have done in eleven days that was finished before the hour.*

*Cost: the day. I sat in the sun for an hour and the wheelwright’s bell is on the bench finished and I did not start the note I owe the Measure about my own stock, and it is not going to be written this week.*

*And Hask is right and I cannot answer him, so I have put that down. He said the ward has a man who can read the ground and the Crown has had that man on an inventory since the second day. That is the strongest thing anybody has said to me this week and it was said to my face by a man holding a crowbar, and I have no answer to it that is not a lie.*

The clerk was on the lane about two hours after the mark was moved.

He came up from the ward end at about the eleventh hour, a young man with a satchel and the careful walk of somebody who has been told which lane to be careful in, and he had a page with a stamp on it and he asked, before he said anything else, who had a copy of the gang’s book.

Hask said, “He does. Do you want to see it?”

The clerk said, “No, sir,” in a hurry. “I am not here for the gang’s book. I am here because the office has had a report that a mark was moved on the fifteenth day and I have to enter what the office does with that, and there is a field for it.” He had the page out and turned round so it faced Tomas, and Tomas read it upside down, which he could do, and which was a thing to be able to do.

The field said: *cause stated by*.

“Somebody told your foreman to move that mark,” the clerk said. “I am not asking you to tell me about the ground. I am asking who told the foreman. If the foreman moved it on his own judgement then there is no man to enter, and the form says *self*, and the file closes. If a man told him, then the office is required to enter that man, and then the office is required to have a word with him, and I would rather come up the lane and ask than send something.”

Nobody in the lane said anything for a moment. Behind the clerk, at the low end of the turn, six men were still standing where they had been standing since the seventh hour, and the crowbar had not moved.

“He moved it himself,” Tomas said.

“That is not what the foreman’s report says,” the clerk said. He was not being aggressive. He was a young man reading a report. “The foreman’s report says that he was advised of a filled service cut by the maker of the Vale Bellhouse, in the presence of the ward, and that he moved the mark accordingly, and that he was unaware that the plan was wrong. That is what came up the lane at the eleventh hour in a fair hand with a book reference at the foot of it. I did not write it. I am only here to complete the field.”

The lane was extremely quiet.

“A fair hand,” Tomas said.

“Copied out fair, sir. From the gang’s book.”

Tomas looked at the millrace and thought about four things, in this order. That the girl had been right about the difference between a note and a fact, and that being right had cost her nothing and him everything. That Brann had initialled a page which was copied out fair, and that Brann had said in advance that he would not be the man who found out and kept it, and had not thought about the office on the other end of the same page. That a man in Kells had spent eleven days learning that being right in a lane is worth nothing unless somebody writes it down, and that he had just watched the last part of that lesson arrive, in a fair hand, up from Fourmile in the space of a morning.

And that he could not have stopped it if he had thought of it, because the whole of the bargain he had struck with this town in the first ten days was that things get written down, and he had built the habit himself, and a man cannot complain of the first fruit of a tree he planted with both hands.

“The maker of the Vale Bellhouse is Tomas Vale,” he said. “Enter that. Enter it exactly, and enter the hour, and enter that the ground was shown to me on a day-book entry in my father’s hand and that the Crown plan was not read, and put my name against the whole of it. And then come and find me, because I am not going to be entered into a Crown return as a man who was *advised* and left there. If the office wants my reasons, it can have them in my own words in its own file, and I will not be summarised.”

The clerk wrote. He wrote for a while, and then he read part of it back, and Tomas corrected two words, and the clerk struck them out and wrote the right ones, and the whole of it took nine minutes in a lane with a bar in it.

Then the clerk put the page away and said, to nobody in particular, that he was sorry, in a way that was not entirely an apology, and went back up the lane to the ward end.

---

Hask watched him go and then said, without raising his voice:

“That is twice now that you have been right in a lane and had it copied out fair.”

“I know.”

“And the third time it will be in a file in Fourmile with a number on it, and then it will be a thing you did, and not a thing that happened.” He lifted the crowbar off his knees. “I am still here, Vale. Six men and a bar and a day that has gone very badly for me, and no court in this town will say one word to my face, and I am not going anywhere. I want you to say so where my men can hear it, and I want you to say what you are going to do about it, and I want a real answer and not the one about not deciding things.”

There were twenty-odd people in the lane by then, because a clerk writing at a wall is a thing people stop for.

Tomas thought about it for long enough that it was conspicuous.

“I will not ask anybody to close this lane,” he said. “I have no power to ask and no standing to ask and if I asked, half the people in this lane would be standing here because the bellmaker asked them to and not because they wanted to. What I am going to do is write down what is in the ground, today, in words a person can check, and I am going to write it the same way I wrote this morning, with the hour on it, and I am going to give a copy of it to the foreman and to the watch and to the board, and that is all I am going to do about your bar.”

“That is a coward’s answer.”

“It is an answer that costs me a page instead of a person,” Tomas said. “You are welcome to call it what you like. I have had eleven days of being the man who decides things and I will tell you the results: three children out of a lane and a seal on a door. If I stand in this lane now and tell this ward what to do about a crowbar, then in a month there will be six things I have told this ward to do and five of them will be mine.”

Nobody clapped, which was correct, because a lane is not a court. Hask looked at him for a long moment, and then shouldered the crowbar and went back down to the turn, and the six men went with him, and they were still there at dusk and they were still there when the gang came off the lane at the seventh hour with the trench opened two yards over.

He wrote her a note on the back of a leaf of the day-book, there and then, while she held the book open, and it said that she would be asked to stand where a sound of his could be heard and to write down how far it came and what anybody said, and that he was asking her because the foreman had asked him to say things where other people could hear them, and that she was not obliged and could hand it back at any hour without giving a reason. She read it twice and wrote the hour on the back of it in her own hand and gave it back, and it is a piece of paper in Kells and the reason he cannot take it back off her is on the next page of this one.

The children came back up the lane at the twelfth hour in a different order and the same lane, and Tomas counted them because he could not stop himself, and there were eleven.

Which is the part Tomas wrote down, because it is the part that mattered: the gang dug from the ninth hour to the seventh on the fifteenth day, three feet down, in a lane where eleven children walked to the landing twice a day, and at the bottom of the new trench the first shovelful was old lime and a lump of bell offcut with the casting seam still on it, from a bell somebody in this town poured before Tomas was born, lying three feet under a Crown renewal on the eleventh day of a year when nobody had asked the ward.
