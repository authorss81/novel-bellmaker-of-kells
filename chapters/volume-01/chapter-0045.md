# Chapter 0045 — The Ward’s Answer

The ward board had the district’s second supplementary return on its own table by the eighth hour of the morning of the twenty-fifth, because a Crown officer had delivered it to every door in the ward in the two days since it was nailed up, and Mrs Wilde’s household at one hundred and eight was the only one Nell Fenn had to ask for twice.

Three of the returns had come back, and all three of them are on the board before anybody is in the lane, and Nell had been up since five and had put three flat stones on three sheets and had copied three house numbers onto the back of the pinned schedule in her own hand, which is a thing she does when she cannot make herself do anything else with a morning.

**One hundred and fourteen, from Absalom Teage**, who had written his own form out himself with a carter’s honesty and had left the reason field longer than anybody else’s, four lines of it, and had put no name in the witness field at all and had written across the bottom: *There was nobody in the room when I wrote this, so there is nobody to put there, and if you want a name, come to 114 and stand in the doorway and ask me and write down what I say and it will be my name in my hand.*

**One hundred and thirty-three, from Mrs Corris**, whose sheet said only: *A surveyor, at my door, at an hour I choose. I am sixty-five.*

**And one hundred and twenty-one**, from a woman of about fifty who had let Nell and Mercy Slee into her front room on the afternoon before and had shut the door behind them and had said that her husband’s name was in the column and had not been in the house for two years and that she was not going to be the one to tell him in a market court, and who had then left the intention box of the Crown’s form **empty** and written in the reason field: *Nobody in this house is going anywhere, and on the tenth day of this month a Crown officer read a line off a paper at this door and took my boy of eleven off the strength of it, and nobody in this house has been asked one thing since, and if you want a name in the witness column you may have mine, and I am alive.*

One hundred and nineteen was not among them. One hundred and eight was not among them.

So the first column of what the ward was about to say had one household in it, and it was not that one, and the one that was not in it had cost a great deal more than the one that was in it, and nobody in the ward had worked that out yet except the two women it belonged to.

---

“You have not got your two people,” Mercy Slee said, at the board, at the seventh hour, with about thirty in the lane.

“No.”

“You said on the fourteenth that you would ask two people whether they would stand beside you, and you told me you would tell me when you had asked, and you have not told me, which means you have not asked, and it is the twenty-fifth.”

“I have asked one.”

“Which one?”

“Mercy.”

“That is not standing beside you, girl, that is a woman who has been in this from the beginning and whose house is on a Crown return and who has a boy of nine.” Mercy Slee put her arms inside her shawl. “I will stand beside you. I have decided to and I have not said so out loud yet, and I am saying it now so that I cannot be talked out of it by being asked nicely. Write it down: Mercy Slee, of one hundred and nineteen, will stand beside Nell Fenn on the twenty-fifth and has asked for nothing and will be named in the ward’s book and does not want to be thanked for it, because being thanked is a debt and she does not want one.”

“And the second?”

“That is your business and I am going to be in that room and I am not going to help you pick, because you will turn round and ask me who would do the most good at the front of a step and that is a different question and I am not going to answer it for you.”

Nell went down the lane at about half past seven and stood in a front room at one hundred and eight with seven people in it who were not of the house and asked a woman sitting in a good chair with her hands in her lap.

Mrs Wilde said no for about nine seconds.

Then she said: “What is the second person for.”

“To stand at the front of a market court at about the ninth hour on the twenty-fifth and say one thing, and to have your name in the ward’s book under it, and to have your house number in it, and to go home afterwards while forty people say things about you on the way.”

“Nonsense,” said Mrs Wilde. “Nobody stands at the front of a court and says one thing. They stand at the front of a court and say something and it gets told back to them a month later with a different word in it. I have been that household. I have been the household somebody told a story about for two years.”

“Yes,” Nell said. “That is exactly what happens and I cannot stop it and I am not going to pretend to you that I can, and I am not going to ask you to stand at the front. I am going to ask you to stand at the side, where you stood on the twenty-first in front of the ward board, with your back half to the lane, and to read out the thing you read out on the twenty-first, once, and then to sit down, and to be named in the ward’s book as having read it, and to go home while forty people say things about you on the way.”

Mrs Wilde looked at the fire that had gone out and had not been relit.

“Say the whole of it back to me,” she said. “The whole arrangement, all the way to the end of the morning.”

So Nell said it back to her, in order, in about a hundred words, including the part about the third column, which Mrs Wilde had not understood on the twenty-first and which took eleven minutes on its own, and at the end of it Mrs Wilde said:

“Then I will do it. Not at the front. At the side, with my back to the lane, and I will read the thing once and I will sit down, and if anybody comes up to me afterwards I am going to say *I have said what I came to say*, and that will be the whole of what I say to any of them.”

“Right.”

“And you will say my name first,” she said. “Not Nell Fenn’s. Not the girl’s. If I am going to be looked at by forty people in a market court I am going to be looked at by forty people who know which door I came out of. You put *Ellen Wilde, of one hundred and eight, dead two years, in her own hand in a column that is not cleared between rounds*, and you read it before I stand up, and then I stand up and read my own part, and by the time I am standing up the room will already know what I am and there will be nothing left for them to find out.”

“That is the best thing anybody has said to me in a month,” Nell Fenn said. “That is better than anything I have thought of and I have thought of it for two days.”

“Do not tell me it is good,” Mrs Wilde said. “Tell the girl at a hundred and nineteen, and then come back here at the eighth hour, because I am not going to a market court having had no breakfast and I am not going to be a woman who faints in front of the Crown.”

---

They sat in the market court at the ninth hour with about two hundred and thirty people in it, which was about a hundred more than had come on the twentieth and about a hundred fewer than had been standing in the lane on the twenty-first, and the court had not been adjourned to do it, and Ferrow had been given a quarter of an hour’s warning and had put an extra bench out for the ward’s use and had not been asked to.

Oren Pike stood where captains stand, which is at the side and a step back, and said four sentences before he said anything else.

“I am going to do a thing at the front of this proceeding that I have never done in this room in eleven years and I am doing it because it is the only honest shape available. I will read the ward’s answer and I will certify the room. I will certify that the three columns were stated, in these words, in this room, at this hour, with this many people in it. **I will not certify the contents.** I am a watch captain and I am one person, and a certification is a witness, and if I put my name to what four hundred and eleven people said then this room will have turned itself into one household, and that is the thing we have spent nine days learning not to do, and I will not do it at the front of my own court.”

“That is new,” somebody said.

“It is not new, it is written down,” Oren said, “on the fourteenth day, in a column headed with three answers and a fourth column for the withdrawals, and the whole of what has happened in this ward for a fortnight is that people have been slower to do this than I have.”

Then Nell Fenn read the answer out, and it is the shortest thing she has ever read in a public room and it is the only one she has ever read that she did not have to argue about first.

*That the ward of Kells, answering the question put to it in the notice of the twenty-first day, states three things, and asks that each of them be kept as a separate return and that none of them be merged with any other.*

*First: the households set out in the first column below, which have stated in their own words that they are removing a person under the age of seventeen from this ward before the twenty-eighth day, and to whom, and by what means. Each of those is one household. Each may be certified on its own. The ward holds the words in the household’s own hand and will deliver them.*

*Second: the households set out in the second column below, which have stated that they are removing nobody. Each of those is one household. Each may be certified on its own.*

*Third: the third column is empty. The households whose lines are in neither column above have not stated an intention. The ward will not state an intention for them and the district may not certify an absence, because a certification is a witness, and a witness is a person, and the third column is not a person. If the district requires a number in the third column, the ward will give the district the number and will give it nothing else.*

*And the ward asks that the three be kept in three columns in whatever office receives them, and that if they are merged, the page be sent back to this court unopened.*

Nell read it out, and then she said the part she had not put in the writing, in her own voice, in the room.

“There is nothing clever about that. I want you to know what it is actually for, because I have been asked twice since the seventh hour and I am going to answer once and properly. It is not a refusal. **A refusal is a sentence and a sentence gets merged.** If this ward wrote *the ward will not remove any person under seventeen*, that is a sentence, and in about a fortnight it will be in a file as the answer of Kells, and it will be the answer of Kells whether we meant it or not, and the seven households in the third column will have been spoken for by a girl of sixteen without their asking.

“An empty column is not a sentence. **An empty column is a number that is missing, and a number that is missing can be counted.** Seven is a number. I have not put seven in the paper because I will not hand anybody a figure they could enter, and the count is in this room and anybody who wants it can get it off the schedule on the board themselves, and if they are too frightened to do that then it is the right answer to give them.

“Now the part Mrs Wilde came for.”

And Mrs Wilde of one hundred and eight stood at the side of the clerk’s table with her back half to the lane and read out the question she had read on the twenty-first, once, without a slate, and in her own words, and sat down.

And there were about two hundred and thirty people in that market court who went absolutely silent, which was not planned and which Nell Fenn has thought about a great deal since.

---

Then the columns.

The first column had one household in it and Nell said the number out and read the household’s four lines and the four lines were about a boy of nine who is not named on their line and has never been named on it, and who was going to Fourmile on the twenty-sixth in a cart because the Crown’s office had put a box on a form with his name in it and a space for where he goes, and nobody had asked the house anything, and the house had filled it in itself.

The second column had twenty-five households in it and Nell read the number and did not read the names, and somebody at the back stood up and asked for the names, and Mercy Slee said, from the step, “No. If you want to know what a house said you go and ask the house. That is the entire arrangement and it is why the number is the only thing in it.”

The third column was empty and she read that out too, in the same voice, and then somebody said *seven*, out loud, in about eleven different voices at once, because they had done it off the board themselves.

“Seven,” Nell Fenn said. “On the hands. There were seven hands, and I did not put a figure on the paper and I am not going to. And somebody at the back of this room has been off the schedule on the board and got thirty-three, and that is a different number and it is a true number, and both of them are in the court book now, because both of them are true and they are about different things. One of them is a number of people in this room. The other one is a number of lines on a Crown page that belong to a house in this ward, and one and twenty-five and seven is thirty-three, and there are forty-one lines on that board, and the eight that are missing are lines with no house in this ward behind them at all, and I have not named them and I am not going to, because naming them is the Crown’s work and has been since the ninth day of this month and it is not going to become the ward’s on my account.

“An empty column can be counted two ways and both counts are true and neither of them is a sentence. A sentence is the one thing a Crown office has never once had to check. That is what the empty column is for.”

And the afternoon went on for five hours, and what happened in it was that between about thirty and forty households came into that court one at a time, over five hours, and said one of three things, and each of them had to say it themselves, and a Crown officer who was in the room could write any of it down and did.

And nothing in that court was merged by anybody, and the person who noticed that first was a Crown copyist from Fourmile who had come down for the day with the district, and who was not on anybody’s list and who had no reason to be there except that his office had sent him, and who said, in about the fourth hour, to a clerk at the side table:

“If I file this, I file three returns. Three. I have been filing one for a ward for eleven years and I have never once had three and I do not have a box for three.”

“Have you a box for none?” the clerk said.

“I have a box for none,” he said. “None is a figure.”

---

And at the sixth hour of the evening, in about two hundred people, with a Crown officer in the room and the clerk’s pen going, **Mrs Corris of one hundred and thirty-three put her hand up and asked for the other thing.**

“The other thing,” she said. “The surveyor. Rudd’s surveyor. I want it, and I want it in writing that I asked for it in a full room, and I want it in this ward’s book that I asked for it, and I would like the district to have it in a form with a date on it, because I have had two years of Crown things with no dates on them from that door and I am finished.”

The room made the noise a room makes.

Nell Fenn did not look at Mrs Wilde and Mrs Wilde did not look at Nell Fenn and Mercy Slee, at the side, in the last twenty minutes of a five-hour proceeding, put her hand over her own mouth and then took it away again, which is the only thing she did in that room in five hours that was not procedure.

Mrs Corris went on, standing up, in a shawl, in about two hundred and thirty people.

“I am not asking for a person because I have faith in her. I am asking because on the twenty-first I had a name put in a Crown column by a Crown clerk in a public room, and I have not worked out yet whether that clerk did me a kindness or did the district a service, and I have not the wit to work it out, and I am sixty-five. And a person who comes to a door and writes down that she was in the room is a thing I can check. A return is not a thing I can check. So I will take the return and I will take the person, and I do not care one half of one farthing which of them does the work, and I would like it entered that I said that in a full market court and that nobody stopped me.”

“Entered,” said Ferrow, at the clerk’s table, writing, with the hour against it, in the market court book, in three hands.

“And I would like the last thing entered too,” Mrs Corris said, “and it is mine and I am saying it because nobody else in this room is going to. A hundred and thirty-three is in the second column and I put it there with my own mouth this afternoon, and I am also taking a Crown certification, and both of those are true at the same time in the same afternoon, and I am not going to be asked about it by anybody. If anybody in this ward wants to come and tell me what I have done, they can come to my door and say it to my face, and I will make them tea, and I will listen, and that will be the end of it.”

Nobody came to her door that evening. That is on the record because Captain Pike entered it, and he entered it with a note that he had asked Ferrow to ask four people in the lane and had been told *no, and why not*, and that the four reasons were good and that he had written all four down.

---

Tomas Vale was in that court from the second hour and was not asked to speak once, and said nothing at all, and rang nothing, and did not go near a bell.

Mercy Slee told him so at the end, on the step, while the room was emptying.

“You were not asked.”

“No.”

“I want that written down that you were not asked, and not that you stood there being silent, which is a different thing and a worse one and is what you would have called it.” She had her arms inside her shawl. “There were two hundred and thirty people in there for five hours and not one of them turned round to look at the man with the bell, and that did not happen because you did anything. It happened because Mrs Corris put her hand up and got what she asked for, and Mrs Wilde stood at the side of a clerk’s table with her back to the lane and read out her own question, and a girl of sixteen gave seven away for nothing.

“You are not the instrument, Tomas. I know it is the whole of your month that you are, and I know you think you are going to be needed on the twenty-eighth morning and that you have got two keepers and a bell that is too bright. Well. There are two hundred and thirty people in this town who have spent today finding out that they can be, and eleven of them have told me so on the way out, and not one of them mentioned you, and that is not an insult and I want it written in your book as a fact and not as a lesson, because you will turn a fact into a lesson and I have watched you do it four times.”

He wrote it down that night in four words, in his own book, which is not this one, and the four words were: *Nobody asked. Nobody turned round.*

---

The district’s reply came at the last hour, and it was not a notice and it was not a form, and it was the shortest paper the commission had put on that board in three weeks, and it was nailed up by a Crown officer who read it out to the people still in the lane and then read it out again because nobody believed it the first time.

*Received, the twenty-fifth day: the ward’s answer of three columns, and the third column empty, and the district notes that it may certify within any column and may not certify the three together, and that a certified answer is a return, and the district accepts that.*

*And further notified: that the renewal upon route L-4 is on the twenty-eighth day as posted, and that* **Crown Officer Ambrose Rudd** *of the commission is instructed to attend at the ward board of Kells on the morning of the twenty-eighth day, and to enter the court, and to be attended by the watch, and to produce at that hour the following, that is to say:*

*The full return for the renewal of the twenty-eighth, entire.*

*The supplementary returns, first and second series, delivered to the ward, with the house numbers of those who have returned them.*

*And the authority of the commission to ascertain what works of metal are in the ground of the yard of the Vale Bellhouse, being a workshop named in the notice of the fourteenth day of this month as the place in which the practice of this ward originated, with such works to be opened in the presence of the ward and entered in three books.*

And under it, in the small hand, a fourth line that nobody read out because it was under the fold:

*And that the district requires an answer, in the watch’s hand, to the question whether any bell in this ward is bound to a claim, and if so by whom, and the district does not accept* none *as an answer and does not accept* none *as a refusal and notes that the register of the seventeenth returned* none *twelve times.*

Nell Fenn read that fourth line out at the third hour of the morning of the twenty-sixth, alone, in the lane, with a lamp, to about nine people who had come out because she was at the board, and then she wrote it out in her own book in her own hand with the hour, and then she did not write anything else, because there was nothing else to write, and because in about forty hours there is going to be a Crown officer at that board with a lawful paper and a list of what he is to bring, and a man in this ward is going to have to stand up and say what the bell is for.
