# Chapter 0033 — The Road That Is Not Closed

The question Tomas put to the west road bridge keeper on the sixteenth day was not *can the road be closed*, and it took him until the third hour of the afternoon to work out that it had not been the question he wanted to ask, and that the question he had wanted to ask was the one every person in the Low Ward had been asking him for four days, which was *is it safe to send a cart along it on the twenty-first*.

Wyman Dace was on the bridge because Wyman Dace was always on the bridge, and he had three slates on the parapet, and the first thing he did when Tomas came up the ramp was turn the third one face down.

“I have written the wind,” Dace said. “I write the wind every day and I have never once written the word safe, and I am going to tell you the reason in one sentence and then we will do the work. A bridge is not a road. A road can be said to be passable by a person who has walked it. A bridge is a thing with a deck and a sill and a parapet and a keeper, and a keeper who says his bridge is safe has told you he does not expect to be asked why afterwards.”

He put the slate down on the parapet face up, so that Tomas could read it, and did not read it out.

*I have walked the west road twice, both times before the ninth hour and both times dry. I have not been on it wet at any hour this month. The bank at the mill narrows is cut to a foot six and the cut has not been dressed in the four years I have kept the bridge, and the steps above it are the town’s and not mine, and I do not keep the steps.*

“That is the honest sheet,” Dace said. “That is what I know. Now go and walk the three narrows and come back and tell me what you found, and I will tell you what it means, and it will not be the same thing, because what it means is mine to say and what you found is yours.”

Tomas walked them.

The first was a quarter mile up from the toll shed, where two carts could pass and nothing else could pass anything else, and where a wagon that had been stopped had to be reversed twenty yards to a widening that could hold one wheel off the road. The second was the old ford approach, where the road came off the hill and dropped, and where a loaded wagon gathering way would not be able to pull itself up if its offside wheel went down. The third was the mill narrows, a hundred yards short of Dace’s bridge, and it was the only one that mattered, and the reason it mattered was that above it, in the bank, there were stone steps, cut, worn in the middle, going up to a cottage, and Dace was right that the steps were the town’s.

The bank at the mill narrows is cut to a foot and a half above the bed of the road for about thirty feet, and it is a soft bank, and the road there is a shelf cut into a hillside, and a loaded wagon stopped in that thirty feet has exactly three things it can do. It can reverse. It cannot turn, because there is no width. And it cannot go forward, because forward is the bridge, and a wagon on that bridge that has taken its own width off the shelf is a wagon on a bridge, and Dace keeps the bridge.

“A chain,” Tomas said, when he got back. “A bar and a chain at the head of the thirty feet, and a man with a lamp. A cart goes through because a cart is five foot on the shafts and can be pulled up the bank and down again in four minutes. A loaded wagon stops, and reverses, and goes back up the hill, and if it reverses badly it is a wagon on your bridge and it is your problem and not mine.”

“It is my problem and mine entirely,” Dace said, “and I have never had a loaded wagon in that thirty feet in four years, and I have had four that came out of that turn too fast off the ford approach and not one out of the narrows, because a driver who comes off the ford fast takes the bank at the ford and then finds the narrows narrow, and the ones who come slow come slow all the way. That is a bridgekeeper’s whole craft, that is, and I have never once been able to put it in a sentence a magistrate would read.”

“Can you put it in a sentence a lane will read?”

“I can put it in a sentence I will stand behind and I will say it in a market lane and a man may write it down.”

---

Foreman Crane had the market landing and was the only man in Kells who could close a lane, and he said so himself, twice, in the first minute, in a way that made Tomas understand he had been waiting all day for somebody to ask him.

“I can close the market lane in an hour,” Crane said. “I can close it from the shed end with a bar and two men and I can do it without the commission and without the reeve and nobody in this district can say a word to me about it, and I have done it four market days running for a flooding landing and twice for a funeral. So do not come to me and ask me whether I can. Ask me whether I will.”

“Will you?”

“No,” Crane said. “And I will tell you the whole reason and then you will not ask me twice, which is what I actually want.”

He put his hands on the landing rail and looked out at the water.

“Six days ago there was a Crown round at four in the morning and three children went out of this ward on the back of a page, and I carried a boy of nine onto a tailboard and I have not been right about it since. And on the seventh day I stood on this landing and asked this ward to be warned with one sound, and three men answered, and one of them refused, and I have been trying since then to work out what I would do if the next time came and I was the only man here who could shut a lane in an hour. And what I have worked out is that I would do it, and I would be right to do it, and it would be the worst mistake I have ever made, and I would not be the one who decided it. I would have been handed it.” He turned round. “So the answer is no to a warning, because the last warning in this ward was carried by a Crown clapper and I have still not worked out whose hand that was. The answer is yes to a road, because a road is a road.”

---

So Tomas wrote the road down, and it took him the rest of the afternoon and most of the evening, and he wrote it in a hand that was not good and did not improve, and it is four hundred words and none of them are an opinion.

*What is on the west road of this town on the sixteenth day, walked twice, dry both times, by Tomas Vale, and read by Wyman Dace, keeper of the west road bridge, who has walked it twice himself before the ninth hour.*

*One. There are three narrows between the toll shed and the bridge, and they are the width of a cart and not the width of a wagon, and this is a fact about the ground and not about either of us.*

*Two. At the mill narrows, for about thirty feet, the bank on the uphill side stands a foot and a half above the road, and the bank is soft, and above the bank there are cut steps belonging to the town and not to the keeper of the bridge, worn in the middle, going up to a cottage.*

*Three. A bar and a chain at the head of those thirty feet, with a man and a lamp, will stop a loaded wagon and will not stop a cart. A cart can be drawn up the bank and down again in about four minutes. A stopped wagon has three ways out and one of them is the bridge.*

*Four. A closed road would be a fact in an office by tomorrow morning. If the Crown does not want the west road used on the twenty-first it will be because a Crown officer has asked a question and got an answer about a ward that shuts its own road in a week of a round. I do not know whether the Crown will ask. I know what an answer to that question costs, which is that a man in Fourmile will have a sentence in his hand saying that the Low Ward declines to keep its own highway open, and that sentence will be true, and I will have to hold it.*

*Five. Nothing in this page is a claim that anybody is in danger. Nobody has told me anybody is in danger. This page is about a road.*

He wrote it out twice, and gave one to Dace and one to Crane, and neither of them said anything about it, and neither of them said no, and neither of them said yes, and Tomas went home at the ninth hour to a workshop with a Crown seal on the door and a damp strip on the floor and had the particular feeling he had come to recognise, which was the feeling of a man who has done the correct thing and found that the correct thing has no market at all.

---

Crane asked him the question on the sixteenth day at the second hour of the afternoon, at the landing, in the cold, with the water out and the millrace making its noise.

“Who pays for a road a keeper has closed?”

“I do not know.”

“Wrong, and you are going to find out, and I want you to find it out before the evening, because if nobody finds it out then this is a favour to one household out of four hundred, and a favour to one household out of four hundred is a thing a Crown officer can put in a return in nine words, and then it is a bribe.” Crane was not being dramatic. He had a rope round his shoulder and a coil of marline in his hand. “A closed road is a loss. Loss goes to somebody. The toll is my toll-keeper’s, and Bede Marrow is a Crown officer and a man of this town at the same time, and if he loses four days of tolls on my word he is going to have to explain it to somebody, and he is not going to be able to explain *three keepers said so in a lane* to a man who is paid not to understand things like that.”

“Then it goes to the market court,” Tomas said, “which has a small fund and has never once spent it, and which will spend it, and which will write the figure on a page and read the figure out twice, and the figure will be a real sum of money that eleven people in this town can check.”

Crane looked at him for a second.

“That is the first answer anybody has given me in four days that is not a favour.”

---

They said it at dusk on the sixteenth day, in the market lane, on the cobbles outside the fish market, where about sixty people had gathered for no reason anybody could later agree on, and the words were said by three people separately, in three sentences, each into a column of its own, and nobody merged them.

The first was Alis Marrow, who kept the toll shed with her husband Bede and who had come down the hill for the thing she had heard about the mill narrows and the steps, and who said it standing up, in one sentence, without a pause in it, because she had rehearsed it on the walk down and did not intend to waste the rehearsal.

“I am not closing a road for a warning and I do not know anything about a warning. I am closing it because my mother is seventy-nine and she goes down those four steps four times a day to the mill and back and a loaded wagon that takes that bank once will take the top of them off, and I have asked you for that and nobody has done anything about it in eleven years.”

The second was Foreman Crane, and he gave it in the words he had already given Tomas, and the difference between his sentence and the third one’s was that his sentence contained the word *road* four times and the word *warned* once, and that a man standing at the back of that crowd could have told from the weight of those two words exactly how Foreman Crane felt about the distinction.

“Nothing is to go up the west road with a load on it this side of the ford, from the bell at the shed to the bar at the mill, and the reason is the bank and the steps, and the warning is not my affair and I have said why and I will say it again to anybody in this town who asks me.”

The third was Wyman Dace, and he gave it as a slate, in a flat hand, standing on the kerb, and he read it and then gave it away to the clerk who came out of the fish market with a lamp, which was Ferrow, the market court clerk, and Dace gave him the slate and said:

“Write it down as I said it and not as a nice way. It is mine and you are holding it.”

*That I have walked the west road twice today before the ninth hour, dry, and I write the wind and not the word safe; and that a bar and a chain at the mill narrows will stop a loaded wagon and will not stop a cart; and that I keep the bridge and not the bank and not the steps, and that if a wagon is stopped in the thirty feet it is a wagon on my bridge, and I say this because I have kept that bridge four years and I have not lost it, and I do not intend to lose it in a week in the year of the round.*

Sixty people in a lane heard three sentences in three columns, and Ferrow wrote them in three columns, and did not read them out loud, because he had been told on the fourteenth day what his one column sounded like and he was not going to do it in a lane with sixty people in it.

The bar and the chain went up at the head of the mill narrows at ten minutes past the seventh hour and a man with a lamp stood there from dark, and the first cart went through at quarter past and the second at twenty past, and the fish market was open and the landing worked and the west road was half closed by three sentences that a market court clerk held on a slate in a hand that was not his own.

And in the ferry road the same evening, before the bar was up, a Crown officer of about thirty, with a satchel and no black case, asked a carter and then a housekeeper and then a woman with a barrow whether the Low Ward had shut its own road, and whether it had shut it on the Crown’s account, and whether anybody had been ordered to. And Ivo Prentice told him, in front of nine people, that the ward had shut a road on its own account with a keeper’s chain and no Crown order anywhere in it, and that a man could check the watch book if he wanted, and that there was a Crown cart still across the culvert at the low turn and it was a fortnight old and would be a fortnight older before it was moved.

Prentice was not wrong and it was the worst thing that could have happened, because a Crown officer who goes back to Fourmile with the fact that the ward closed its own road in a week of a round has been given the sentence Tomas had predicted on the previous page, except that Prentice had supplied the rest of it himself.

---

The household that was moving goods was at 91, in the upper ward, and Tomas had been told about it on the fifteenth day by a woman at the washhouse who had said *the Hodlers have been packing since the second hour*, and who had not been able to give a reason, and had said so three times in three different ways.

He went up there at the eighth hour on the sixteenth and Marda Hodler was standing in her own lane with a rope tied round three sacks and a boy of four holding the end of the rope, and she was not ashamed and she was not sorry, and she told him before he asked, which he had learned to expect and which twice this week had been the only way anybody had told him anything true.

“I am moving three sacks of linen, my mother’s, and two chests, into my sister’s at Fourmile, and I am going on Friday with the cart, and I have told four other houses I am going on Friday with the cart.”

“Why are you going on Friday?”

“Because a young man told me to.”

“Who?”

“A young man behind a counter in Fourmile. He was not in uniform and he was not one of yours, he was one of theirs, the ones with the ink.” She took a breath, and Tomas felt the ground come up under him the way it had twice in eleven days, in a trench and in his own yard. “I went in on the fourteenth to ask what happens to a house that is not on their paper, and he said it was not permitted to say. Which is what I expected. And then he said — and he said it kindly, and he said it the way a man says a thing he has said many times — he said *the round comes at first light, and they will want the children out of the ward, and the ones that are out of the ward on the day are the ones that are not moved.*”

Nobody in the lane said anything for a moment. Behind Marda Hodler, up the hill, about four hundred and eleven people had spent six days in various rooms not saying the word out loud, and a woman had walked ten miles round and been told it by a clerk’s mouth, and had come home and started packing.

“What is your first name,” Tomas said, and then stopped, and said, before he had got the rest of it out: “I am sorry. That is the wrong question. I am not going to ask you that and I am not going to ask it later. I am asking: did you write it down anywhere, or was it word of mouth?”

“Word of mouth,” Marda Hodler said. “And I have said it to four houses, and two of them have started, and one of them is Siss Larke at 96 and she has done more of it than I have because she has a boy of fourteen and I have a boy of four.”

“Then hear me and hear the shape of it, and do not put my name on it, because I will not be standing next to a sentence about a clerk when this goes wherever it is going. Nobody has told any house in this ward to move a child before the twenty-first. There is no order. There is no notice. I have been in a Crown lane, a Crown trench and a Crown yard for three days and I have not seen one word about children leaving this ward, and I will write down that I have not seen it in my own hand and I will give the page to the watch and the board and it will be the fourth thing this week that I have written down instead of decided. And now hear the other half, which is worse.

“A clerk at a counter said a true thing to you kindly, and he said it because his office has a practice of entering answers before it serves them, and that is on a piece of paper in this town and it is on the board, and you are the only person in this ward who has heard the practice come out of a mouth, and I cannot take it back off you, and the four houses you have told are the cost of my finding out that you had been told. And if I ask you to stop telling people, I am asking you to lie to your neighbours, and I will not do that, and I would think less of you if you agreed to it, and if somebody asks me in four days what I did about the Hodlers moving to Fourmile I am going to have to say that I did nothing, because there was nothing I could have done that was not worse.”

He shut the day-book and did not write it down, which was the correct answer, and cost him something to be trusted with, and he has written that last part down.

Marda Hodler looked at him for a while.

“They are taking the children,” she said. “You are telling me it is not true, and you are a maker, and you are the man who put my house on a Crown piece of paper without asking, so you will understand that I do not care a great deal about your not knowing.”

“I would not either,” Tomas said. “I am telling you that I have gone to the only length I can go to, which is to write down that I have not seen it, and to put my name on that page so that in a month, if a round comes and no child leaves any house, there is a page in this town that says a man in this lane on the sixteenth day could not find a word of it in the Crown’s hand.”

And Marda Hodler said the thing that made the whole of the day worth its cost, which was:

“Then I will wait until Friday. Not because I believe you. Because it costs me four days to wait and it costs me everything to be wrong.”

She went back to her packing. Tomas went down the hill at the ninth hour and got about forty yards, and a Crown road hand came past him going up with a spade over his shoulder — the youngest of the gang, nineteen, Perry, with a satchel and a book — and she said, without stopping, that the trench was filling again and that the drain channel had gone soft on the ward side and that she was going to have to write about it, and he said, *write about it*, and she said, *I am, that is what I said*, and went up the hill to the Crown gang’s lodging with the only thing in this ward that was getting better.
