# Chapter 0020 — The Hearing Before the Hearing

The notice on the market court board had been up for nine hours, and by the time the light went the town had decided what it thought of it, which was that the Crown was going to take the bell, and that this time the taking would be lawful, and that the question worth arguing about was not the law but whether a bell that could be sounded in the court with a man saying no in it was worth keeping at all.

Tomas spent the evening on the ground floor of the watch house, because that was where the paper was.

Oren had laid the whole of it out on the long table in the order in which it had been made, and he had done it without being asked, and when Tomas said so he said, “It is not a favour. If I put the documents in an order that flatters you, then when somebody objects to the order I have already agreed with them.”

The date board from the market court: the filing date three days before the carrier sounded, the route, the inclusive count, the clerk's broken seal.

The joint receipt, signed by two men who disliked each other, naming one object and no other.

The three separate witness slips from the first day, with the boy's line marked as a boy's account and not as a finding.

The route fragment, the crush-cut tag, the J. Vale sign-out, and the two-note account of the gate bell.

The rope, taken in the market court in the rain, with three corrections initialled and the thin sound entered that Oren had not wanted.

The three entries from yesterday, in three columns, with the withdrawals in a fourth. The withdrawal procedure, receipted. The Measure's conditional challenge, with its two conditions, the refused key, and the limitation that the inspection had been made without sounding the bell.

Tomas stood at the end of it and did the arithmetic he had come to do, which was not about guilt.

“The order came first,” he said. “The order was filed three days before the carrier rang. I have said that in a public board, in a court, and to a guild, and it has never once been disputed in writing.”

“It has been disputed in the yard, twice, by a man who wanted it to be later,” Oren said.

“Then let it be disputed in the yard. It is not disputed here.” He moved along the table. “The line was cut and rejoined with a bypass after the carrier rang. The service ticket says the clapper was taken out and the carrier disconnected after a warning was filed, and that no keeper answer was recorded. That is a maintenance record and it is a very good piece of paper for us, and it is also a record in which nobody's name appears at all.”

“Which is what a man looks like when he is not looking.”

“Which is what a room looks like when nobody is in it.” Tomas put his hand flat on the table without touching any of it. “The court bell is iron, has a rope, and has no line, and Jerm inspected it, and the Crown says the bell sounded in this ward carrying a claim is a signal apparatus in a workshop. So they will call it connected. It is not connected to anything. It hangs from a beam.”

“Then let them prove it.”

“They will not want to prove it,” Tomas said. “They will want to say it. There is a difference and it is the whole of the next four hours.”

The Crown's man arrived at the seventh hour with two officers, and he did not come to argue about the bell. He came to the table, read the columns, and asked Oren for all three as one packet.

“For the hearing file. The filing clerk requires a single response received on route L-4.”

“You are asking me to merge three entries written in three hands at three different moments by three people who were not in the same room,” Oren said, “and to file the result as one response received by the ward.”

“The file is laid out as the filing requires, and the filing requires one response received, and the number of mouths that produced it is not a column the form has.”

“It is your business, because your name is on the paper that merges them.” Oren put his hand flat on the third column. “You may have a copy of each. You may not have a packet, because a packet arrives in an office already agreeing with itself, and I have spent a week watching this town be handed papers that arrive agreeing with themselves.”

The man looked at him for a long moment and then, unexpectedly, took the copies.

“Captain, in eleven years of filing I have merged a divided answer into one, because the alternative is a file saying a ward could not make up its mind, and such a file gets read as a ward that will not answer, and a ward that will not answer gets a Crown answer written into it inside six months.” He tucked the copies under his arm. “I am not defending it. I am telling you what you are up against, so that a man who knows what is coming can decide where to stand.”

“Then say it again tomorrow in front of the market court clerk, and he will write it without your name and it will still be true.”

The man left. Oren watched him go.

“He is not a villain,” he said at last. “That is the part I find hardest to explain to my men. He told me the practice in a room with no witnesses, which was decent, and he told it to me because he wanted somebody on this side to know before the hearing, and both of those are true, and he will still file a merged packet at four in the morning if the commission tells him to.”

“Then we do not need him to be a villain. We need the columns in the room tomorrow before he is.”

Below, in the yard, the miller's daughter bell was gone and the chandler had come and gone and the market clerk's copy of the three-answer book was pinned to the ward board in a covered box with a slate weight on it, and there was a queue of four people at the market court, which was more than Tomas had hoped for and fewer than he had needed.

Tamsin came in from the towpath at the eighth hour with mud to the knee and her satchel empty.

“I got as far as the toll shed,” she said. “One woman at the second cottage. A man at the ferry steps who wanted to know if it meant the road was closing. I said the words and I did not ring again, because nobody asked me to ring again.” She sat down. “The village past the toll shed will hear it tomorrow, or the day after. One house will not hear it at all. I have written both of those down and I have not corrected either.”

“And your hearing?”

“Bad enough that I watched the gate bell instead of hearing it. You are making a small face and I would like it recorded that I said so.” She put her hands flat on the table. “Vale. Write me the sentence.”

“Which one?”

“The one you drafted before you came down here. I saw you fold it. You have had a piece of paper in your coat all evening that you have not shown me, and you wrote it, and I have watched you write things all week, and I know what your sentences look like when you have already decided to keep them.”

He took it out.

It was one sentence, and it was the best sentence he had ever written, and it read: *Kells will be warned before dawn, and no child in this ward will be taken by surprise, and the maker of the Bellhouse answers for both.*

He had not been able to find a single flaw in it for six hours.

Nell Fenn read it first, because she was standing in the doorway with the household fragment in her hands and had come up to give it to the record and had stayed.

“That is four claims,” she said. “You can only stand behind one.”

“Two of them are true.”

“One of them is a promise about a whole town made by one man in a coat, and the Crown has spent ten days showing me what happens when a person is entered on a roll for saying something in a room. And you cannot stop a surprise, and if you say you can, then the morning somebody takes a child at four in the morning instead of at dawn, there will be a hundred people standing outside this workshop with your sentence in their mouths and my brother’s name in their heads.”

Tamsin read it twice and gave it back.

“Delete two words,” she said.

“Which two.”

“‘before dawn.’ You do not know the hour, and if you say before dawn then you have promised a warning you cannot time, and the morning it comes late you will have made yourself a liar in front of every house in this ward. And delete the second half. Do not say no child will be taken by surprise. That is not a thing a bell can promise, and the day it fails, it will have been said by a man who promised it, and that man will be you.” She looked at him steadily. “You want one sentence that does not have to be broken. That is the sentence. It is duller than the one you wrote.”

”What is it?“

“*I will warn that the inspection is posted for dawn on route L-4, and I will name the water, and I cannot tell you what to do.*” She shrugged. “It is dull, and it is the only version of it that will still be true at noon.”

He looked at it for a while.

“I had it in the frame on the eighth day,” he said. “I had it there and I took it out and put this in my coat, because the one in the frame is a small sentence and this one is a large one, and I told myself I was being careful.”

“You were being frightened, and it is the same thing, and you may write either word you prefer.”

He tore it in half, and then in quarters, and put the pieces in the watch stove where Oren's men could see him do it.

“Write this instead,” he said, and wrote it on the back of the withdrawal receipt, in the flat hand, and both women read it and neither of them said anything, which was the correct response and cost him more than praise.

Nell Fenn then put the household fragment on the table, which she had said on the fourth day that she would not do, and Oren understood before she said it that she had changed her mind for a reason and was not going to be argued out of it.

“I am keeping it,” she said. “I am keeping the original, in my coat, against my ribs, where it has been since the day you read the date on it. I am having it copied because the Crown has a file with my brother's name in it that says he was counted and did not come back, and if a clerk can write a line for a child then a clerk can write a line for a house, and the copy is so that somebody can one day ask him what he saw.”

The market court clerk had come up and was writing before Nell had finished, and then he stopped, and pushed the pen across the table to her.

“Write it yourself,” he said. “Your words, your spelling, and I will sign underneath it as the copyist and not as the man who made the thing.”

She wrote for a long time. Nobody hurried her. When she had done it, she read it out once, and it said that the Fenn household was entered as three people, that a fourth line had been cut away, that a clerk had written the boy as not returned in the same ink and at the same time as the line for the others, that the fragment carried a survey seal, and that there was a date under the seal, three days earlier than the carrier.

Oren read it twice and signed it, and then, because the table was there and the hour was late and he had not eaten since noon, he did something he had no right to do and had been thinking about for nine days.

He opened the drawer at the end of the table, and took out the L-4 file.

“Captain,” he said. “That is Crown property under a joint receipt and you have just opened it in a room with a Crown copyist in it.”

“The receipt covers the carrier. The file is the Crown's paper and the Crown gave me a copy of the docket and I have kept the rest because somebody was always going to ask me where the file was, and the honest answer is that it was in a drawer in my own watch house where I could read it.” He set the file on the table. “You are entitled to look at anything in this room, in front of the clerk, and the clerk will write down what you saw. I am not going to do it quietly and I am not going to pretend to be a thief about it.”

There were six sheets in it.

Two of them were the inspection order and its covering note, both of which the town had already seen the plainest parts of.

The third was the route docket with the withdrawn service entry, which Oren had read aloud in a casting room a week ago, in the dark, with the name Iven Sore on it.

The fourth was a Crown works order for the culvert at the low turn, and the date on it was the day after Nell Fenn had pinned three answers to the ward board, and Tomas read it twice and said nothing at all, because there was nothing yet that it proved and everything that it might.

The fifth was a service ticket that Oren had seen a copy of and now saw whole, folded around a wooden sliver, and it said that the carrier's clapper had been removed, that the carrier had been disconnected from the active line after a warning was filed, that the route was held for maintenance review, and that no keeper answer was recorded, and beneath that, in a smaller hand, a line that had not been on the copy: *answered in office, L-4 ward roll, per local practice.*

Not *no answer*. Answered in office.

The sixth sheet was a schedule, headed *ROUTE L-4 — RESPONSES ENTERED BEFORE SERVICE*, with forty-one household lines down it in a clerk's hand, each with a house number, a count of persons, and a single mark in the column headed response.

Tamsin read it upside down from the other side of the table, which she was not supposed to do, and then made the clerk turn it round.

The Fenn household was the twenty-second line.

FENN — six persons — response entered, the day the round was posted, before the round was held, and two of the six marked as accompanying, and no names at all, and in the response column a clerk's designation with an office number and not a single signature.

“Read it again,” Oren said, and the clerk read it again.

“Six persons,” he said. “Jory and Edan are two of the six, and Nell is a third if the fragment can be trusted. The round that took the boy was posted a week ahead and held on a day, and this response is entered on the day the round was *posted*.”

“Six,” Tomas said. “The household fragment says three, with a fourth line cut away. So the Crown's sheet has three people in it that the ward's own paper does not account for, and nobody has put a name to the difference.”

“Nobody has put a name to anything on this page,” the clerk said, a little sharply. “It is a form. Forty-one houses went down it the same week, in the same hand, with the same office number in the same column, and if you want to know what that means you will have to find a person to ask, and every person you are going to ask is on a roll somewhere.”

“Who entered it?”

“An office. No signature, an office number. That is how the other forty are done.”

“Entered before the household was asked anything.”

“Entered before the round was held. Whether they were asked anything before that day, I cannot tell you from this page, and neither can the page.”

Tomas had put his hand flat on the table again, not touching it, and he was aware of the old pull, the pull that had nothing to do with resonance and everything to do with wanting a name to be angry at, and he heard himself want it, and he let it be heard, and did not act on it.

“Write down what the page says,” he said. “Not what it means. What it says. Forty-one households. Response entered in office. No signature. One of the forty-one is the Fenn house, the response is dated before the round was held, and two of the six on that line are marked *accompanying*.”

“Which two,” said the clerk.

“I do not know. The page does not say, and I am not going to supply the two, because if I supply them and I am wrong then I have put a name in a court book that a Crown clerk can read out, and if I am right then I have told the Crown which two of them to go and find.”

“That is the correct answer and it took you nine days,” the clerk said, and wrote it down as *two persons marked accompanying, unidentified on the page*.

“And what it means?” Tamsin said.

“It means somebody could write down that a house had answered, in an office, without any of the house being in the room. And I do not know who, I do not know why, and I do not know what a person is supposed to do when a piece of paper says their family said yes.” He straightened up. “It also means the Crown's claim that the ward gave a single response on route L-4 is not a theory. It is a way of doing things that has been here long enough to have a form and an office number.”

“Write that last sentence on a Crown document and you will be a man arguing that the Crown forges answers,” Oren said. “Write it anywhere, and you will be a man who has said it.”

“Write what the page says,” Tomas said. “In the court book, in the clerk's hand, with the office number, and let the town draw the other line by itself. I am not going to help them draw it. I am not going to stand in a court at four in the morning and tell Kells what it is allowed to conclude about its own paper, and I will not be the man who tells them what to feel about it either.”

The clerk wrote it. It took a long time, and he wrote every line out twice, and then initialled the second copy, and the second copy went to the watch archive, and the first went into a box with the columns and the withdrawal receipt and the rope account, on the table, overnight, in a room with a Crown man in it, which was the only safe place any of it was going to be.

At the last hour, when they were all tired enough to be careless, Gib Hask came to the watch house door and stood in it without coming in.

He wanted to know whether the file had said anything about the wire under the ground. Oren told him honestly that it had not, and that nothing in it had said anything about the wire, and that he was sorry.

“Right,” Hask said. “Then I will be at the court in the morning, and I will say the same thing I said on Wednesday, and you can put it in your book, and if the Crown takes the bell out of the yard I will be the one who takes the wire out after it, and the two of us will be able to agree on something for once.”

Nobody invited him in, and nobody threw him out, and Oren wrote the visit in the book because a man's visit is a thing that happened.

Oren walked Tomas out at the last of the light.

The river had dropped another foot. The road was open. The carrier was in the watch store above them, upright in its frame in its padded cradle, the retaining bolts still released from the transfer and the red seal still tied around the lower brace, and in five hours a woman named Collis was going to stand at the north gate with a commission and a roll.

“I am not going to stop them taking the trial bell,” Oren said.

“I know.”

“I do not have the votes and I do not have the Crown and I have two men on my gate who will carry out a seizure at four in the morning if it is lawful, and I will not put them in a position where one of them has to choose in front of a crowd.” He shut the door. “I am telling you so that you can decide what to do with a workshop that is open and shut at the same time.”

“That is what it has been since the third day.”

“Yes,” Oren said, “but on the third day I still had a carrier to bargain with. Tonight the carrier is the Crown's problem and the trial bell is yours, and the difference is the only leverage either of us has left, and I would spend it on the columns and the schedule and the withdrawal receipt, and not on a bell.”

“I would spend it on a bell,” Tomas said. “That is the difference between us, and it is the reason you are right.”

Oren almost smiled, and did not, and went back inside.

Tomas walked up the hill under a sky that had gone clear and cold over the west, with the torn half of a good sentence in the watch stove and a page of a Crown schedule in his coat that he had not been able to stop reading, and the forty-one household lines went through his head all the way up the lane like a list of people who had been made to answer a question in a room they were not in.

The Vale Bellhouse sign hung crooked over the door. Under it, the no-ringing order, the Crown's wax, and Oren's mark, in that order, in the same weather, all three of them still there and all three of them removable by nobody alone.

The workshop was open, because it was always open, and there was a day-book on the bench with a column in it for tomorrow that was longer than any column he had ever written, and none of it in hand.

He put the schedule sheet down on the bench where Nell could find it in the morning, and did not lock the door, because the door was the only part of the Vale Bellhouse that no office had ever been able to close.
