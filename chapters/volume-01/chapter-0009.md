# Chapter 0009 — The Missing Surveyor

Tomas waited until the second watch had passed before he went to the survey store.

The town had gone quiet in the uneven way that followed a disputed order. Shutters were closed along the west road, but light showed under a few doors. Someone had placed a lantern on the river towpath, and its reflection moved across the black water. The Crown maintenance crew was still at the north gate, coiling a length of copper line and loading it into a handcart.

Tomas carried the cracked tuning file in a cloth pouch. The teeth had bent when the raised ridge on the trial blank caught the file. He had not repaired them. His ears still carried a thin ringing from the binding, louder when the night air pressed against them. A damaged tool and an ordinary ache were easier to account for than a tool he had lost while pretending to look for something else.

The survey store occupied a stone room beneath the market court. Its front door was barred from the inside, and a Crown notice had been nailed over the lock. A side loading door stood open, but the shelves inside had been partly cleared. Service ledgers had been removed, leaving pale rectangles in the dust. Tomas had no warrant to open the front room, so he did not force it. He walked around the building to the rear, where a drainage gap showed dark earth and the edge of a stone channel.

The buried route emerged beneath a loose slab. Tomas knelt and brushed the soil away with his fingers. A braided copper line ran from the north wall toward the river, its braid flattened where it crossed a stone joint. A newer section had been spliced in with bright metal and a sleeve of black pitch.

He placed the red thread along the line. It ended at a coupling that had been cut and rejoined with a narrow bypass. The cut was not the one beneath the Bellhouse. This was a later repair, made after the carrier had been delivered, and the sleeve still held a thin smear of gray wax. The bypass remained under tension rather than being capped, which meant the route had been opened without being made wholly dead.

Tomas took out his notebook and drew the route. He added the service marks he could see: a Crown survey triangle, a small number 4, and a shallow notch made by a file rather than a knife. The marks were ordinary enough to be overlooked. Together they told him where the line had been opened and closed.

Under a loose board he found a service ticket folded around a wooden sliver. The writing had been entered in a different hand:

FIXED SURVEY CARRIER — CLAPPER REMOVED  
DISCONNECTED FROM ACTIVE LINE AFTER WARNING FILED  
ROUTE HELD FOR MAINTENANCE REVIEW  
NO KEEPER ANSWER RECORDED

No name had been entered beneath the notice. No household had answered. The ticket recorded a maintenance action, not a person’s consent.

The north-gate bell repeated the two-note pattern at that moment. Tomas closed his eyes. The sound arrived through the ground first, then through the stone wall, then through the air. Before the note reached the bell above the gate, he felt a faint two-part vibration in the bypass beneath his fingers. The initiating sound had traveled through copper, not through the carrier’s empty crown.

He waited for the tone to show him a person or a meaning. Nothing did. The ringing was not a sentence he could open with his hands. It was a pulse, distorted by distance and the condition of the line.

He opened his eyes and followed the physical route south.

The line ran beneath the market court, split at a stone culvert, and rejoined near the old north-gate wall. One branch ended at the watch bell. Another continued toward the river landing. A third, older branch disappeared beneath a row of Low Ward houses. Tomas traced it until he reached a boarded doorway behind the chandler’s shop.

A Crown maintenance crew had stopped at the towpath ahead. Three men in gray coats were pulling staples from a shallow channel and coiling the exposed copper. Their leader saw Tomas and lifted a hand.

“Watch route,” he said. “You’re not authorized.”

Tomas showed the service marks in his notebook. “I’m examining a line connected to the survey carrier.”

“The carrier is not in this section.”

“It was connected to this section.”

“Connections can be cut.”

“Then the service record should say so.”

The crew leader looked toward the river. Tamsin stood near a moored skiff, her dark hair tied beneath a scarf. She had seen Tomas. One hand rested on the strap of a courier satchel; the other remained beneath her coat around the recovered handbell.

Tomas walked toward her. The crew leader followed a few steps, then stopped when she raised a Crown courier token.

“This section is closed,” she called. “If you touch the line, I will record your name.”

Tomas stopped. “You found your handbell.”

She looked at him without answering. “I found something.”

“Was it yours?”

“It was taken from my route. Ownership is a separate question.”

The crew leader said, “The courier is with us.”

Tamsin turned. “I am not with anyone. I am watching a public maintenance job and keeping a record. If the line is closed, put the closure in the record.”

The man’s jaw tightened. “You are causing delay.”

“I am causing a witness.”

Tomas moved to the edge of the channel and looked down. The line had been cut cleanly, then closed again with a narrow bypass. Beside the cut lay a small wooden tag marked with a date and an initial. The tag had been crushed under a boot, but enough remained to read: L-4, third watch, J. V.

J. V. could be a clerk’s initials, a maintenance code, or a name somebody had chosen to leave lying in the mud. Tomas copied the mark beside the route fragment he had carried from the Bellhouse, then drew a line through none of it. A matching initial could be a clue or a convenient lie, and the difference was the whole of his evidence.

Tamsin crouched beside him. “Your notebook is full of questions without answers.”

“It is a notebook.”

“That is not a defense.”

“It is a record of what I have seen.”

She glanced toward the crew. “I followed the green wool to the cooper’s loft. The handbell was there, wrapped in a Crown delivery cloth. A boy had opened the lockbox after a man promised him a copper coin, and Mira had been told to hide the bell until the watch passed. The man wore a maintenance key.”

“Did you get the bell?”

“Yes.”

“Did the boy identify him?”

“No. He had never seen him before. The key had a square hole in its bow.”

Tomas looked at the cut line. “Square hole?”

“Like the old north-gate keys. The watch uses them for relay lockboxes.”

“That does not prove who used it.”

“No.”

She had kept the skepticism he needed, though it made his chest tighten. He pointed to the cap on the line. “This was cut after the carrier rang. The service mark is newer than the coupling under the Bellhouse.”

“Then someone changed the route after the warning.”

“Or someone tried to hide how it reached the gate.”

Tamsin looked at him. “You are still not sure whether the warning is about the inspection.”

“I am sure the inspection is a danger. I am not sure the inspection is the whole danger.”

“That is a careful sentence.”

“It is the only one I can support.”

The crew leader called for them to move away from the channel. Tamsin rose and offered Tomas a folded paper. It was a route note, written in her own hand.

“The posted notice names route L-4,” she said. “The stolen bell was authorized only as far as the river landing. The alteration made it repeat farther. Someone wanted a signal to look local while moving beyond its route.”

“Do you know who altered it?”

“No. I know who could have done it. That is a shorter list, not a better answer.”

Tomas took the note. “Why give this to me?”

“Because you are the maker of the fixed carrier. Because I need someone to compare the wear, and because the watch captain will not let me keep the handbell at the station without a public record.” She paused. “And because you asked for supervised comparison instead of trying to take it.”

“I did not ask for your trust.”

“No. You asked for my handbell.”

“That is different.”

“It is.”

A Crown clerk came along the towpath carrying a ledger. He saw the crew, then Tamsin, then Tomas. “The route is to be sealed at the north gate. The carrier is to be held for inspection. The Crown inspection commission has issued a temporary holding writ, and Deputy Crake is carrying it as the local enforcement officer.”

Tomas’s attention sharpened. “For which bells?”

The clerk opened the ledger. “The fixed Crown survey carrier. Any trial bell made from the same stock. Every loose relay bell in the Vale Bellhouse. The writ names the objects.”

Tamsin looked at the crew leader. “The trial bell is private.”

“The writ does not distinguish private from public.”

“The handbell is mine.”

“It is Crown-issued.”

“It is a stolen object that I recovered. You may claim it, but you may not call it a local answer without a record.”

The clerk’s face went pale. He looked at the ledger as if the words had multiplied.

Tomas took a breath. “Where is Halden?”

“At the Bellhouse,” the clerk said. “He is waiting for the captain.”

The maintenance crew began loading the cut line. Tamsin caught Tomas’s sleeve before he could follow.

“Do not go back to make the bell speak,” she said.

“I am going to protect the evidence.”

“Those are often the same wish in different clothes.”

He looked at her. “What would you do?”

“I would write down what the line did, who was present, and what I can prove. Then I would make sure the record was in more than one pair of hands.”

“The record is not the warning.”

“No. But it is what remains when people decide the warning was inconvenient.”

Tomas handed her the route number written on a small piece of paper. “Take this with your own handbell. Do not answer it. Carry it as a record, not a signal.”

She looked at the paper. “You are asking me to help.”

“I am asking you to carry a fact. If you refuse, I will find another way to make it public.”

“Do not turn my choice into a test of your character.”

“I will not.”

She tucked the note into the handbell case. “I will carry it to the watch house and place it in the public record. That is what I choose. I will not carry a warning beyond that.”

“Thank you.”

“Do not thank me for doing what I was going to do anyway.”

They separated at the towpath. Tomas returned to the line and copied the service marks, the cut, the bypass, and the date. He did not listen for a hidden answer. He had learned that the distinction mattered, though the town was not yet ready to hear it.

When he reached the north gate, Halden Crake was waiting in the road. A Crown officer carried a broad black case. The market had closed behind them. The fixed watch bell hung above the gate, silent for the moment.

“Vale,” Halden said. “The commission is paper, and paper is coming to the Bellhouse before midnight. You have until then to surrender the carrier and the trial bell.”

Tomas looked at the notice in the clerk’s hands. “And if I do not?”

“The holding writ gives me authority to secure the named objects. The broader bells will be taken at the hearing.”

“Which hearing?”

“The one that follows the inspection.”

“That is not a hearing.”

“It is the law’s answer.”

Tomas looked at the black case, then at the sealed gate bell. The route had been cut and bypassed, the handbell was recovered, and the order had arrived before the warning. Every fact was ordinary. The danger lay in what someone could make ordinary facts do to other people.

He put his hand on the notebook in his coat.

“Then I will answer at the hearing,” he said. “Tonight, the carrier stays in its frame.”

Halden’s face tightened. “You may be sealing your own workshop.”

“I may.”

The Crown officer opened the case. Inside lay a seal roll and a pair of heavy restraints meant for a bell frame.

Tomas did not look at them. He looked at the locked casting room above the gate, where the fixed carrier waited under a watch seal and the trial handbell sat beneath a no-ringing order.

For the first time, the warning had a date, a route, and a hand in the machinery. It still did not have an answer that anyone had chosen to give.
