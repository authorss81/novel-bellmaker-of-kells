# Chapter 0030 — What the Town Does With It

The Crown answered the ward’s publication before the eighth hour of the morning, and it answered it the way it answered the water, which was to come in on a level where nobody could say it had been ordered.

Two of the three notices nailed on the board the previous evening were still up, sun-bleached by nine hours and readable. The round of renewal upon route L-4 is revised and **posted for the twenty-first day**, with the form to be exhibited in full and any person admitted as a witness. The practice published upon the ward board and read at the market court **originated in the Vale Bellhouse** and is not the act of the market court, the watch or the ward, and the commission will consider the standing of an originating workshop.

A third notice went up an hour ago, and it was the only one that was new, and it was the shortest thing the commission had ever nailed to that board:

*NOTICE, that the market court of Kells is to sit at the tenth hour of this day, and that at that sitting the court is to take the matter of the market court call bell and of every bell in this town joined to any line, wire or ground, upon the question of the court’s authority to describe, exhibit and carry them. THE COURT IS REQUESTED TO PRODUCE THE BELL.*

It is a small sentence and it is the second time the commission has said it, and the difference between the two sentences is the difference between a notice and a summons, and everybody in the lane understood the difference without anybody explaining it, which was the most frightening thing about it.

Tomas read the notice about his own workshop three times in the lane with the board at his back and then went and did something about it, which was to get a chair and sit down in the arcade with his day-book on his knees, because he had discovered over eleven days that the only reliable way to keep from answering a Crown notice was to be visibly doing something else.

Oren found him there at the ninth hour with a fourth thing, which was not nailed to anything, because it was not for the public.

“The district has ordered the watch to produce the Bellwrights’ Measure’s road map,” he said.

“For what?”

“For the file in the L-4 matter. The return is a schedule of ward withdrawals, dated and keyed, fifty-three of them, and the Crown wants it by the eleventh hour tomorrow.” Oren sat down heavily. “I have to produce it. I cannot refuse. The Measure is a Crown-licensed guild and its returns are Crown returns and I am a Crown-appointed watch captain in a Crown town, and if I do not produce it, then the Crown produces it, and a Crown seal on that map in a district file is a thing I cannot argue with in a room in Sennet for the rest of my life.”

“Then produce it.”

“I am going to produce it. I have written down that I am producing it under compulsion and not as a witness to its contents, which is worth almost nothing, and I would like you to know that I have done that and that I know what it is worth.” He turned the paper over and there was a fourth thing on the back of it, in his own hand, and it was a list.

“Jerm refused a Crown instruction at the gate on the tenth — four days ago now — and recorded it, because I made him record it by putting my mark under his, which is a thing I do and which is not a favour. The instruction was to write the Crown’s marks on the ward’s slates. I have named him, and I have named the two who did the seizure, and I have entered what each man was told and what each man did, and I have entered that I set the hour. And I have entered the line I told you I would enter, about the children, and about the hundred and forty people, and about a captain’s arithmetic.” He shut the paper. “I have been a watch captain for eleven years and I have never had a day like that one in a book, and I would like to tell you that it does not help, and it does not, and I did it anyway.”

“The two families on the ferry road,” Tomas said. “The rider told Tamsin last night.”

“They are at this court’s front step at the tenth hour with a paper between them, and I have read it, and it is a petition to the commission to place their two houses upon a Crown line, and it is signed by nine adults from four households, and the reason is the cart.”

---

The court sat at the tenth hour and it was not a court session, it was a market court room with about two hundred people in it, and Oren had the Crown’s notices read first and then gave the floor to anybody who wanted it, and the afternoon went the way such afternoons go, which is that the first twenty minutes are useless and then everything that anybody actually meant comes out.

Tomas did not speak for the first hour and a half. He sat at the side of the clerk’s table with the day-book shut, and he was asked four times in that hour and a half what the ward should do about the revised round, and he said the same thing each time, in four different words, and the fourth time he said it out loud on purpose.

“I am not going to tell this room what to do,” he said, “and I want to say why in a public court and not in a lane afterwards, because the reason is the reason and it is not modesty.

“Six days ago three of us stood on this step with a claim about the water, and it was read twice in the same words, and three men answered and none of them agreed, and the count went onto a piece of iron so that anybody at the back could hear that one of the three was refusing. That is the only thing I know how to do and I have not learned a better one in eleven days. What I have learned is that the moment a person standing at the front of a step decides what the people behind him want to hear, the three answers become one answer, and the one answer is the one he chose, and the man who chose it will spend the rest of his life saying that they agreed.”

A man in the middle said, “Then what are we here for?”

“You are here to say three different things,” Tomas said, “and for me to write down the three, and for the market court to keep them in three columns, and for nobody to merge them. That is the whole of my part and I am not enlarging it, and if the room decides to do one thing then the room will do one thing without me.”

Three positions were stated. By the end of the afternoon nobody had managed to reduce them to two, and that was the first success of Tomas Vale’s life in this matter, and nobody thanked him for it, and he had not expected to be.

The first was the ward’s existing one, and it was stated by Mercy Slee, and it was that the ward keeps its record and its three questions and takes no route from anybody, including the Crown, and that the ward is prepared to be poorer.

The second was stated by Ivo Prentice of the ferry road, who had a sheet of paper in his own hand, and who was not a fool and who could not be treated as one.

“The cart has been across the culvert a fortnight,” he said. “The works order is a Crown order and the frames have not been stood and the reeve’s men have not come, and the road is going to be a hole before the frost, and I have written to Fourmile and I have had no answer at all. We have asked the Crown for a line. Do you know what a line does? A line means an order, and an order means a cart comes. That is the whole of what I want, and I am not going to stand here and be ashamed of it in front of eleven witnesses, because the ward has a page and three questions and a copper wire under it that nobody laid by consent, and a page has never moved a single stone.”

“And it will be a Crown line,” Oren said, “and a Crown line comes with a Crown keeper, and the Crown keeper will be a Crown keeper, and in a year that line will be somewhere in a file with your house on it and a column called *response* and nobody in your house in the room.”

“I know,” Prentice said. “I have read the same notice you have. I am telling you that I have a cart at a culvert and you have a page, and only one of the two can be had this month.”

The third was stated by Gib Hask, who stood at the back with his two men and did not come forward, and who said what he had come to say and then sat down, which was the most effective thing he had done in eleven days and cost him the least.

“I want the ground,” he said. “All of it, out, from my house to the washhouse and up past the stage. And I am not going to argue for it here, because I have argued for it three times and got a book entry each time and no copper, and I am done arguing with documents. I am telling the room that the Crown’s road gang has been in my lane this morning with a line and a peg, and that their foreman stood at my gate and told me his men are under the lane tomorrow to renew, and that he said it the way a man says a thing he has been told to say and does not want said twice. I have got six men and a bar. I would be obliged if this court would tell me in public whether it objects to my taking the wire out under a Crown order or not, because I would rather be told to my face.”

Nobody told him to his face. The clerk wrote that nobody had.

Oren read all four of the Crown notices out first, and then he read Officer Rudd’s objection from the day before a second time, because he had said in open court that he would, and the room listened to the whole of it again without complaint. That was the point of doing it.

And then the court took the item the notice actually called, and the court usher stood up, which was not a thing she did.

“I have been asked to produce my bell,” she said. “I will say three things and then I will sit down, because I have three and I would rather use them well.

“It is iron. It has a leather handle and a striker on a thong and a rope, and there is no wire under it and there is no groove in its crown and there is no wax in it of any kind, and Watchman Jerm looked under it and inside the loft and at the beam on the eighth day and wrote it down and I signed it. It has hung in that loft nineteen years and it has never carried a word until the eighth day, when it counted three answers, one of which was a man refusing, and a count is not a line.

“And I am not going to have it described, exhibited and carried by an office that has not put one of its own hands on it. I do not mind the court hearing the matter. I mind the difference between a court hearing a matter and a court handing a thing to a man with a case, and I have been ringing this bell long enough to know which one I am standing in.”

Nobody spoke. The usher sat down, and the clerk wrote down that the market court had produced its keeper and not its bell, and that the bell itself remained in the loft under the keeper’s own hand, and that the market court would hear the matter and had not been asked to surrender anything.

Deputy Crake, from the arcade, said one sentence into the gap — the commission had noted the distinction and would take it up with the district — and then went out into the lane and stood where he could be seen standing there, which by now half the ward understood was a thing he did when he was frightened.

The question nobody managed was the children.

It came up at the third hour, when a man at the back asked whether the ward should be moving them out of the Low Ward before the twenty-first, and it got about nine words into the air and then Crown Officer Rudd stood up and read the new notice about the market court bell, which was the item actually before the court, and everybody who had been about to say a thing about children spent the next half an hour listening to a paragraph about the authority of a market court to exhibit a bell. Tomas wrote in the day-book that the moment had passed. He underlined it. He was wrong about the last part of that, and a girl with a slate told him so an hour later.

---

The Crown’s filing clerk was in the room the whole time.

It was Ansel Stroud, who had sat at a folding table under the arcade the day before and let a carter make him write two sentences in a margin, and who had spent the evening after that writing a letter to his office that he did not send, and who was here in his other capacity, as the man who had discovered on the tenth day that the form was the problem.

He had a case and a pencil and he was, Tomas understood by the fourth hour, a man in genuine difficulty who had been handed a form with one response column on it, and who had discovered in a lamplit watch house four nights earlier that the form was the problem.

At the seventh hour, when Oren asked the room to state the ward’s answer to the commission — a thing nobody had asked for, and Oren had insisted on it — the clerk stood up and asked, quite quietly, whether he might file it.

“You may file it,” Oren said.

“I will have to file it as one. There is one response column and there is no provision in the form for three positions and I have raised that with the office twice this week and I have been told that the office considers the point noted.”

“Then file it as one, in the words they are in, and read it into the book as you file it, and I would like the reading to happen with the room full, so that everybody here hears how a divided answer sounds when it is put in a single box.”

The clerk looked at the room, which was very full, and then said something Tomas had not anticipated, and the room could see that it cost him something to say it.

“I will read it both ways,” he said. “I will read it as the form has it, which is one response received on route L-4, in a single column, which is what it will be in Fourmile before the week is out. And then I will read the three positions, in the three columns, with the words I wrote down beside them, which is what it will be in this book, and the two will be in the same room at the same time, and everyone in here will know exactly what is about to happen to their answers.” He put his pencil down. “I want it entered that I was asked for both and that I asked for both, and that my office is going to be asked who let me do it, and I could not do it twice.”

“Read them,” Oren said.

He read them. And the sound the room made at the second reading was not a cheer and was not noise; it was the noise of two hundred people hearing, for the first time, the exact sentence that their three positions became when a clerk put them in a box, and the sentence was: *the ward requests continued and recorded communication with the Crown, acknowledges the making of a public practice at its board, and declines any Crown line, any Crown keeper, and any connection to a Crown wire.*

One sentence. All three positions gone. The record of what a ward actually wanted, made into a thing no household in it would have said.

“Well,” Oren said, when the room had gone quiet. “Now they have it. And every house in this room can go and ask a clerk, in a week, in Fourmile, what it means, and be told it is one response, and put our book on the counter and say *we are three, and here is the book, and what is your response column going to do with it*.”

“That is the weapon,” the clerk said. “For about a day.”

Nell Fenn had been at the side of the clerk’s table for the whole of it with a slate, because somebody in the room had to have the three positions in her own hand before a filing clerk turned them into one, and she had been chosen for it by nobody and had simply done it.

She came over to Tomas while the room was still arguing about the Crown’s notice.

“You wrote in your book that the question about the children passed,” she said. “It did not pass. It got to nine words and a man read out a paragraph about the court bell and everybody looked at him, and I was the one holding it.”

“I saw you holding it.”

“You saw a girl holding a slate.” She turned the slate over so he could see it, and it was nearly full. “I have got thirty-one houses on here that have somebody under seventeen in them, and I have got which three of those households lost a child on the tenth, and I have got the fact that the ward has spent eight days arguing about a column and has not once said the word *children* out loud in a room where a Crown man was writing. That is not a question that passed. That is a question that four hundred and eleven people have agreed to be quiet about, and I do not know who taught them, and I am not certain it was the Crown.”

She put the slate away.

“I am not saying it at the ward board tomorrow on my own. I have been thinking about it since the third hour and I have got no standing and I have got a mother who will say it in the wrong room if I do not say it first somewhere better than the board. So I am going to ask two people whether they will stand beside me, and if neither of them will, then I will say it alone and it will be in a bad room, and that will be what asking two people first is for.”

“What are the two people?”

“I will tell you when I have asked them,” Nell said, “and if I tell you first you will turn up.”

---

Tomas took the job in the arcade at the last hour of the afternoon, and he did not tell anybody he was going to.

The wheelwright came up the lane with a handcart and no bell, which in three visits Tomas had never seen him do, and the man looked at the yard — the clean square on the bench, the bare shelf where eighteen bells used to be, the no-ringing notice, the Crown’s wax, the watch mark, all three on one door — and then he put a bell on the bench.

It was the counter bell. It was the bell that had stood on the filing leg from the third hour of the third day and gone back into his cart untuned because it was hung a quarter inch long and its note beat against itself, and because a bell with a live claim in it had been standing four feet away under a cloth and a man could not dress metal with a live thing in the room.

“Right,” the wheelwright said. “It is eleven days late and I have wanted to shout at you about it and I am not going to. I am going to say one thing and then I am going to leave the bell.”

“Say it.”

“I have had three offers for this. One of them is in Miremarket and he is dearer and quicker than you and I have told him no, and I told him no because a man whose whole trade is taken off him by a Crown officer is not going to be still in business in three years, and I would rather have a debt than a stranger.” He put his hand flat on the bench. “So I am not going to shout. Bring it when it is ready. I will pay the same price and I will pay it this month, and if you have to tell me it is another month, then tell me that, and I will come back next month, and I am not going to be one of the men who goes to Miremarket and says the Crown did it.”

Tomas looked at the bell.

It was not a signal apparatus. It was not joined to any line, wire, or ground, and it had no claim, no keeper, and no witness matter, and there was nothing whatever in the world that a Crown notice could take off it, and it was hung a quarter inch long.

“All right,” he said.

He got the bench clear — the day-book, the slate, the receipt with the margin on it, the forty-one-household copy, the withdrawal procedure receipt, the three column-headings on one sheet in Ferrow’s hand — and he put them on the shelf by the door where Nell would find them, and he took the counter bell off the bench, and he put a line round its lip and took it up a quarter inch, and the note that came off it was the note it was supposed to have had.

He dressed it for an hour and ten minutes. It was ordinary work, and it was the best hour and ten minutes he had had in eleven days, and at some point in the middle of it he noticed that the shop was quiet in a way it had not been quiet since the second day, and understood that the quiet was not peace; it was a workshop with one bell in it that he was not allowed to ring and one bell on the bench that was a wheelwright’s, and that this was his life at the end of a stay and probably his life for a while, and that there was no version of the two facts which did not have a Crown notice nailed to a board four streets away.

Sabin Fell came down the lane at dusk and looked at the bell on the bench, and said nothing about it, which was how Tomas knew he had come about the other thing.

“They have taken the map.”

“I know. Oren is producing it under compulsion and he has entered that he is not a witness to what is on it, which is the only move there was and it is a small one.”

“It is not a small one and do not let him have it.” Sabin had his coat over his arm again and he was not coming into the yard. “Fifty-three years of that craft. Every ward that ever took its answers back, kept by men who thought keeping it was the same as agreeing to it. It goes into a district file tonight with a Crown seal on it, and there is a clerk in Fourmile who will read it and cannot tell that every one of those fifty-three was a refusal and not an agreement. And in six months somebody in that office will use it as a list of wards that do not answer, and the next man who comes up this lane with a form will have read it, and he will be right about his facts.”

“You want me to say something.”

“I want you to have heard it said in a yard, by a man who did not make it happen and cannot unmake it, while there is still somebody in this town telling you things instead of recording them. That is all. I am not going to help and I am not going to stop you, and I will not be in the room when you decide, and I would like that written down as a thing I asked for.”

Tomas wrote it down in the day-book in Sabin’s own phrasing. Sabin read it over his shoulder and said, “Good,” and went up the hill.

At the last hour the west road had a lantern in it and the Crown’s gang was in the low lane below the ward, marking out with a line and a peg where a renewal was to be done on the fifteenth day, which is tomorrow, in the lane where the children walk to the landing and where the copper runs.

Hask’s two men were at the low end of it with a bar and a spade and no intention of leaving.

And the ward, which had spent the day stating three positions in three columns and which had heard, at the seventh hour, what those three positions sounded like when a clerk put them in one box, went to its doors and found that the question nobody had been able to say in a public room was going to have to be said on a doorstep, in the morning, out loud, with somebody’s name on it.
