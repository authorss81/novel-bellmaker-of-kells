# Chapter 0023 — The Receipt

The pale rectangle on the casting-room floor was the shape of a frame that had stood there for four days and had been gone for eight more, and by the second hour of the afternoon he had swept around it three times, which is twice more than a man needs.

The yard was the loudest it had been in eleven days, and it was loud in the way an empty yard is loud. The bench where a bell had stood under a cloth for a week had a clean square on it in the dust of everything else, and the frame that had carried it was not in the yard either, because the frame had gone with it, because Oren had said *do not unframe it*, and Oren was right, and Tomas would have done it wrong.

He put the day-book on the bench and opened it to the day and looked at the two columns.

Paid: nothing, and it was going to stay nothing, and that was arithmetic and not despair.

Lost: the miller’s daughter, gone to Miremarket on the sixth day with a Crown mark she would not have on her rim. The chandler’s flour, gone on the seventh. The chandler’s work, gone on the sixth with Nell’s refusal behind it. A wheelwright’s counter bell, owed since the third hour of the third day, untuned, a quarter inch long, in the man’s cart and not in this yard.

And at the top of the lost column, in a line of its own, because it did not belong under any of the others, he wrote:

*one small trial handbell, workshop-bound, taken into Crown custody at about twenty-five to four on the tenth day, before the round opened. Never rung. One keeper, one answer.*

He looked at that for a while and did not soften it, and then under it he wrote a second line, and it was the first entry he had made in the day-book in ten days that was not about metal.

*Three children taken out of this ward between the fourth hour and the sixth hour this morning under a schedule nobody in the ward had seen. The bell that was supposed to warn about this inspection was rung zero times and would have told anybody nothing, and that is not the point. The point is that the warning I did ring on the eighth day cost a market day and a pig and a cart of flour, and this morning it cost three children, and I do not have a column for the second thing and I am putting it in this one anyway.*

Oren came down the lane at the third hour with the ledger shut, and he looked at the day-book open on the bench and said nothing about it. An hour earlier Tomas would have found that unbearable. He found it unbearable now for a different reason: he was being given room to record a thing he had no right to record.

“The no-ringing order,” Oren said. “You are going to ask me.”

“I am going to ask you.”

“It was made pending the hearing. The hearing was held before dawn and the commission did what it came to do, and on the plain words the condition has spent itself.” He put his thumb on the door. “I have re-made it. My hand, this afternoon, on the same notice, and it reads that it continues by the watch at its own hand until either the Crown lifts it in writing or the watch lifts it, and the watch has not lifted it. So the millrace bell is exactly where it was at eight o’clock this morning and it is exactly as mute.”

“Thank you.”

“Do not thank me. I have re-made it because the alternative is that the order dies at noon and the bridge bell is free to be rung by anybody with a crowbar, and the first man through that door will be the Crown’s own man with a list.” He looked up the yard at the bell on the north wall. “And I will tell you the other thing, since you would otherwise spend three days finding out. The commission will be back for it. The deputy did not promise it and he did not threaten it, and he said the word *bridge* four times in a sentence this morning in a tone I have not heard him use before, which is a man looking forward to an argument he expects to lose.”

“Then the bridge bell is the next item on a list,” Tomas said, “and I have no answer to it.”

“You have got until they come for it. Do not spend the interval making a hero of it.”

---

The receipt came at the fourth hour on Crown paper, brought by a clerk with a second copyist behind him carrying an inkhorn, which told Tomas before it was read that they expected him to write something.

The form was competently drawn. It had a box for the article, a box for the number of articles, a box for the condition, a box for seals, and a box headed *DESCRIPTION OF ARTICLE TAKEN*, ruled into three lines.

*ONE (1) SIGNAL APPARATUS, WORKSHOP-BOUND, BEING A LATHE-BOUGHT HANDBELL [sic] WITH ATTACHED WITNESS MATTER, CAPABLE OF PUBLIC SOUNDING, BEARING A SINGLE CLAIM, REMOVED FROM THE VALE WORKSHOP AND ITS YARD, SEALED AND WRAPPED IN OILED LINEN WITH ITS ASH FRAME.*

Tomas read it three times. Then he read the second copy, which was identical, and then he gave it back and asked for a pen.

“I will sign for the article,” he said. “I have got no standing to say it did not go, and it went in front of a hundred and forty people, and I will not have this form say anything else. I want the description box altered.”

“By whom?”

“By me, in the margin, and initialed by your copyist, and you may write *maker objects* beside it, and I will sign that too.”

The clerk looked at the copyist. The copyist looked at the form. It was, Tomas understood, a decision about eleven seconds long, and he could see the moment it turned: the copyist was not going to be the man who held a pen over an uninitialled correction in the margin of a Crown custody receipt, and the clerk was not going to be the man who had asked for one.

“Write it,” the copyist said. “I will initial it. I am not agreeing with it. Initial is not agreement.”

“Good. Put that in the margin as well. *Initialed by the copyist, not as agreement.*”

So Tomas Vale wrote, in the ruled margin of a Crown custody receipt, in the worst hand anybody had ever seen:

*The article described above was not capable of public sounding and did not sound. It carried one claim, made by one maker, on one sheet, on one day, and accepted by one person. It was bound to one keeper’s duty, which was mine, to warn that an inspection was posted for dawn on route L-4. It carried one informed answer, given by one person, who understood the claim, understood what the bell could and could not do, and was free to refuse and did not. That answer was given to me in private, was never given in public, was never carried by this bell as a public answer, and was not a response of the ward, the market court, the watch, or the Crown. The witness matter is the market court’s own L-4 posting and is packed separately and may be read by anybody. The article is bound and unspent. It cannot carry a second claim until the duty is answered or the binding is deliberately unmade by the keeper.*

He stopped there with the pen on the word *unspent* and had a bad quarter of a minute.

Because there was one more thing he could have written, and he was going to write it, and it was the only piece of information in the whole of this form that the Crown did not have, and he was about to hand it over on the strength of a principle he had articulated to himself nine days ago in a different mood.

He looked up.

The clerk had a small, tired, entirely sympathetic face. The copyist had stopped pretending to be busy.

Nell Fenn was not there. She was in the Low Ward, four streets and a lane away, at a washhouse or a door or a board, sixteen years old, with a brother and a father on a Crown page, and she had no idea that Tomas Vale was holding a pen.

*Name her*, said the part of him that had spent ten days learning that a record which will not say a name is a record that can be used against anybody.

*Name her*, said the part of him that could picture, perfectly, a Crown officer at a desk with her name under a heading that said ANSWER, in an office where a column nobody in Kells had ever seen was waiting to be filled in.

He wrote the name.

*That answer was given by Nell Fenn, of the Low Ward, privately, and has never been given in public.*

Then he initialed it, and the copyist initialed it, and the clerk folded the second copy into the Crown file with the expression of a man who has been handed something he will enjoy later, and the two of them went down the lane, and Tomas stood in the yard with the first copy in his hand and understood with complete clarity that the description was now true, and that being true had cost a girl something he would never be able to itemise, and that those two facts were going to walk about together in his head for the rest of his life.

He had known there would be a bill. He had written down on the ninth day that he would tell her himself, in daylight, in a room she chose. He had nine hours of daylight left and he could have used every one of them. Instead he had used forty minutes on a form, because the form was in front of him and the ward was four streets away and the argument had been *a paper that will not say a name is a paper that can be used against anybody*, and that argument had been true, and it had not said anything at all about a girl who had not been asked.

He went down the hill.

She was at the board with her mother’s coat over her arm, and the board had the pinned copy of the court book in its covered box and Nell’s own sheet above it, sun-bleached to the colour of old linen.

He did not begin well. He began by saying the word *record* twice, and Nell said, “Do not stand on that step, you are in the light and I cannot see your face.”

He came down and stood where she could see him. Second day running he had been told to move and had moved, and he had not yet learned that this was the whole content of the improvement.

“Nell. I have put something on a Crown paper this afternoon that I need to tell you about before anybody else does.”

“Tell me.”

“I have written your name on it.”

Nell did not move at all. Behind her, a woman was beating a mat on the step of the next house and the beating went on, and two children were arguing about a hoop in the lane, and the board with the covered box was four feet from Nell’s shoulder.

“Under what,” she said.

“Under the sentence that says the bell carried one keeper’s duty and one informed answer. I wrote that the answer was given by you, privately, and has never been given in public, and it is true, and the Crown has the paper now with your name and my name on it and a description of what you agreed to.”

“You put it in writing,” Nell said. “In a Crown office. Where a clerk can read it out.”

“Yes.”

“You put it in writing before you asked me,” she said, “and I want you to hear that sentence, because it is the sentence, and I have been hearing a version of it for ten days in a different voice.”

“You are right. I did not ask you. I had nine hours of light left and I used forty minutes and a form, because the form was in front of me and you were four streets away, and I decided it was more important that the record be complete than that you be asked. That is the whole of it. I will not soften it and I will not explain it twice.”

“Was it the right thing to do?”

“No.”

“You wrote to the court on the eighth day that a person may say no with no cost, and the whole of your argument was that paper is only worth what it says and no more,” Nell said. “And you have made a paper that says my name.”

“Yes.”

“Then it is a true paper and it is a paper that has to hurt me, and you are going to have to sit there while it does, and you are not going to be able to fix it, and if you try to fix it I will have to say something I have not got ready.”

“I will sit,” Tomas said.

She sat. On the low step of the board, with her mother’s coat across her knees, in the last of the light.

“You can say it,” she said, after a while. “I have got it ready. I am not going to use it today.”

“Thank you.”

“I am going to use it on a day when it is more use, and you are going to be in the room, because that is what a witness is for.” She looked up. “What did it cost?”

“Two days’ work, a lost contract, three children, and your name.”

“Write down what you just said in that order. Three children before my name. If you write my name first every time then I am the cost of this and I am not, and I have watched a person become the cost of a thing by putting themselves at the front of the list.” She stood up and put her mother’s coat on. “You can do that yourself. You have never once asked me for anything that was not already written.”

Sabin Fell came up the lane at the sixth hour with his coat over his arm, and stopped at the gate instead of coming in. That was his way of saying a thing had happened and the yard was not the place he was going to discuss it in.

They stood at the gate.

“You let them take it,” Sabin said.

“I did not let them and I did not stop them and the difference between those two is the whole of the last eleven days and I have learned it too late to be proud of it.”

“No,” Sabin said. “You did not.” He shifted the coat. “I am going to tell you what is going to happen, and I am telling you because nobody in this trade will tell you, and the reason they will not tell you is that they are all hoping it does not happen to them.”

“Say it.”

“On the fourth day the Measure would have put a word in a file and closed the matter, and I thought that was the shape of it, and I was wrong. The taking was too public. There are a hundred and forty people who saw a lawful thing carried out of this yard in front of a court, and a trade that has been arguing for thirty years about whether a bellmaker may be trusted with a route has just been handed a demonstration.” Sabin looked at the crooked sign over the door. “Within a fortnight the word will not be *contested*. It will be *Vale*. It will be a name you say when somebody asks whether an independent workshop is safe, and it will be said by men who have never met you and who will be believed, because they will be able to point at a morning and say the bell went and the children went, in that order, both in the same hour.”

“They did not go in that order,” Tomas said.

“They went within forty minutes of each other, and the forty minutes will be the story, and nobody who tells it will care about the order, because the order is the boring half.” Sabin put his coat on. “I will not lie to you about the other half. There are four houses in the Bellwrights’ Measure who will now say that a man who loses a bell and loses a child in a morning should not be holding a licence. Two of them will say it in the open. One of them is Iven Sore and he will say it and he will be right, and I do not know what he will do about it.”

“He has a challenge outstanding that I have satisfied and not discharged.”

“Then he may discharge it for you,” Sabin said, “and he will want a room and a witness and a day, and he will not want a crowd, and when he asks for one you will give it to him without asking him why. That is the whole of what I have come to say. The rest of it is that I am sixty-one and I have buried a wife and a son-in-law and I will not watch a boy of thirty-two learn the lesson from me.”

He went down the hill. Tomas stood at the gate and listened to him go, and then went back into the yard and did not sweep around the rectangle again, which he noticed, and did not examine.

---

At the seventh hour a Crown clerk and two porters came into the survey store with a written demand and emptied four shelves of the L-4 working papers.

It was done competently and in twenty minutes and it was, Tomas understood, the cheapest thing the commission did all day. Duplicate inspection sheets. The route fragment’s two certified copies. The gate-house relay log. A bundle of maintenance returns from eleven years with the L-4 entries at the top of each bundle, in eleven different hands.

“Collected for the file,” the clerk said, “on the commission’s standing instruction. Duplicates may be retained by the ward. These are the office copies.”

Tomas watched him put them into a chest and fasten it and there was nothing whatever he could do, and he discovered that he had been waiting all day for something to be angry at that he could hit, and that what had arrived instead was a clerk fastening a chest.

By the time Tomas came back up the hill the porter’s boy had come back too, with a note from the chandler, and a second note from the miller’s boy who ran errands on the ferry road.

The chandler’s note said that he was taking his bell work to a Miremarket house from the start of next week, that he was sorry, and that Tomas had done the man’s father’s clapper well enough that he would say so to anybody who asked him. The particular cruelty of the sentence was in the second half of it, and Tomas wrote the whole thing out longhand in the day-book because he was not going to be the man who paraphrased it.

The miller’s note said that the mark on the rim had not come off, and that the mark was now, if anything, more firmly on the rim, and that the bell was at a Miremarket house and the feast was on the eighth of next month, and that if the notice ever came off the girl might come back.

He put the wheelwright’s counter bell down on the bench where it had stood for seven days, in a clean square of dust, and looked at it, and did not start it, because the bench had to be clear and the bell was on the filing leg where it had always been, and there was nothing in the yard worth putting in a bell tonight.

He sat down and wrote the day up properly, and the last entry in it was not a loss.

*In the crown clerk’s hand, at about twenty-five to four, at the hearing: forty-one households entered as answering on route L-4, and two hundred and six persons entered as answering. The market court’s ward roll for the same round, in Ferrow’s book: thirty-eight households, one hundred and forty-one persons. Sixty-five persons differ, and I cannot yet say which office is right, and I will not say the Crown’s is wrong, because the Crown’s figure is the one that will be quoted and the Crown’s figure is the one a child’s name will be looked up in, and that is a reason to be exact and not a reason to be comfortable.*

*Question, unanswered: whose office is counting.*

He closed the day-book and the yard went dark, and down in the Low Ward somebody was making an enormous noise at the board, and when he went down to see what it was, at the ninth hour, with his hands in his pockets and nothing useful in his head, he found about thirty people standing in front of a sheet of paper that somebody had pinned up in the night without asking anybody.

It was a copy of the schedule. The heading, the forty-one household lines, the counts, the response column, the office mark, the dates on the posting days. It had been made on a watch-house copyist’s press, and it had been pinned with a drawing pin through a drawing pin so that rain would not take it, and beside it somebody had written in charcoal, in a large hand, the number of persons.

*206.*

Under that, in a different hand, smaller and shakier:

*Nobody in this ward was asked.*

And beneath that, already, in a third hand, the argument — three households’ worth of it, somebody’s grandmother saying it out loud at the board while a man who could not read stood and listened to his own name on a piece of paper in a lane he lived in, and a woman saying *my house is not on that, my house is not on that*, and a man reading out the numbers of the houses that were on it, and the noise getting worse, and a Crown notice, on the same board, saying that any person disturbing the board would be reported to the watch.

Tomas stood at the edge of it for a quarter of an hour and listened to forty-one houses being argued about in a lane where the people who lived in them had not been asked, and understood that the bell was gone and the stay was spent and the water was down, and that from this hour forward the thing that was going to happen to him would be done by other people, in their own words, in a place where he had no standing to say what it meant.
