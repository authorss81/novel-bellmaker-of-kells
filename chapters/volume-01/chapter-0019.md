# Chapter 0019 — The Cost of a Public Tone

Three counted strokes of iron in the rain had cost Tamsin Rook the whole of the following morning, and the bill came due in the way it always came due: not as pain, exactly, but as a room in which she could not tell what people were saying.

She sat on a bollard by the market lane with her back to a wall and let the market come to her.

The first trouble came at the eighth hour, from a woman she had never met, who said her name loudly and wrong.

“You had a bell rung in the court yesterday and it said everybody was to go out of their houses.”

“No.”

“She said my mother said it.”

“I rang nothing. I do not own that bell and I have no authority over it. What was rung was a court bell counting three answers, and one of the three was a man saying he would not carry anything, and the count exists so that nobody can say it was all one thing.”

“The woman at the chandler said two.”

“The woman at the chandler was at the back and heard a count. That is a different failure from mine and I am not going to take it off her.” Tamsin shifted so she could see the woman’s mouth. It helped and it did not. “What did your mother do?”

“She packed a box.”

“Was the box worth packing?”

The woman looked at her as if the question were in a foreign language. Then she said, “It was not,” and went away, and Tamsin sat on the bollard and worked out that she had just been more useful in thirty seconds than the whole of yesterday’s assembly, and that this was not a thing to be proud of, because the woman had packed a box for nothing and the nothing had been done to her by a sentence in a crowd.

She went to the watch house at the ninth hour.

Jerm had the account of the four strokes written in his square hand, with the time, and beside each stroke the words that had been spoken, and he had written them in the order they had been spoken, which was more than she had expected from a gate watchman.

“Oren wants the slips,” Jerm said. “Not to keep. For the hearing file. He says a Crown clerk’s man came at sixth hour and asked for the three as one packet, and Oren said no, and the man said the packet is how it is filed, and Oren said then it can be filed as three, and the man said he did not have authority to say no on that, and Oren said that was the first honest thing anybody had said to him in a week.”

“Did the man take anything?”

“He took a copy of the heading.” Jerm looked up. “That is not the slips. He wanted the slips.”

“He will come back for the slips, and he will say the hearing needs one response, and there will be a man in the room who agrees with him, because a hearing is a place where one clean answer is worth more than four ugly ones.”

Jerm’s pen stopped. “What do you want me to do?”

“Copy all three into the watch archive separately, and the withdrawals into a fourth heading, because a withdrawal is a record and not an absence. Every copy keeps its own heading, its own date and its own name, and they go in as entries and not as one with three under it. Then put a second copy of each on the ward board, tied, in a covered box, so the Low Ward has a copy that is not in this building. And write in the index that there are three and three withdrawals, and that all of it was made in one afternoon, so nobody can ever come in here and ask for a packet and be told there is one.”

“That is a great deal of paper.”

“Yes. I have two paid runs for this week and I am going to turn both of them down, so yes, it is a great deal of paper, and I would like you to know that I know what it costs, because you will otherwise assume I did not think about it.”

She was at the north gate at the tenth hour with Jerm and a slate.

The rain had gone. The river was a foot and a half down on the low gate’s second mark and falling, and the mud on the towpath had the look of a road that would be passable by noon and impassable by dark.

“Log it,” she said. “I am going to ring once, at the gate, and you are going to write down who says they heard it and who says they did not, and I am not going to ask them first. I am going to stand where I can be seen and ring, and you are going to walk.”

It was a poor instrument for the job. A handbell is meant to be carried, and a handbell rung from a gatepost is a bell telling the truth about itself: it is loud, thin, and gone in four counts.

She rang it once.

They waited, and then they walked. Jerm went as far as the landing, and came back with a boy who had heard it and a woman behind a shuttered window who had not, and both of them said so within a minute of being asked, and neither of them said it politely, because neither of them had any reason to be polite to a courier with a slate.

Jerm went further, past the landing, up the ferry road, and was gone for the better part of two hours. He came back with a list.

“Eleven people between the gate and the landing,” he said. “Seven heard it. Four did not, and one of the four is the woman in the shuttered window, who was a hundred feet from the wall when I asked her and who says she was asleep, and I am going to record that she says she was asleep and not that she did not hear it, because that is what she said.”

“Go on.”

“Past the landing, on the ferry road, which is where the route is not supposed to reach at all: five more asked, and three heard it. One of the three is a woman at the first cottage past the toll shed, a mile from this gate, and she walked down to ask who was calling the market.” He read the last line off the slate. “So. Seven of eleven inside the route. Three of five outside it, and it reached. And the second cottage past the toll shed heard nothing at all, a mile from the gate and well outside the route, and the man there was annoyed about it.”

Tamsin looked at the numbers, which were hers and which she could not improve.

“So the reach is a bad map,” she said. “It goes too far and it does not go far enough, and neither of those faults is mine and both of them are mine now, because I am the one holding it.”

“You are the one holding it.”

“Then here is the rule and I am writing it where you can see it, because I am not carrying a rule I cannot show a witness.”

She wrote on the slate, and Jerm copied it into the watch book afterwards at her instruction, and the rule was this: the handbell is a repeater, not an author. It repeats the note it has and cannot add a word. It is rung only when a named person is walking beside it who can say, out loud, what the note means and who asked for it. It is rung for the whole of a record or for none of it. It is not rung to fill a place left by a person who has withdrawn, and it is not rung to carry only the parts that are convenient.

“The rule about the record is the one the Crown will hate,” Jerm said.

“The rule about the record is the one I care about. If I ring this thing carrying two men who said yes and a man who said no, and the man who said no is not audible because I could not carry him, then I have done exactly what the wax in this crown was laid over the older groove to do. I have made a local thing sound like more than it is, on purpose, and with a better excuse.”

Tomas came up the towpath at the eleventh hour with his coat open and a sheet of paper in his hand and the look of a man who had been awake all night with a plan.

She saw the plan arrive before he did.

“Before you say it,” she said, “say the first part of it again, the way you said it in your head.”

He stopped.

“I was going to say that the river is down and the road is passable and the way to the upriver villages is along the towpath, and that if your bell runs the towpath from the gate to the landing and past it to the toll shed before dark tonight, then every household past the landing will hear the claim before the dawn, and that the people on the west road and the low ward will hear it from Crane and Cray the same as yesterday, and that the handbell will do the work that your voice cannot reach because your voice only goes as far as your legs go, which is not far enough.”

“That is my plan.”

“It is a good plan. It is also an instruction, and you made it in one breath, and I have watched you do it since the day you met me.” She held up the slate. “Ask me.”

He looked at the water for a moment, and at his own feet, and when he spoke again it was slower.

“Tamsin. Will you walk the towpath tonight and carry the record upriver?”

“Yes.”

“Then I have already decided, and that was the arrangement, and you were going to let me have the satisfaction.”

“I was going to let you see whether you could ask. You can ask. It took you eleven seconds and I counted, and I will deny counting if you tell anybody.” The wind was cold off the water and she put her hood up. “Here is what yes comes with. I walk it. Not a courier, not a runner, me, on foot, with the slate in my hand, because a handbell carried by a man on a horse is a man on a horse and not a keeper. I ring once at the gate, as my own repeater, with a person beside me who can say what it means, and I do not ring it again unless somebody at a door asks me to and names what they want repeated. I do not take Crown wages for it and I do not put it on a Crown docket. The route ends where the route ends unless a person asks for more, and if a person asks, I decide, and the reason I decide is that it is my bell and my hearing and my name on it.”

“And the toll shed village.”

“The toll shed village will hear it tomorrow afternoon, or the day after, and the second cottage will hear nothing at all, and I am not going to pretend otherwise to make the plan look better.”

“I will not say it either.”

“If you say it in a court, I will take my bell and go, and I will be right to.”

“Since the day you got here I have handed you things to carry and called it a favor,” Tomas said, “and I have never once asked whether you wanted to.”

“Not once, as far as you are concerned.”

“Not once,” he said. “I have been doing the thing where I need something carried, and I hand it to you, and because you are competent I have been reading your competence as consent.”

“That is the correct diagnosis and I want it in the book.”

“It is going in the book. In my hand, today, where you can see it, because I am not going to be the man who is one notch better at asking and still has to be watched.”

He wrote it on the back of his claim sheet, which was the sheet the Crown wanted, which made it a slightly ridiculous place to put it, and she liked him for it.

“One more thing,” she said, and stopped, and then went on. “My hearing is worse today than it was yesterday and I do not know by how much. When the watch changes tonight I will not hear the gate bell. I will be able to see it move and I will be able to feel the stone, and if I go deaf in the middle of a walk I will sit down, and I am telling you now so that tonight is not the first time you find out.”

“Then I walk the part of it you cannot hear.”

“You do not. You keep to the doors and the words and I keep to the bell and the road, because that is the actual division of the work and not a kindness to my ears.” She shouldered her bag. “And you will not tell anybody at that hearing that the courier carried it. If I carry a warning the morning after a Crown inspection, in a town where a Crown bell rang once without a clapper, then the first question anybody asks is whose hand is on the bell. And if the answer is yours, this stops being a route and becomes your errand.”

“It was always going to be my errand.”

“Yes,” she said. “That is the part I keep having to work out.”

She walked up the towpath alone, because the boy who usually ran messages had been sent to the upper village and she would not put a child’s name on the same slate as this. Behind her the gate bell went for the change of watch, and she felt it in the stones under her boots and did not hear it at all.

At the market court at noon there was a notice on the board that had not been there at the eighth hour, and the crowd around it was the bad kind of quiet.

Deputy Crake had posted it himself, at the eleventh hour, on Crown paper, and his clerk was standing beside it to explain it to anyone who asked.

AN EMERGENCY CUSTODY HEARING IS SET FOR THE FIRST HOUR OF LIGHT ON THE DAY FOLLOWING THIS POSTING, AT MARKET COURT, BEFORE THE RESCHEDULED INSPECTION OF ROUTE L-4. THE MATTER IS THE WHOLE OF SIGNAL APPARATUS HELD IN THE VALE BELLHOUSE. THE COMMISSION ASSERTS THAT THE PUBLIC SIGNAL MADE YESTERDAY IN THIS COURT HAS DEMONSTRATED THAT THE WORKSHOP IS ABLE TO SOUND AN ANSWER IN THE WARD WITHOUT THE AGREEMENT OF THE WARD, AND IS THEREFORE MORE DANGEROUS THAN BEFORE, NOT LESS.

The last sentence had a clerk’s hand under it, and the clerk looked as though he would have preferred to be standing somewhere else.

“Read it out,” Halden said, to nobody, in a voice pitched for the lane. “The whole of it. I want the ward to hear the last line in my mouth and not on a piece of paper.”

“You made a bell sound in my town,” he said, “and half of what came out of it was a man saying no. That is not a warning system. That is a crowd. And a crowd that can be made to ring on demand by one workshop, in a ward with a Crown inspection at dawn, is exactly the thing this commission exists to stop, and I will be asking the hearing for every bell in that yard.”

A woman near the front said, “Some of us will be glad. We never wanted the bells.”

“And that,” Halden said, “is the first sensible thing anybody has said on this matter all week, and it has been said by a woman who was not asked to say it, which is how it ought to be done.”

Tamsin read the notice twice, and then read it a third time looking for the part that named a time it could be beaten, and found that the notice was competently drafted and could not be beaten, and that the only place in the whole of it that was soft was the last sentence, which said *without the agreement of the ward*, and which was, strictly, and to her lasting irritation, correct.

She copied it out into the watch book with the time and the seal, and then walked to the Low Ward board and pinned her own copy beside Nell Fenn’s sheet, which was still up, sun-bleached, with its three lines about not being asked to move anybody.

Two papers about the same bell, side by side, in a place where people already went to argue. It was not much. It was what a courier could do, which was to make sure the two stories had to stand next to each other, and to refuse, for one more day, to be the reason either of them was believed.
