# Chapter 0040 — The Twenty-First Day

Nobody had told the ward what to do about the twenty-first day, and by first light on the twenty-first there were three hundred and forty of them standing in the market lane and the low lane, by Oren Pike’s count at the north gate, which he entered in the gate book with the hour and the fact that it is his count and not an official one.

Forty of them had said yes to a Crown officer at their own door the night before. About three hundred had come without being asked by anybody, including about sixty who had no business being out of bed and who were out of bed.

There was no speech. There was no bell. There was a girl of sixteen at the ward board with a slate reading out her own line of the previous night, in a market court, with a lamp on the clerk’s table and the door open, before anything else, because she had written at the bottom of it that it must be read out tomorrow before anything else, and the day she stopped reading it out was the day this ward stopped being able to check anything at all, and so she read it out, standing up, in a flat voice, to a market court and a lane and a Crown officer with a black case:

“That on the evening of the twentieth day the Crown obtained from about forty households in this ward, by asking at their doors, a list of the households that intend to attend the round in person, and did it in a satchel, in a hand, with a lamp, and without a form.”

Then she read the second line, the one underneath, about the cheaper notice, and the room was extremely quiet, and Deputy Reeve Crake was at the back with his hat off for once and did not say a word about it in the court at any point on that day.

---

The round opened at first light and it went on until near the middle of the day and it is on a page in a district office in Fourmile, and the page is the surveyor’s return and it is the only document about the twenty-first of this month that anybody wrote on purpose.

It says that the renewal upon route L-4 was conducted at first light on the twenty-first day, at thirty-seven doors, with the full form exhibited entire at the market court from the fourth hour, and that any person was admitted as a witness, and that the ward had set out a table and a chair in the arcade and that the Crown sat behind it, and that the Crown sat behind it because Captain Pike had compelled a counter on the ninth day and nobody had ever withdrawn the compulsion.

It says that at twenty-two of the thirty-seven doors, a person of the household put the ward’s three questions to a Crown clerk in public and had the answers written in the margin of the household’s own line, in the clerk’s own name, and initialled, and that at nine of those twenty-two, the answer given was *I am not permitted to say* and the objection was written down and the name of the person who wrote it down was written down.

It says that the *Witnessed by* column on the return for this round was filled in two lines, and that both names in it are a person’s name in a hand from the house, and that the office had proposed neither of them and had accepted both. And that one of the two was put in a margin at a public counter the previous afternoon by a woman who had been asked for nothing, and that the other has been in the same field for two years, through every round since, and the field is not cleared between rounds, and that this is the second of the two and not the first, and that a filing clerk in Fourmile is entitled to read it either way.

It says that one child was removed from one household, at the fifth hour and thirty minutes, on the strength of a witnessed return.

*And it says*, in Surveyor Rhea Collis’s own hand, at the end, and this is the sentence that will be read in a district room in a month:

*That I was conducted to thirty-seven doors and I saw the form exhibited entire and I saw an objection written in a margin in front of me at house one hundred and thirty-three. And that I certify this round as a round, and I do not certify any household’s answer, and I decline to countersign any record which asserts that any household on route L-4 assented to or received an answer, for the reasons I entered on the twelfth day and have not been permitted to withdraw. And that I will say the following in my own return, unasked: a form that is exhibited entire, and a procedure that is published, and a clerk with a conscience, together make a round that is conducted properly and conducted entirely, and that is not the same thing as a round that is lawful, and that I was on the tenth of this month at a gate at four in the morning and I was on this morning at a court at four in the morning, and both of them were proper, and one child went in a wagon both times, and the difference between them is a column with a name in it and a margin with a name on it, and I do not believe a field and a margin can carry a child out of a house and I have been a surveyor for eleven years and I am obliged to write down that I no longer know what a form is for.*

---

House one hundred and eight was the Wilde household, and the girl was ten, and nobody in the ward had ever heard of the Wilde household, and the Wilde household had never been marked before.

The line said five entered, five returned, and in the *Witnessed by* column, in a hand from the house, in ink, a name.

Mrs Wilde read the form in the lane at four in the morning with a lamp. She read it twice. And then she said, in a voice that about sixty people heard:

“That name has been dead two years.”

Nobody said anything.

“That is my mother. That is Ellen Wilde, and she gave her answer at this door in her own hand two autumns ago, and she was standing in this doorway when she did it, and her hand is on that page because she wrote it herself, and there have been rounds at this door every month since, and not one of them has ever cleared it out, and she died in the autumn of that year and there are four of us in this house and there have been four since.” She held the form up so that the crowd could see it, and held it for a long time, and about nine people came forward and looked at it. “I am not saying that man is a liar. I am saying that nobody has ever cleared it out.”

The officer was very young and he did not want to be there at all, and Tomas saw his hands.

“Ma’am, the column is not cleared between rounds. It has never been. I cannot strike out a name on a witnessed return. I can take your objection and it will be entered, and the return stands.”

“Then it is a dead woman standing in my doorway every time you come up this road.”

“Yes, ma’am.”

And then he read the rest of it out, because he had to, because it was on the form and the form was what they did, and the rest of it said that the household’s objection was received and entered, and that the objection did not suspend the renewal, and that the child named on the line was removed at the fifth hour and thirty minutes to a Crown wagon standing in the north road, and was carried north, and that the removal was made on the witnessed return of five returned, and the objecting household’s count of four was noted in the margin in the clerk’s own name and initialled.

The girl was called Hattie. She was ten. She was not in her shoes; somebody in the crowd had her shoes. Her mother went with her as far as the wagon step and did not get on it, and there was no scene at all, which is the thing Tomas could not get over afterwards: there was no scene, and three hundred and forty people stood in a lane and watched a child of ten walk up two steps into a wagon, and not one of them did anything, and every single one of them had a reason, and the reasons were all true.

Absalom Teage was one of them and he gave his out loud to Tomas afterwards, in the lane, without being asked, in a voice like a man laying bricks. “I have got a boy of nine. I stood in that lane for two hours thinking *that is a girl of ten and I have got a boy of nine and I have written things in a margin*, and I stood there. I will write it down tonight and I will put my name to it and I will read it out. I do not know what else there is.”

Renn, at the low gate, said one thing to him at the sixth hour, and nothing else was said to him anywhere all morning.

“You have got two keepers and a claim about a drain, and the wagon is standing in this lane, and there is not a bell in this town that says anything about a wagon. Say that to yourself later and see whether it makes you feel better.”

“No,” Tomas said. “It is not even the reason. I want you to know that, because I will write it down, and I want the record of it to be right. It is not the reason I did not ring. The reason I did not ring is that a bell cannot be made to say a thing it was not bound to say, and if I had tried, the sound that came out of that yard this morning would have been a version of a sentence, and there is a man on the ferry road who has already made a version of one of mine, and this ward has spent nine days learning to tell the difference. I did not ring because a bell of mine in that lane this morning would have made a version, and there are already too many, and I would have made one.”

Renn looked at him for a moment.

“That is a better reason than the one you had,” he said. “Put it in the book. And go and write down what you saw at one hundred and eight, because you were the only man in that lane who was not holding a paper.”

---

Deputy Reeve Crake came down the lane at about the ninth hour, alone, without a black case, and stood in front of Tomas and did not begin with the Crown.

“I am going to say one sentence to you and then I am going to go and stand where I can be seen standing there, and I would like it entered that I said it and that I would rather not have said it.” He had his hands behind his back. “You have spent a month teaching this ward to ask questions and write the answers down, and it has worked, and it is the reason twenty-two doors got an objection in a margin this morning, and if this commission had been a bad office the margin would have saved that child at one hundred and eight. It was not a bad office. It was a correct one, working correctly, on a form that was filled in by a dead woman, and there is no version of the month you have had in which the procedure could have stopped it, and I would like you to sit with that for a year instead of a night, because in a night you will get it wrong and build something on it.”

“That is the first thing anybody in this commission has said to me that I believe and did not want to hear.”

“It is true, and it is not a gift, and I am not being kind.” He shifted. “The crown of it is this. A ward that publishes a procedure can make an office legible. It cannot make an office stop. It can only make the office’s paper good enough to be worth reading, and then the reading of it is done by a clerk in a building, and the clerk is right, and the child goes. You have made a beautiful instrument for showing a man what he is doing. Nobody has built one for stopping him, because it cannot be built out of paper, and everybody in this ward knows that and has spent a month being extremely careful, and I would like it noticed that careful is not the same as effective and that you are all about to find out how different they are on the twenty-eighth.”

He looked up the lane, at the three hundred and forty people still in it.

“I would rather have nine houses and a clean return,” Crake said, “and this morning I had thirty-seven houses and a clean return, and it cost me a child, and I am going to have to write the word *clean* on a page about it in about an hour, and I am going to write it, because it is a word about my conduct and not about yours.” He went, and he did stand in the lane where he could be seen standing there, for eleven minutes, and then he went home.

---

Tomas Vale stood four feet away for the whole of it with a slate in his hand and a bell in his yard half a mile off, and he did not ring it, and he did not move, and about four hundred people saw him not move.


Tomas spent the whole of the round writing.

That is the whole of what he did on the twenty-first of this month, and it is the first thing in twenty-one days that he has done that nobody asked him for and everybody needed.

He wrote down what the ward actually did, house by house, in three columns, in the yard, on the bench, with the door open and the three seals on the outside of it, and the three columns were headed, in his own hand, and the headings are the reason the page matters:

*The first thing done at this door. The second thing. The third thing.*

Not *position*. Not *answer*. **First thing, second thing, third thing.** Because a column headed *position* gets merged by a clerk into a response, and the ward had learned that on the fourteenth day at the cost of its whole argument, and Tomas Vale was not going to be the man who invented a new column and then watched it get folded.

He had the Crown’s own exhibit of the round in front of him and the ward’s book and the gate book and a slate, and he wrote at about a hundred and ten words an hour for eight hours, and the page is fifty-one lines long and it is the only document about the twenty-first that a person in the Low Ward can check against their own door.

And the twenty-second line of it, because he wrote the houses in order and the Wilde household is one hundred and eight:

*One hundred and eight. First thing: a person of the house read the form aloud in the lane at four in the morning and said that the name in the Witnessed by column was her own mother’s hand, and that her mother had been dead two years, and that there has been a round at that door every month since and the column is not cleared between rounds. Second: the name was not struck out, and the reason was given aloud, that the column is not cleared between rounds and cannot be struck out on a witnessed return. Third: the objection was entered in the margin in a clerk’s own name and initialled, and the objection did not suspend the renewal, and a child of ten went to a wagon at the fifth hour and thirty minutes on a witnessed return of five returned, and the mother counts four, and both figures are now on the same page in this town, and the Crown’s figure is the one that moved the wagon. And I want it written that a woman of this house has been trying for two years to get a name off a page of theirs, in writing, and has been told to wait, and that what she got instead was a clerk’s margin and a child, and that both of those are the same office.*

He wrote out at one hundred and thirty-three, because the round went there and he was not going to leave a house out of a page because he disliked what it said: that Mrs Corris’s name went into the column at the fourth hour of the twentieth and was the first name in it in eleven years in this ward, and that the Crown took no child from her house, and that the column is never cleared between rounds.

There was a room at four in the morning on the twenty-first, and it was the same room there was no room in on the tenth, and it existed because a girl of sixteen had pinned a notice on a board saying the form would be exhibited in full, and there were twenty-two objections in a margin at that table before the sixth hour, and a Crown clerk wrote nine of them in his own name and initialled them, and nobody in that court said one word about the fact that there had been a room, and Tomas wrote it in his day-book as the fourth thing and could not make a sentence of it.

Nobody noticed that either, and it was the single worst fact of the twenty-first, and half of it had been done in this ward, in daylight, by a woman who had every reason for it, and who had been shown in a public room by a frightened clerk that a name goes in that column when a household puts it there, and who had not known that she was also showing a district office that the column could be read.

He wrote out one hundred and nineteen, and the entry says that Mercy Slee put the *Witnessed by* question at the Crown table in the arcade at the fifth hour and had the answer written in a margin, and that the answer was *the column is printed and it is empty on this ward’s returns and I am not permitted to say why*, in a clerk’s own name, initialled, and that the clerk who wrote it said afterwards, in the open, that he had been asked by his office what he had said last night in a filing room and that he had answered it truthfully, and that he would not say what he had said.

And he wrote, in the fourth line of that entry, in brackets, the thing that mattered: *(Mrs Slee’s house is on no roll. It is now on the district’s amended return of the seventeenth, as a person having public standing in matters of signalling, dated. It was entered by a watch captain under an order and he has not asked her.)*

---

The Crown’s answer came at the eleventh hour and it was nailed at half past, and it is the shortest notice the commission has ever put on that board and it did not mention the round that had happened that morning.

*NOTICED, that the practice of the ward board and the market court of Kells in the matter of renewal returns is a matter for the district and no longer for the local court; that the commission will not sit on it; and that the renewal upon route L-4 is posted again for the twenty-eighth day, and that the form will be exhibited entire, and that any person may be a witness.*

And under it, in the small hand, a second thing, which was not a notice at all, and which nobody in that court had asked for, and which was going to be the end of this month and not the end of anything:

*AND NOTICED, that the district will consider, on the return now made by the surveyor of the northern district, the question of the renewal of the ward’s own line, of the possession of the ground in which it is laid, and of the persons under seventeen in the Low Ward.*

Nobody read that bit out for about a minute and a half. Then Nell Fenn read it out, in full, twice, the way she read everything, and then a man at the back said, “*the persons under seventeen*,” and about half the crowd turned round and looked up the lane, and Tomas Vale, standing at the wall with fifty-one lines in a day-book, understood that the Crown had spent nine days building a procedure and had spent the twentieth night finding a cheaper one, and had put the ward’s unasked question on a board in a sentence a man could say in a doorway.

Because it is not a question about a return, and it is not about a column, and it is not about a form. It is the question that four hundred and eleven people have not said out loud in a room where a Crown man was writing, and it is now on a board in a Crown hand, and it will be on a district file by the twenty-fourth, and there is a Crown surveyor going to Fourmile with a return that says thirty-seven doors, twenty-two objections, one child, and a sentence about what a form is for.

And a Crown office that cannot get a ward to answer has learned the cheapest thing there is, which is to ask the question out loud in the ward’s own name and let the ward’s silence do the work. Nine days of a ward not saying it, and then a second line on a board, and it is the most efficient instrument anybody has used in this town.

---

Oren recorded three answers separately at the market court in the afternoon, because that is what he does.

He recorded the answer of the ward board, in one column, in his own flat hand, and it was that the ward would hold its page and check it.

He recorded the answer of the market court, in a second column, and it was that the court would hold a hearing on anything a person brought, and would not hear the matter of a bell as a matter, and would continue to produce its keeper and not its bell.

And he recorded the answer of the watch, in a third column, and it was one sentence, and it is in three books:

*That the watch will produce, to any person, the register of bells kept since the seventeenth day, being hours and places and no names; that the watch will not produce a name; and that the watch’s answer to the district’s amended return of the seventeenth is the covering page read into the market court on the seventeenth and entered in the gate book, and that the word standing in that office means, in this ward, that a person stood in a lane and was heard.*

Tamsin’s route was not used on the twenty-first, and the reason was stated out loud, in the lane, at her asking, because she would not have it kept.

“I have not rung my bell,” she said, “and I am not going to, and I am not going to at the next one either, and if anybody in this ward wants me to they are welcome to come and ask me and I will refuse them, and I would rather be refused in front of people than have everybody assume I was busy.

“And here are the four reasons, and the fourth one is the real one. One: my bell is a call to come to a door and nothing else, and it is not a thing that carries a claim, and a claim about a wagon is a claim. Two: the Crown’s register covers every bell in this ward with hours and places, and the low drain gate is the end of my route, and there is a place in a Crown book where a bell went off at the eighth hour on the nineteenth and no name against it, and I am not going to add a second line to that page while there is a girl in this town with a broken wrist. Three: a bell in that gate last week reached a man on the ferry road who put a Crown’s words in my sentence and repeated it four times, and it is still being repeated tonight, and it will be repeated tomorrow, and I did not put it there and I cannot take it out. And four: I cannot hear it.”

She said the fourth one in the same voice as the other three and about a hundred people did not hear the difference and about nine did, and Tomas was one of the nine, and he wrote it down in the yard that night in four words, and the four words were her fourth reason and nothing else, and nobody ever read them but him.

And then she did the thing she had come down the lane to do, which was to be asked about it.

“Nine of you heard the difference,” she said, “and one of the nine is a man who will write it down and eight of you are going to be asked about it in a week by somebody who is very polite about it. So I am going to say the rest of it here, where it is a woman saying a thing out loud in a lane, and not something two people found out between them. I had it in my mind on the thirteenth that nobody in this town was going to be told, and I was right about the reason and wrong about the method, and I have had eight days to think about that and this is what I have come to. A thing said once by the person it belongs to is a record. A thing found out about in a lane is a rumour, and next month somebody who wants this bell will have heard the rumour and will not have heard me, and will pay a man to find out whether it is true.

“I cannot hear it. I have not been able to hear the note of a bell since the fourth of this month, and I can still see a bell move and I can still feel one in a wall two streets off, and if a man in Fourmile comes to me with a form and my name in the right column he is going to know all of that before I have said a word, because I have just said it in a lane, and I would rather it came out of my mouth. You may write it down. All of you may write it down. And the price of it is mine and I have priced it, and I am not going to be asked whether I thought about it first, because I decided it at first light and I have been waiting all day for a lane.”

Tomas did not look at her. He knew what the fourth reason was because she had told him alone at the low drain gate on the thirteenth, and he had been carrying it around for eight days with nowhere to put it down, and this was where she had put it down, in a lane, in front of about a hundred people, without asking one of them whether it was allowed.

---

And Perry came to the board at the third hour of the afternoon, with her right arm in a sling made out of a Crown gang’s own satchel strap, and she gave Tomas back the piece of paper he had written on the fifteenth day asking her to write down where his words reached.

“I am giving this back,” she said. “I have read it about nine times since and I have decided I do not want it. I have still got the gang’s book. The book is Crown property and the book is wet and the entries for that hour are illegible, and the foreman has written *illegible* in the right place and initialled it, and I have watched him do it, and I want you to know that a Crown foreman wrote *illegible* in a Crown book and initialled it, and that nobody in his office is going to be pleased about it and he did it anyway.”

“You do not have to give it back.”

“I know I do not have to. That is why I am doing it.” She held it out. “I wrote down where your words reached. It was the market lane, at twenty past eight, and the west road, and the toll shed, and I had got to about six names of people who told me they had heard it, and that is when the cart came over, and after that the book is water. So the honest answer to the question your paper asked me is that I know where it reached and I cannot prove one yard of it, and if anybody ever asks me — and somebody will, because there is a line in a Crown register that says four strikes in the market lane of Kells and a district office is going to want a witness — then I am going to say the same thing I said to my foreman on the seventeenth, and I have thought about it for two days and I have not found a better version.”

“And what is it?”

“I do not know. And the foreman does not know. And the clerk at the north gate wrote *none* in the third column four times and initialled each one, and that is the only document in this business that I would put my name to.” She put the paper down on the board. “And I would like it noted, by somebody, in any book, that a Crown road hand with a broken wrist thinks the Crown register is the best piece of paper in this whole thing, and I do not know what to do with that, and it is going to follow me for the rest of my life.”

Tomas did not take the paper. He read it, and he did not sign for it, and Mercy Slee entered in the ward’s book that it was returned, and that the maker did not take it back, and that the reason he did not take it back was, in Mercy Slee’s words, *that he has learned the one thing this month, which is that a man who takes a thing back off a person who is frightened has had it twice*.

And at the last hour of the twenty-first day, a serjeant of the commission named Teale came to the ward board with a page in his hand and read out a fourth paragraph, and there were about forty people there because it was late and most of them had been standing in a lane since first light.

It was a question. It was a Crown question. And it was the ninth and last sentence of a notice that was going to a district file on the twenty-fourth.

“The district will consider, on the return of the surveyor of the northern district, the question whether the ward of Kells intends to remove the persons under seventeen in its bounds from the ward before the next renewal; and to whom, if it does, the ward proposes to deliver them; and the district will accept any answer given to that question, and will certify it, and a certified answer will be a return.”

He put the page down on the board and looked at about forty people, and he said:

“I am obliged to read that. I am not permitted to say anything else about it, and I would like it entered that I read it and that I said nothing else, and that I will be at the north gate at the sixth hour tomorrow and the seventh and the eighth.”

Then he went, and the forty people stood at the board, and the page was on the wall with the four notices on it, and nobody at the board said anything for a long time, and the first thing anybody said was a woman asking whether the district would take an answer, and a second one saying that it had just read a sentence off a piece of paper that said it would, and then somebody laughed, once, and it was not a good laugh and it was the only one all day.

Oren Pike’s register was in the watch house with twelve entries for the nineteenth — six in a yard, one at the low gate, one at a board, and four in the market lane — and a single entry for the seventeenth, and nothing at all in it for the twentieth or the twenty-first, and it goes to the district on the twenty-fourth in a leather wallet with the other returns in it, and no name in it anywhere.

The day-book on the bench in the yard of the Vale Bellhouse was open at a page with fifty-one lines on it, three of which said *first thing* and *second thing* and *third thing*, and there was a fresh slate on the bench and a clean space on it where a wheelwright’s counter bell had stood since the fourteenth and was collected on the seventeenth, and on the slate, in Tomas Vale’s own hand, were four things he had carried out of this month and had not lost. A route fragment, made in his own hand and kept. The three column-headings, copied out of the market court book, and the fifth column for the withdrawals. The copy of the Crown schedule, co-held with Mercy Slee of house one hundred and nineteen, and the pinned copy on the board with the drawing pin through the drawing pin and the charcoal under it in somebody else’s hand. And the custody receipt for his trial handbell, with his correction in the margin, in his hand and the Crown copyist’s initial, and the initial is not agreement.

He put the slate down and went out into the yard and shut the door behind him from the inside, which nobody had ever forbidden him to do, and stood in the dark a while with his hand on a bell that was his own and had nothing in it about anything that had happened this morning.

And nobody in Kells said the word *children* out loud in a room where a Crown man was writing for nine days, and on the twenty-first of this month the Crown wrote it itself, in a second line under a printed notice, at the eleventh hour, and nailed it to a board in front of about forty people, with about three hundred and forty of them still standing in a lane four streets away, and it is now on its way to a real place with a real date on it, and the people of the Low Ward are going to have to answer it out loud, and a man in a lane who has spent nineteen days learning not to be the man who answers things is going to be standing in that lane when they do.
