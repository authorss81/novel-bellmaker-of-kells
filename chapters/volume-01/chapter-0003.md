# Chapter 0003 — The Stolen Handbell

Tamsin Rook reached Kells the next morning, shortly before noon, with one boot scraping mud, three hours of sleep behind her, and no handbell.

The absence had a weight of its own. It sat against her hip where the small case normally hung, and every time she turned through a crowded lane she expected the faint copper knock of it against her thigh. Nothing answered. The north-gate relay lockbox had been opened with a master key sometime in the night, its wax seal cut cleanly enough that the watch first suspected a careless clerk. Tamsin knew the cut was not careless. The lockbox had been opened from the inside, and the theft had been arranged to look like an accident of procedure.

She kept the route map in her coat and the missing-lock report folded in the market court. She did not ask the north-gate watch for help. That was a mistake she could not afford twice.

The market lane was wet underfoot. Fish scales glittered in the seams between stones, and a cart had left a wheel rut that filled with brown water. Tamsin moved around the rut while a watchman in a blue cloak called after a fruit seller. The watchman had a square face and a short temper. She recognized the type. They preferred a visible thief to a missing object.

“Handbell courier,” he said when he saw her. “You’re the one.”

“Depends on what you’ve heard.”

“Your bell is missing. The lockbox was forced.”

“It was opened with a key.”

“By someone with a key.”

“That is a better description.”

The watchman came closer. “The captain wants every house searched. The people in the west lane are already nervous about bells.”

“Then search the lockbox first. Check the key register. Ask who signed out the master after the last watch change.”

“Those are Crown matters.”

“Your lockbox.”

“It is under Crown seal.”

“So is the bell.”

Tamsin kept her voice low enough that only he could hear. “If you search every house on a courier’s word, someone will take something else before you reach the fifth door. If you check the register, you may find the person who actually opened it.”

The watchman’s mouth tightened. He wanted the simpler anger.

A woman carrying a basket stopped nearby. Her eyes moved to Tamsin’s belt, then to the north gate. A boy with one bare heel was playing at the edge of the lane. The child had a strip of red cloth tied around his wrist, the color used on courier cases.

Tamsin crouched. “Do you know where couriers keep their bells?”

The boy looked at her as if she had asked whether the river ran uphill.

“In the lockbox,” he said.

“Until somebody takes it.”

“Did somebody take it?”

“I can’t prove that.”

The child’s mouth became uncertain. Tamsin showed him the report without giving it over. “Have you seen a small handbell near the gate? This one has a flat crown and a worn handle.”

The boy looked toward an alley between a dyer’s shop and a cooper’s stall. “Mira saw it.”

“Who is Mira?”

“The girl with the green scarf. She said a man paid her to watch the lockbox.” He paused. “He had a Crown-looking key, but he wore no Crown colors.”

Tamsin thanked him and stood. The watchman had heard enough.

“You are recruiting children?”

“I am asking them what they saw.”

“You are starting a search.”

“I am finding the place to begin.”

“Captain Pike will not like it.”

“I wasn’t planning to ask him for permission.”

The alley smelled of wet lye and old rope. Tamsin found a scrap of pale wax near the cooper’s threshold, flattened by a heel. It held a fine gray strand, not the red wax of the Bellhouse handprint. She lifted it onto the edge of her route map. The strand came from a Crown courier cord, used to tie down a case during rain. Someone had carried the handbell through here under cover of a delivery.

A shopkeeper came out carrying a board. “You’re looking for Rook’s little bell?”

“You know me?”

“Everyone knows the courier who argues with the watch. It was taken this morning.”

“By whom?”

“Someone who knew the lockbox schedule.”

Tamsin looked at him. “Who told you that?”

The shopkeeper shrugged. “The watch.”

“Did you see the thief?”

“No. I saw a woman with a covered basket. She went toward the river.”

It could have been a lie, a guess, or the shape fear had given a real stranger. Tamsin did not ask the shopkeeper to choose among them. She marked the alley on her map and turned toward the old gate.

The lockbox stood in a stone niche beside the north gate, beneath a roof of lead. Its door hung open. The wax seal had been cut and replaced with a thin smear of red. Inside, the empty hooks made a row of dark half-moons. A maintenance label marked L-4 had been peeled away, leaving a pale rectangle.

Tamsin crouched and examined the lock. No scratches. The key had turned cleanly. She touched the lower hinge and found fresh grease. Someone had maintained the box recently. Someone who knew which parts to oil and which marks to leave.

A small gray fiber clung to the hinge. She wound it around her finger. Not courier cord. Wool, dyed the green of the dyer’s alley. The stolen bell had been wrapped in that cloth, or its thief had been.

Tamsin stood as the watch arrived in force. Captain Oren Pike came through the gate with two men behind him. He was broad and tired, with a leather strip tied around his left wrist. His gaze found Tamsin, then the empty lockbox.

“Courier Rook,” he said.

“Captain.”

“Report.”

“Bell stolen from the lockbox between second and third watch. The lock was opened with a master key or a careful copy. The seal was replaced. There is green wool in the hinge.”

Oren crouched and looked without touching. “You have your own reasons for noticing wool.”

“I have a reason for noticing what I can carry.”

His gaze moved to her belt. “Where is your handbell?”

“Missing.”

“That is not an answer.”

“It is the fact you asked for.”

Oren rose. “We will search the lane.”

“No.”

The men looked at one another. Tamsin pointed toward the west road. “Search the lockbox register first. Whoever opened it knew the schedule. If you put your hands through every basket, you will find a frightened child or an old enemy and call it progress.”

“And if I do not search?”

“Then you will find a thief who has had time to move the bell farther.”

Oren studied her. “You have a route map.”

“I carry one.”

“Give it to me.”

“No.”

That answer came out harder than she intended. Oren’s expression tightened.

“Your bell is Crown-issued.”

“My route is not. The bell was assigned for a specific route, and it carries a signal that people in the Low Ward have agreed to recognize. It is not a public order.”

“Everything that rings becomes public when there is a missing child.”

“No child is missing. A bell is.”

“Those words will not comfort a parent.”

“Then give the parent a true account.”

For a moment the market lane seemed to narrow around them. Tamsin felt the old pressure behind her left ear. It began as a thin vibration, the memory of a relay bell struck too hard in a military yard. The sound had continued for three days after the route was shut. Some nights it returned when she was tired, and she could not tell whether the note was outside her or inside her skull.

She kept her face still.

Oren noticed. “You are hurt.”

“I am inconvenient.”

“Those are not opposites.”

“They behave the same way when a man with a seal wants your hand.”

He glanced at the report she still carried. “Who took it?”

“I don’t know yet.”

“Then you have no reason to keep the map private.”

“I have a reason to keep my route out of a search that would turn every person along it into a suspect.”

Oren’s jaw tightened. He was a man who had learned to dislike secrets because one had cost him his brother. The market-court story said Tomas Vale had been part of that secret, or of the silence around it. Tamsin had no intention of handing a Crown witness the same excuse.

“I can give you the route number,” she said. “Not the people who keep it.”

“Route number alone is not useful.”

“It is useful if you stop looking for a thief and start looking for the schedule that made the theft possible.”

Oren held her gaze. “You are asking me to trust a courier without a bell.”

“I am asking you to check a record before you damage someone’s door.”

At last he gave a short nod. “Bring the map to the watch house. I will compare the lock schedule with the route map. If you hide a person who took it, you will answer for that.”

“If I hide anyone, I will tell you I have done it.”

“That is not a promise.”

“It is the truth I can offer today.”

They returned to the watch house. Oren spread the lock register on a table while Tamsin laid out her route map. The authorized route for her handbell ran from the north gate to the river landing and then through the Low Ward. It was not permitted to repeat beyond the landing, where the signal could be taken up by the public flood bell. The map’s edge had been rubbed smooth from years of use.

Tamsin pointed to the route. “The handbell has a Low Ward signal. It can repeat the route note, not add words. If it was altered to carry farther, the wear will show.”

Oren looked at the report. “Someone altered the bell?”

“Someone used it to repeat a signal outside its authorized route.”

“That is a serious accusation.”

“It is a physical observation.”

“Against whom?”

“Against whoever handled it.”

He leaned closer. “You have been a Crown road-ringer. You know how to make a handbell carry farther.”

“I know how to maintain a route. I do not know who changed mine.”

“Your hearing.”

Tamsin’s fingers closed around the map’s edge. “What about it?”

Oren looked toward the door rather than at her. “The report says forced resonance. A relay officer’s warning that the sound had damaged your hearing.”

“The report says that.”

“Is it true?”

“It is not false.”

He waited.

Tamsin released the map. “A Crown relay was struck in a military yard. The order came through a route that had been answered by someone who had not given permission. The sound kept going after the line was cut. I heard it for three days afterward. Some of it never stopped.”

“Why answer with a route you did not trust?”

“Because refusing meant losing the route. I thought I could carry the damage myself.”

Oren’s expression changed slightly. Not sympathy. Recognition, perhaps. “And now you carry other people’s bells?”

“I carry mine until I decide it is no longer safe to carry it.”

“That is not the same as owning it.”

“No,” she said. “It is the part I am still learning.”

The watchman with the square face returned with the register. A master key had been signed out shortly before second watch by a Crown maintenance clerk. The name had been entered as J. Vale. The surname matched Tomas’s.

Tamsin looked at Oren. “That is either a useful clue or an easy lie.”

“Vale is the Bellhouse name.”

“Vale is common enough in Kells.”

“Not in the lock register.”

“Easy lies often use common names.”

Oren closed the register. “I will not search your route. I will check the clerk and the sign-out. You may keep the map for now. Bring the bell to me if you find it.”

“I will bring it to myself.”

“Courier.”

“It is my bell.”

“Until I decide otherwise.”

“No,” Tamsin said. “That is the part where you decide whether a Crown issue is more important than the person who carries it.”

Oren’s mouth tightened, but he did not order her to surrender the map. “The fixed carrier and your handbell may be connected. I am arranging a supervised comparison at the Bellhouse.”

Tamsin glanced toward the empty lockbox. “I will compare a handbell, not decide what your maker says it means.”

“That is a distinction I can remember.”

“Good. The comparison is mine to accept or refuse, and the answer will remain mine.”

Tamsin folded the map and tucked it into her coat. The gray wool fiber remained caught in her finger. She unwound it and placed it beside the lock register.

The wool led her back through the alley and behind the cooper’s stalls. Mira, the girl with the green scarf, was waiting beneath a loading board, as if she had known someone would follow. She pointed toward a ladder fixed to the cooper’s loft.

“He said to hide it until the watch passed,” Mira whispered. “Then he said he would come for it.”

“Did he give you a name?”

“No.”

Tamsin climbed. The loft smelled of shavings and oil. A Crown delivery cloth lay beneath a broken hoop, and under it lay the courier handbell.

It was smaller than the fixed survey carrier by half, with a flattened crown and a handle worn pale on one side. A dark line of wax had been worked into the crown’s groove, widening the old signal beyond its assigned route. The alteration was crude. Whoever had made it had not understood how to tune the handbell so much as how to make it obey longer.

Tamsin wrapped the bell in the delivery cloth and sat with it until her breathing had slowed. The old pressure behind her ear returned as she touched the crown. She let it come. It did not tell her who had stolen the bell or why.

She found the green scarf’s owner in the lane below and asked whether the man had returned. The girl shook her head. Tamsin gave her a coin for the cloth and told her to remember the square key, not to follow the man, and to tell the watch only what she had seen. The girl asked whether the bell would make the watch take everyone’s bells. Tamsin said she did not know. It was the first answer that did not sound like an evasion.

When she returned to the watch house, she placed the recovered handbell on the table beside the lock register. Oren examined the handle and the wax without taking it from the cloth.

“Your route map says this bell should stop at the river landing,” he said.

“It does.”

“The wear says otherwise.”

“Someone changed the wear.”

“Or someone used it to carry a signal farther than the route permits.”

“That is the same observation.”

“It is not the same conclusion.”

Tamsin rested her hand over the crown. “I will keep it. You may compare it with the fixed carrier under supervision, but I will not surrender it to the watch and I will not let you call it a public answer.”

Oren looked toward the empty lockbox. “You are asking for a joint record.”

“I am asking for a record that says the bell was recovered and what it carries. I am not asking you to trust me.”

“Those may be different things.”

“They usually are.”

Tamsin folded the cloth around the handbell and tied it to her belt. The familiar weight returned, lighter than the case and more dangerous than the missing lock had been.

At the door, she looked back once more. The empty hooks caught the gray light. Someone had taken a small bell because it could carry a signal farther than it had been authorized to. Someone else had left a route that pointed toward a fixed carrier with no clapper. The two facts did not yet form a conclusion, but they were no longer separate accidents.

The Vale Bellhouse stood above the gate, its sign gray with weather. Tomas opened the door before she reached it, as if he had been watching for her. He looked at the cloth bundle under her arm, then at the casting-room shutters.

“Your handbell was taken from a route that connects to the carrier,” he said. “If I ring the fixed carrier once in the market, the line may show where the signal was redirected. It could force the person who handled it to answer.”

“Or it could frighten every family on the west road into telling you where to look.”

“It would be evidence.”

“It would be a bait made from a bell you do not understand.”

“I know the carrier’s frame.”

“You know its frame. You do not know who has accepted its warning.”

Tamsin kept one hand on the bundle. “I will let you compare the wear. I will not let you ring the fixed carrier to hunt for a thief, and I will not let the watch search my route because the lockbox was opened with a key.”

Tomas glanced toward the shutters. “I was not asking you to give me the handbell.”

“You were asking for a reason to use the larger one.”

“Yes,” he said. “I was.”

The admission took some of the anger out of her. Not all of it.

“Compare the wear in daylight, with the door open,” she said. “Do not decide what the sound means. Do not decide what my answer means.”

“I can promise to ask.”

“Ask, then. I may still say no.”

Tomas stepped aside. Tamsin entered the Bellhouse with the recovered handbell, the route map, and the boundary she had chosen. The fixed carrier remained locked in the casting room. None of the three bells shared a frame.

Tamsin looked back once as she passed the threshold. “You do not get to turn my bell into a command because you are afraid of what happens if it stays silent.”

“No,” Tomas said. “I am trying not to.”
