# Chapter 0052 — What Marta Vell Is Not Told

He left the barrow at the head of the lane where the lane comes down off the field and the road turns into the village, because the road turns into a square about eighty yards on and there are about nine houses that look into it, and he was not going to carry a Crown object into a square in front of nine houses on the morning after he left a town with a district wax on its door.

He took the cradle off the boards and set it in the grass at the top of the lane and made the four bolts fast there, and he wrote the hour in his book, and the words he wrote under it were: *Set down outside the village, out of the square, not carried in. Bolts made fast at the place it was set down.*

Then he walked down the eighty yards with nothing in his hands at all, which is the first time in about a month he has walked anywhere with nothing in his hands, and it felt so badly that he turned round twice before he got to the water.

---

The bell is on a post at the head of the water and it is a small bell in an iron headstock and it has a rope on it and it is not joined to anything. Marta Vell rang it at about the seventh hour, four strokes, unhurried, and then she coiled the rope and hooked it on the post and went in.

He waited until she came out again, which took about nine minutes, and she had a cloth in her hand and the cloth was wet.

He got about nine words in.

“Ma’am. My name is Tomas Vale and I am a maker of bells and there is a thing I have to tell you about your bell that I should have told you two days ago and did not.”

“You have a Crown bell in a field at the top of my lane,” Marta Vell said.

“I have a Crown object in a field at the top of your lane, and it is a survey carrier and it is in a frame and it has no clapper in it, and I am not its keeper and I cannot ring it and there is a receipt in my coat that says I may not say what it means.”

“Then you have told me the interesting part of it in the first breath,” she said, “and you have done it standing in the middle of my water with your hands empty, and I would like you to go up the lane again, and I would like you to do it now, and I will not be cross about it.”

She said it without any temper at all. That was the thing he had not been ready for. He had been ready for temper, and temper he could have stood in.

---

He went as far as the first gate and stood at it with his back to the lane so that she could see his back and not his face, which is what Nell Fenn did in a market court on the twenty-fifth and which he had watched her do and thought about for a week.

“Mrs Vell,” he said, to the gate. “I will not come past this. I have something to tell you and I will say all of it and you can shut the door at any part of it and I will go up the lane and I will not come back today.”

“Go on, then.”

“On the morning of the thirty-third of last month, at about twenty past seven in the morning, a woman at a gate on the towpath four miles below here asked you to ring this bell four times and she named what she wanted repeated, and you rang it, and she did not ring again and did not repeat it. That is on a page in a town four miles from here and it is in a district file and it is not wrong and nobody has done anything wrong.” He kept his back to her. “And in a building in a town called Fourmile there is a schedule, and on that schedule there is a line, and the line says that the water bell at Ocklebridge is a bell of this ward, and there is nothing at all in the field where the keeper goes, because the form has no field for a keeper of a water bell. You are on that schedule. You are not on it in your own hand. Nobody asked you. Nobody has ever told you.”

The yard was quiet for a while.

Then she said, “How long have you known that.”

“Since the twenty-eighth of last month, in a district building, off a copyist's arm.”

“And it is the thirty-fifth today.”

“Yes.”

“And you are telling me it at four in the morning at the top of my lane because it is on your way and because you are a stranger,” said Marta Vell, “and if you had not been on your way you would have gone down the river and I would be standing here this morning ringing my bell at seven and not knowing that for another year, and that is not a reason to tell me, that is a reason to not care.”

“Yes,” Tomas Vale said. “That is fair. I have been carrying it two days and I have not slept properly on either of them and I have written it down twice and both times I have written *told*, and *told* is the wrong word and I did not know it was the wrong word until you said it.”

---

He stood at the gate for a while. Then he said the thing he had come down the lane to say, and he said it in the order he had written it, and it was the fifth clause of a custody receipt signed by a watch captain in a real town at about the fifth hour of the morning of the thirty-third.

“The receipt says that the object in my cradle is not mine to certify and not mine to describe, and that no answer given by any person to any signal carried on or by it may be entered by anybody in any book as the answer of a household of this ward, and that I have no standing to speak for any household in Kells or in any ward.”

“I do not know what any of that means,” Marta Vell said, “and I have not the least wish to, and you have come down here this morning to read me a piece of paper.”

“I have come down here this morning to read you a piece of paper,” he agreed, “and I want you to know before I read the rest of it that I read this clause to myself on the thirty-third as a reason not to tell you, and that is not what it says, and I read it that way because a receipt is a thing that stops me doing things and I am a man who has spent a month looking for a thing that stops me doing things. It stops me entering an answer for you. It does not stop me telling you a fact. Those are two different clauses and I put them together because I did not want to be the one who had to come down here.”

“You put them together,” Marta Vell said, “because you did not want to come down here. And now you have come down here. And I have told you to go up the lane.”

“Yes.”

“Then go up the lane, Mr Vale.”

---

He went up the lane about forty yards and stood at the barrow and took out his book and wrote, and what he wrote is the sentence this chapter is named for and what he wrote afterwards under it, and both lines are in the book in his own flat hand with the hour against them:

*A receipt is not a permission.*

*And the other half, which I did not expect and which is the true one: a permission is not a reason. I have been carrying that clause for two days as though it were a wall, and it is not a wall, it is a rail, and a man who leans on a rail for two days will lean on a person next. She was right. She was right in about nine words and I have been on a road for two days to be told it by a woman at a water bell.*

---

He came back at about the ninth hour because the rain had stopped and the smithy opposite the water was opening, and the woman was on her own door step with the cloth over her shoulder.

“Mr Vale,” she said. “I am going to say something and then I am not going to say anything else about it for a week, and you are going to go away and not improve it, and if you improve it I will know, because I have been the person at this bell for thirty years and I know what it sounds like when somebody comes back with a better version.”

“I will not improve it.”

“You may write something down,” Marta Vell said, “and I will not read it and you will not read it, and if you want me to write something down I will write it and it will be mine and I will not show it to you and you will not see it again until I decide you will. That is the whole of what I am offering and it is not very much and I am not going to pretend it is more. I have a bell to ring at seven in the morning and I am going to keep ringing it at seven in the morning, and if your schedule in Fourmile says I am a bell of some ward with nothing in the keeper's box, then that schedule is a paper with a hole in it and the hole is where I am.”

“I am not asking you to read anything,” Tomas Vale said. “I am not asking you to look at a single thing.”

“Good, because I would not, and because a woman at a water bell who starts looking at papers about herself is a woman who is on a roll in about a fortnight, and I have watched that happen to a man with a ledger and I have watched it happen to a woman with a bill, and I am not going to be talked round by a thing that is happening to somebody else.”

He put his hands out of his pockets, deliberately, so that she could see them.

“Then tell me what I have asked for,” he said. “Not the schedule. Not the district. Tell me what I have asked for, in your own words, and if the words are wrong I will go away.”

Marta Vell considered him for a moment.

“You have asked me to write down, in my own hand, with the hour on it, what I do every morning with this bell and who has ever come to this water and asked me to ring it, and you have asked me for that instead of for the schedule, and the reason you have asked me for that is that a page in my hand about my own morning is a page nobody else has ever held.”

“Yes.”

“And you want the hour on it because you are a man who puts hours on things.”

“Yes,” Tomas Vale said, “and I want the hour on it because I have spent a month in a town where everybody wrote down what they thought and nobody wrote down when anybody had said it, and it turned out that the hours are the part anybody can check.”

She went in and got a slate, and a stub of chalk, and she did it standing on her own step with the rain coming off the eaves, and he stood about six feet away with his hands behind his back and did not read it, and it took her about eleven minutes, and at one point she stopped and said *wait, I have to get a thing straight or the whole of it is wrong* and went in and looked at something and came out and went on.

When she had done she turned the slate round and held it against her own body so that he could not see it, which she had decided on in advance, and he had seen her decide on it in the middle of the eleventh minute.

“There,” she said. “That is a night’s work done in a morning and I do not want it back for a week, and I will read it myself in about nine days and if I do not like what I have written I will burn it, and if I do like it I will do something with it, and what I do with it is mine and you may not have a copy.”

“I will not ask you for a copy.”

“You will not ask me for a copy and I am going to give you one thing off it,” she said, “and only one, and it is because you will otherwise go away and tell yourself I refused you, and I did not refuse you, I refused the schedule. I have rung this bell every morning for thirty years and there has never been a keeper in it, and the field for the keeper is not in the form, and a form that has no field in it for the only person in the entry is a form that is not about the person.”

“Yes,” Tomas Vale said.

“And the first name on that page is not yours,” Marta Vell said, “and it is not going to be, and you may ask me in nine days and I will tell you then, and if you ask me before nine days I will not tell you, and that is not a refusal, that is a week, and the week is mine to ask for and I have asked for it.”

---

At the fourth hour of the afternoon the post came up the lane, on a cart that does the Kells road twice a week, and the postmistress of Ocklebridge brought him one thing, and it was not from the north post because the north post does not stop at Ocklebridge on that run.

It was a letter in a stiff grey cover with a seal on it that was a ward board's seal and not a Crown's, and it had been in a bundle of Crown papers that a district rider had left at the Kells ward board on the second of the month, and Mercy Slee had found it in the bundle on the third, and it had gone into the Kells bag and come up the river road on the thirty-fifth because the ward board still has a bag on that cart and still keeps a bellmaker's name on the list of what that bag is for, which is on the list in her hand and has been since the eighth.

The letter itself is unsigned.

It is four paragraphs and it is written in a hand that is careful in the way that a hand is careful when the writer has had to write slowly with a cold finger, and it is the hand of a person who has written out a thing they have never written down before. It says that there are six of them in the house and that a form comes to the door once a year and is asked for the house and that a man used to live in it and does not live in it any more wrote in it, and that the district has sent the form back with his writing on it and a stamp on it, and that they have posted it back, and that it has now come back with his writing on it four more times, and that the years are on the copies and the oldest copy is six years old, and that they would like to know whose writing it is and whether they are permitted to write no instead, and that they do not know who to ask, and that the answer at the foot of the form in the small print says the form is to be addressed to the district, and that they have addressed it to the district twice, and that the second time a clerk wrote *forwarded* on the wrapper and sent it back, and that they do not want their name on it any more because of that.

There are no names in it. Not the household's and not the man's.

And on the outside of the cover, in a hand that is not Nell Fenn's and is not anybody's in that town except one woman, four lines:

*Mr Vale. This came to the board inside a rider's bundle of the district's own papers on the second and I have read it and I am not going to keep it in a drawer on a board for a week, because you are the only person left who is going to a door with it, and I have written your name on the bag list in the hand I always write in and that is all I have done. I have not asked this household for a name and I will not. They have written to a district office twice and a clerk wrote* forwarded *on the wrapper, and a person who has been forwarded is a person who has been answered by a box, and there is a girl of sixteen in this town who spent nineteen days on a count and put one question to that same office and got a better answer than the household got, and the reason she got it is that she was in a room, and the reason this household got* forwarded *is that they are not in a room, they are a lane, and an office has no instrument for a lane.*

*Do not answer them. I mean that. You are going to want to and there is nothing in that letter you can answer, because the only person who can answer it is the person whose hand is on the form, and he may be in it or he may be gone, and either way the office has him in a column and not in a house, and I have watched that sentence take a child out of a house in front of me this month.*

*You do not have to do anything with this. That is not me being kind. If you go up there and you do not get the household a good afternoon, it is because the good afternoon was never available and it is not on you and I will not have you carrying it if you do not have to.*

*And I have not been thanked and I am not going to be, and you may write that in whatever you are keeping and it will be the only line in it that I have asked for.*

*— Mercy Slee, of one hundred and nineteen, ward board, Kells.*

Tomas Vale read it twice on the step of a net-shop in a river village of about two hundred persons, in a stiff grey cover with a ward board's seal on it, and he wrote the hour in his book and the seal and the fact that the letter is unsigned, and then he wrote the four lines from Mercy Slee and did not improve them, because she had written them in a hand he has been reading for a month and he knew exactly what a Mercy Slee looked like when she had been careful, and she had been careful, and he was not going to be the man who improved it.
