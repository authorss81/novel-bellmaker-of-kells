# Chapter 0069 — The Approving Return

The approving return is on the second leaf of a schedule at the back of a file in a district office in a town called Helling, and it is the best piece of paper Tomas Vale has read in a month, and he did not like it at all when he read it and he has not stopped reading it since, and on the morning of the fifty-fourth he read it for the ninth time with a cup of cold water standing at his elbow and no particular reason to read it a ninth time.

He had asked for a copy on the forty-eighth and had been refused a copy in a room with about nine people in it, by a deputy who had told him in his own name that the office has no instrument for copying a return of a practice, and had then let him read the return where it sat for as long as he liked, and had stood at the counter while he did it without once looking at what he was doing, which was the most respectful thing that has happened to him in a month.

So what he had was not the document. It was his own four-column page and the hour, and the hour was the seventh hour of the afternoon of the forty-eighth, and the deputy's name at the bottom of the entry, which was **I. C., Deputy District Officer, this office, this day, about the sixth hour**, and his own hand recording that he had read a thing where it sat and had taken nothing.

He turned to the page now and read his own handwriting, and it was a fair hand, and it said:

> *An auditor's return of a practice. Recommended: that where a household is returned as unable to make up its mind, the office record one hand against the line and treat the line as answered. The return says this is a settlement of the difficulty. The difficulty is a household. The return is signed and the approval is signed and the approval is the name of a man and not a district.*
>
> *I have read it nine times over four days. Every word of it is correct. There is no sentence in it that is wrong. I have gone at it as a man goes at a form that has been written to do a thing, and it is not that, and I have not found the place where it does the thing, and it took a woman on a market square two days ago to show me where the thing is, and the thing is that it is not false.*

He put the pen down and did not write for about a quarter of an hour, and then he wrote four lines and dated them and they are in the book entire, and the third one is the one that decided the day:

> *A thing may be a machine and still be correct. I have been angry in a town four counties back for a month about a column and a stamp and a habit, and the column and the stamp and the habit have done exactly what they are for, and the man who designed them did it well, and he wrote down the reason for it and his name at the foot of it, and I have been carrying his name about for six days as though it were a threat.*
>
> *Nobody is to tell me to read it the other way. There is nobody here. That is the difficulty and it is not a small one: a thing I have got to be argued into is a thing I could have been got out of by a woman with a slate.*

---

The thing the approving return recommends, in the words the return uses, is these.

Where a household is returned as *unable to make up its mind*, the office does not leave the line open. The office records **one hand** against the line, in the answering column, and the line is answered, and the return is made up, and the schedule goes on to the next house.

The return does not say the hand is the household's. It does not say the hand is anybody's. It says that where a household cannot make up its mind, one hand is recorded and the line is answered, and it gives its reason, and the reason is this, and Tomas Vale copied it out in his own hand on the forty-eighth and read it nine times since and it is a good reason and he has not been able to get round it:

> *A household is not a person. A household is a number of persons who do not always agree and are not required to, and a return is a question put to a household, and the answer to a question put to several people who do not agree cannot be a sentence. The office is not required to hold the household in a room until it agrees. **The line is a line in a schedule and a schedule does not hold open. If a line may be held open then every household in the district that cannot agree is a line held open, and the cost of a line held open is a second round and a second round is a cart and a man and a day, and a man will not come twice to one door.** The office therefore records one hand and treats the line as answered, and the record is not false, and the household is informed, and the household may come and dispute it at the office at any hour the office is open.*

Every word of that is true. He has tested it nine ways over four days and he has not found a false statement in it and he has found two things in it that are not said and he has not been able to make either of them into a lie, and one of them is this, and he wrote it down on the fiftieth and it is still true on the fifty-fourth:

**A household is informed.** Some household. By some means. At some point. In a form or a letter or a word at a door or not at all.

And the other one is the harder one and he did not get to it until about the sixth hour of the evening of the fifty-first, on a market square, from a woman with a barrow who would not give a sentence until she was asked the fourth time, and he has been carrying it about three days and it has not got lighter:

**One hand does not know what the other four hands wanted.** It is not a copy of their answer. It is one person's answer, put in the place where a household's answer goes, and the record does not say it is anybody's, and so the record does not say it is not. A hand in a column is a fact about a hand. In a schedule it is a fact about a house, and the house cannot tell, because the house is not a person and has never been asked.

He got up at about the eighth hour and went down to the landing and had a cup of something hot and asked the man on the crane the question a man asks when he wants to know about a town and not about a man.

“What do people here do,” he said. “When a return comes and four people in a house do not agree.”

The man on the crane thought about it properly, which nobody had done for him in nine days.

“They send somebody,” he said. “From the district. Two of them, with a book.”

“And if the four of them still do not agree when the two of them are standing in the kitchen.”

“Then they put one of them down,” said the man on the crane, “and they go, and it is done, and there is a paper in a drawer somewhere in this town with one of them on it and nobody in that house can tell you which one.” He drank his cup. “That is not a thing that happens. That is a thing that happened. It has happened in about nine houses in this town that I know of and I am not going to tell you which nine, and I have got a mother in one of them and I did not know for about two years until she died and a woman from the district came to the door about the paper.”

He put his cup down.

“Do you want the district office or do you want the houses,” he said.

“The houses.”

“**Then do not come to me again, and there is a woman on the far side of the square who takes in washing and has been at this nine years and knows every door in that town and there is not one thing she knows that is not paid for, and I have just told you that and I would like it noticed that I told you.**”

---

Ivet Slade took in washing at the far side of the square in a front room with a copper in the yard and about nine lines out on a day, and she was doing a sheet when he came in and she did not stop, and he stood by the copper for about four minutes, which is a thing men do and she let him get through it.

“I am not here about the washing.”

“Then you are here about nothing, because a man who is not here about the washing is here about the district and about one sheet, and they are all the same sheet.” She wrung it out. “Say it and go, and I am working, and I have nine lines out.”

“I have read a document,” he said, “and I cannot give you a copy of it and I cannot read it to you entire, and it is an auditor's return about a practice, and the practice is that where a household cannot make up its mind, one hand is recorded against the line and the line is answered, and I have written out the part of it that says the reason, and I have written the reason out in my own hand and I will show you that, and that is the whole of what I have.”

She stopped with the sheet.

“Then show me that,” she said, “and not the other thing, and if you have come to tell me that I am on one I have been on one since I was a child and I knew it inside a year, and you did not come here to tell me that.”

“No. I did not come here to tell you that either, because I do not know that you are, and I am not going to find out by asking you.”

“Then what do you want.”

“**I want you to read the reason.**” He put the day-book on the table by the copper, open, and turned it round, and did not touch the page. “That is a return in a district office four counties from here and the return says the whole of the practice is a good answer to a good question and gives the reason in about ninety words, and I have read it nine times in four days and there is nothing wrong with it, and I have been carrying it round this town for two days looking for somebody it has been done to and I want you to tell me whether I am looking at the right shape of thing, and if I am not, I will go away and I will not come back to this front room again.”

Ivet Slade came and read the page. She was not a quick reader and she did not pretend to be. She read the ninety words twice, and the second time she put her thumb on the first line of the reason and went along it, and at the end of the second time she took her thumb off and wiped her hands down her apron and stood up straight and said:

“That is the office's reason and it is a good one and I have never in nine years had anybody stand in my front room and say it out loud in writing, and I want you to know that you have just done the single most useful thing anybody has done in my life about a sheet of paper, and it is no use to me whatsoever, and both of those are true at once and I would like both of them said in whatever you are writing.”

“What I am writing is the half that is no use to you,” he said, “and it is this. **Where a household cannot make up its mind, the office records one hand and the line is answered. The record does not say the hand is the household's. And the household is told, and the household may come and dispute it at any hour the office is open.** That is the sentence. Not a lie. A sentence that is a door, and the door is a real door, and it opens at office hours.”

“And the office is open from the ninth to the four.”

“Where I am from it is open from the tenth to the second. I do not know about yours and I am not going to guess.”

“It is the ninth to the four here and it is the ninth to the four everywhere and it has been for about a hundred years and everybody knows it and the office is shut at the four because the clerks go home and the office is shut on the last day of a round because the cart has gone.” She sat down on the bench by the copper. “**That is the whole of it, that is the whole of it, and nobody has ever said it to me and you have made me say it in a room with a stranger in it.**”

She told it in about nine sentences and he did not write while she was doing it, and afterwards he asked her whether he might.

“You can write it down,” said Ivet Slade. “You may not write my name against it, and that is not because I am frightened of my name, and I will tell you why, because I have nine lines out and about forty houses on my list and I have taken in washing at this address for nine years and every one of those forty knows exactly where I am at any hour of any day, and there is not one door in this town that has ever asked me for a piece of paper, and that is the reason I have said yes to you, and if my name goes in a book with a hand against it then I am a household and somebody will be able to come and find out who I live with and who does my washing and whether I pay my rates, and I have not got a form for a person of my own, and a person of my own is a thing that is on a roll somewhere, and I have spent nine years being a woman who is not on anything, and it is the only thing I have got and I am not giving it up for a page in a book about other people.”

“You do not have to be on anything. I am not going to put your name anywhere.”

“Write it as the woman at the copper,” she said. “That is not a joke, it is a description, and there is a copper in the yard and anybody in this town who wants to find me can walk in here and say *the woman at the copper*, and nobody can walk into a district office and say that, and that is the whole of what a copper is.”

He wrote *the woman at the copper* and read it back and she made him change the comma, and then she sat on the bench with her hands in her lap for about a minute and said:

“Now tell me the other half. You have not told me what you want and you have been in this room for about eleven minutes and you have not asked me for one single thing and I have been waiting since about the fourth minute. So say it, and if it is a favour I will tell you what it costs and if it is money I do not have it and if it is a thing I can do then you are in the wrong front room.”

“I am not going to ask you for anything at all. I want to give you a decision to make and I am the wrong person to make it and that is why I am here.”

---

That is the whole of it, and it took about two hours, and it is the passage of the fifty-fourth that decides the fifty-sixth, and it is in his day-book in four columns with the hour against each one, and the four columns are the four things she was told before she was asked to decide anything, and he did not merge them, and she made him read all four back and then she chose.

**One.** *The return says the practice is a settlement of a difficulty, and the difficulty is a household, and the difficulty is real, and a schedule does not hold open, and a second round is a cart and a man and a day and a man will not come twice to one door. She agrees with all of it. She said the district is right about the cart and she has never once seen anybody in this town who could be got to agree to anything by being asked twice, and that is not a complaint about her neighbours, it is an observation she has made from a front room for nine years.*

**Two.** *The return says the household may come and dispute the line at any hour the office is open. She says the office is open from the ninth to the four and has been for about a hundred years, and the clerks go home at the four, and the office is shut on the last day of a round because the cart has gone, and no door in this town has ever been at a district office in its life.*

**Three.** *The record does not say the hand is the household's and does not say it is not. She says her household is five and there is a sister in it who has not been asked a question about a return in nine years and does not know there is a line with a hand on it, and that when the sister asked her, two years ago, what her own hand on a piece of paper meant, Ivet Slade told her it meant that somebody had been to the house, and she has thought about that answer every week since.*

**Four.** *And the cost, both ways, which he gave her and which she made him put in the book in the plainest words he could find and which he did not improve:*

> *If the line is opened, the office has to send a second round to a house, and the second round is a cart and a man and a day, and the district is right that the man will not want to come, and the day it comes the five people in that house are a house that has been sent to twice and the second time is the one that costs, and the thing that gets taken out of a house is never a hand in a column. It is a lease, or a rent, or a landlord, or a removal to two rooms over a yard, and the person who is in the column is not the person it happens to, and nobody ever asks that, and in another town four counties back it has already happened to a family and the man who ordered it was owed money and is not a villain.*
>
> *And if the line is not opened, the sister goes on the roll as if she had agreed to something she was never asked, forever, and there is no cost to the district and no cost to the household in money at all, and no way at all of finding out what it costs, and that is the reason it will not be opened by anybody, and the reason is not wickedness. It is that the second thing is free and quiet and it is on paper, and the first thing is expensive and it happens at a door.*

She read that twice and then sat on the bench with her hands in her lap and looked at the copper for about a minute and a half.

“Right,” she said. “Then I have decided, and you may write it down in your own hand, because it is a decision and a decision is a thing that is worth having on paper, and I am not asking you to take it anywhere.

“**I am not going to open the line. And I am going to have my own answer put in, in my own hand, on my own paper, and I am going to take it to that office myself on a morning when it is open, and I am going to ask them to write down that the line is answered and that the hand on it is mine and not my sister's and not anybody else's, and I am going to say it out loud to a clerk.**”

“That is not the same as opening the line.”

“No,” said Ivet Slade. “It is not. And I have thought very hard about it for about two minutes and I am not a fool, and I know exactly what it is: it is me standing in a room with a hand in a column and saying *that one is mine*. And what it buys is that in nine years, when somebody comes with a piece of paper about my household, there is one line in it that a woman came in and claimed, and it is hers, and it was not taken, and she was not frightened, and it says the hour on it.

“**And what it costs is that my sister is on that line for the rest of her life and she will never know that she is on it, and I am not going to tell her, because she is not going to ask, and because if I tell her she will spend a year being frightened about a form and I have watched that happen to two other women on this street and both of them are easier now and neither of them is any happier.**”

He wrote it down, and he read it back, and she corrected one word and he made the change, and then he said the thing he had come to that front room to say and had not said until then.

“I am not going to come back here.”

“You are not.”

“Not tomorrow, and not in a week, and not when it goes badly, and not when somebody in an office has been unkind to you about a sheet of paper, and not if I find out in four days that I was wrong about all of it. **You have made a decision and you are going to carry it to a building yourself, and if I come back here after that then whatever I want from you next is not a question, it is a consequence, and you would not be able to tell the difference, and neither would I.** I am writing that down at about the second hour of the afternoon and I am going to read it back to you and then I am going to shut the book.”

He read it back and she said, “That is fair, and it is more than fair, and you do not need to be that careful with me, and I am saying that as a person who has been doing this on her own for four years and would have got on with it perfectly well without a man coming round with a day-book.”

“I know you would have.”

“Then why are you being careful.”

“**Because I have been careful with about four people in a month and every time it has been the correct thing to do and not one time has it been comfortable, and I have noticed that pattern in myself about four days ago and I do not like it, and I would rather say so in a book than go on being careful and call it kindness.**”

She laughed at that, which she had not been going to do.

---

## The post comes up

The post came up at about the fifth hour of the evening and it is the fastest thing on the reach and it does not wait, and a man off it took eleven letters off the slip and would not give anybody a story about a single one of them.

One of the eleven was for him. It was a square of paper with the corners turned down and an old fold in it, and the address was in a hand he had been looking at for about nine days in a smith's shop, upside down, under a mark, in a column, and it was small and upright and it had not been written in a hurry, and it had been written twice, because in the third line the same word had been started and abandoned and begun again.

He took it up the outside stair and opened it standing at the window, because it was still light, and the frame was in the yard behind the shop with the bolts made fast where it was set down, and the bell was on the sill in its crate with the sacking over it.

> *Vale. The first name on my page is not going to Kells. It is not going down the water and it is not going into a book with a stamp on it, and I am not going to be asked for it a second time by anybody, and I have had two days since your letter came and I have not been able to write until this morning, and the reason I have not been able to write is not that I was busy.*
>
> *I set nine days and the ninth was the forty-fourth and you did not come, and I did not send to you, and nobody has written to me about it but you. So there is nothing wrong with me and you should not go telling yourself that there is. The day was mine and it has gone and I am not making another one. I have a bell at about the seventh hour every morning and it has been there thirty years and it will be there tomorrow, and there is nothing in that bell that belongs to you or to your district, and I am not sorry about the bell and I am not going to be talked into being sorry about it by a man who is thirty miles off with a barrow.*
>
> *You are not a bad man and I have thought about that for two days, and I want it written that I have thought about it, because you did not write on the forty-fifth and I heard that afterwards from a carter, and I would have written on the forty-fifth.*
>
> *I am going to give you one thing and it is not the thing you asked for, and you may make what you can of it. When they entered my bell they came to the door and they told me a day, and the day they told me is the day it goes in the book, and it is not the day they came. I have never seen them before and I have never seen them since and there was no man at my door on any day at all. I keep a water bell and I keep the days of things, and I know what the day a thing was asked for looks like, and the day I was given is not it. It is earlier. I have not been able to find out by how much.*
>
> *That is one fact and it is a fact about a day and not about a name, and I have said it out loud to nobody in nine years, and I am saying it to a bellmaker in a town I have never been to because you wrote to me and asked me one question and did not ask me for the thing.*
>
> *I have rung the water bell this morning. It is the same as it was. That is all I have.*
>
> *Marta Vell, of the water bell at Ocklebridge, this date, the seventh hour.*

He read it four times and then he took out the day-book and copied it out entire, word for word, including the place in the third line where the word had been started and begun again, and he read the copy back against the original letter and then he wrote under it, and this is in the book entire:

> *The fifty-fourth, about the fifth hour of the evening. The answer is no, and it is the first refusal of a term she set, and it is hers to give and she has given it, and it cost her two days and a word begun twice.*
>
> *The debt was mine and I am writing it down in the plainest words I have: I let a term of hers run out without asking her whether it still stood, and I did not do it out of carelessness. I did it because on the forty-fifth I found out I was going to want something, and I decided a man who wants something should not be the one to reopen it. That is a rule I invented on the fortieth of last month and it has cost me the only thing I went to Ocklebridge for, and I would do it again on about half the days, and I am not going to pretend to a reader of this book that I would do it again tomorrow.*
>
> *And she has given me a fact. A date. The day a line went into a book about a water bell, and the day was not the day anything happened at her door, and there was no man at her door on any day at all. I am going to write that down and I am not going to do anything with it tonight and I am not going to join it to the five dates.*
>
> *It is not one of the five. The five are dates against certified returns in a drawer in a house two miles past a water bell, six in that house, one name, one hand, and a brother-in-law who has not been in the house for six years. This one is a date against a line about a bell in a book with a number on the spine in a building in Fourmile. **They are two different documents and a person who joins them has joined them because they are both dates, and I did not come thirty miles to be a person who joins things because they are the same shape.** I do not know how the five dates got there. I wrote on the fortieth that I do not know and am not going to guess, and I have got a sixth fact of the same kind in my hand this evening and I am still not going to guess, and I would like it recorded that holding a sixth one did not make me feel cleverer and I had expected it to.*
>
> *What it does is this, and it is small and it is the only thing I have got: **the office is given a day and enters the day it is given, and the day it is given is a fact about the office and not about the thing.** A woman kept a bell for thirty years and was given a day, and the day is earlier, and she knew, and nobody ever asked her. I have no use for that. I have got no household and no bell and no schedule and no standing, and I could not open a line in a book in this county if the Crown paid me. And I have written it down, and it is the first fact anybody has given me in a month that I could not have got out of a form.*

He did not write to her again. He read the carbon of a river-post receipt in the inside of his coat, which had a date on it, and the date was the fifty-second, and he put it back, and that was the whole of the reckoning, and there was no second letter and there was no second asking, and both of those are hers and not his.

---

He was in the room over the draper's at about the eighth hour of the evening of the fifty-fourth with the lamp turned down and the day-book open on his knee, and he was copying out the four columns of the front room into the book entire, word for word, including *the woman at the copper* and the comma she had made him change, and it took about ninety minutes, and it is the longest single entry in the book.

And at about the ninth hour of the evening, when he had shut it and was sitting with the lamp turned down, there was a knock on the outside stair, and it was a rider who had come up the landing road at a walk and who had a letter in a hand that was not a post hand, and who would not give it to a woman at the door.

He said, “Is the man with the barrow at this house.”

“He is not from the Crown,” said the woman at the door, in the voice of a woman who has said that sentence in a lane about nine times, “and he has said so himself about nine times.”

“Then I will come up.”

The rider came up and read the direction on the letter and gave it over and put his finger on the address, which was a name and a town and nothing else, no district, no parish, no parish of Kells, and then he said:

“**That is not a Crown hand and that is not a district hand and I have carried four letters this month that were on a Crown hand and this is not one of them, and I asked the man who gave it me at the yard because I do not want to have brought a thing up nine miles to the wrong door and be told off for it.** And the other thing I will say, and I say it to about nine people a week and it is true of all of them, and it is that the man who gave it me read the address off the top of a page with nine lines on it and there was a great deal of writing on that page and he did not write any of it himself, and the hand is not his.”

“Then you have read the letter.”

“No. **I have not read the letter, and I would not, and if I had, I would not be able to un-read it, and a rider who has read it is a rider who knows a thing in a town he does not live in.**”

He was gone down the stair and down the road at a walk, and Tomas Vale turned the letter over and looked at the wax, and it was good wax and it was a private seal and it was not a Crown device, and on the back, in the same good upright hand, somebody had written four words in pencil, which were the courtesy, and the courtesy was *at your convenience*; and in the other half of the same upright hand, with a different pen and about nine hours old, somebody had written *replies to follow*, and had underlined it once.

He did not open it that night.

He wrote, in the flyleaf at the back where he keeps the reasons, and it is in the book entire:

> *The fifty-fourth. A rider came up the landing road at the ninth hour of the evening with a letter that is not a Crown hand, and I have not opened it and I am not going to open it tonight, and the reason is not care and it is not caution and I am not going to pretend to myself that it is either of those things.*
>
> *It is that I have spent four days in a town being careful in public, and I have written down about eleven things in a book that I have not acted on, and a letter arriving at the ninth hour of the evening is the most interesting thing that has happened to me since a woman at an inn yard read four clauses out loud, and I can feel myself getting ready to be interested, and I know what that feels like, because it is the same feeling I had at about the ninth hour of the morning of the thirty-second in a watch house when a man in a chair read back three things I had wanted, and I went out of that building the best I had felt in a month and I had learnt absolutely nothing.*
>
> *A letter is not a door. A man on circuit is not a door. I have said that in a book about Marten Cope and I meant it as a compliment and it was the complaint, and I am not going to make that mistake twice in a county, and the test is simple and it is the only one I have got: if a thing makes me want to be a better man by the morning, it is not a door, it is a compliment, and I have had about two of those in a month and one of them was from a woman in a smith's shop who told me that a man cannot be a good keeper of a page of paper.*
>
> *The woman at the copper made a decision at about the second hour of the afternoon and I read it back to her and shut the book, and I am not going back, and I have written down why, and the reason is that after a decision the only thing I could want next is a consequence and she would not be able to tell the difference and neither would I.*
>
> *And the approving return is still the best piece of paper I have read in a month and it is still signed by a man whose name I have had for six days and have not once been told what to think about him by anybody, and nobody in this county knows his name, and I have written it out twice now and both times I could not make it into a lie, and the second time I came nearer, and near is not far.*
>
> *The letter is on the table. I am going to bed. If the man whose name is on the return has written to me, then tomorrow I will walk into a room with a man who has read a thing before he wrote to me, and I will find out what that is like, and I have been asking people what a document is for a month and I have a fairly good idea that I do not know.*

At about the seventh hour of the morning of the fifty-fifth he opened it. It was two lines and it was signed, and the name at the foot of it was the name at the foot of the approving return, and the letter said:

> *Mr Vale. I understand from a deputy at Helling that you have been reading a return of mine in that office and that you were courteously refused a copy. There is a market in this town that runs to the fifty-third and I am on circuit for it, and I have a room until the fifty-seventh, and if you are still here I am at your service at any hour you can name, and I mean that literally, and you may name a late one. Bring nothing. I am told you carry a great deal and it will all be safe, and I have no use for it, and I will not be permitted to keep it.*
>
> *I do not know your business and I have not looked into it, and I would rather you did not tell me. What I have in my bag is a schedule this town keeps about a market, and you may look at it where it sits, and if you would rather I did not take it out of my bag in a room then I will not, and I will tell you that I have had it in the bag for two days and read it twice, and I do not think it is good of me to have read it twice, and I am telling you so that you will not waste an hour finding out whether I have.*
>
> *Pell Rill, auditor, at your service, in this town until the fifty-seventh.*
