# Chapter 0064 — The House They Left

She started at the fourth hour and she did not have a slate, which she had given away on the water on the evening before with a man called Osgood, and the first thing she did about it was buy a second-hand slate off a stallholder at the top of the market for a penny and a half, and the second thing she did about it was write on the back of it, before she did anything else, in her own hand, with the hour:

*No slate. Second-hand, from a stall, a penny and a half. The first one is on the water in a man's coat and I did not ask for it back and I am not going to. I have got a thing I can write on and I have not got a thing anybody can look at over my shoulder, and I noticed the difference about four minutes after the penny and a half and that is the whole of what I got for it and it is not what I got it for.*

The three questions were the same three and she used the same words and she did not improve one of them, which took her about a mile of walking to be certain of.

*First: has any person ever put their name to a paper for this house — a Crown form, or a return, or a certificate — without anybody asking them to?*

*Second: is the person who put their name there living in this house now?*

*Third: do you want that written down, and may I write your no as a no and not as nothing.*

---

The house is at the top of a lane about a hundred yards from the district office and it is a good house, two floors, a door with a name on it in a tin frame that has been painted over four times, and a small garden wall, and about six rooms that a person could keep in a week.

There were four people in it on the forty-ninth and there had been four people in it for about two years and there were three of them in the two rooms over a yard two streets down, and the fourth was away.

The three said no to the first question at three separate doors and said it fast, and one of them said, at the second door, in a voice with nothing in it at all, “That is what they say about us in the lane.”

“What is what they say about you in the lane.”

“That we are not our sort any more.” She said it and then she shut the door, and it was not an answer to the second question, and Tamsin Rook wrote it down as a sentence and put the sentence in a column of its own, and by the fourth house the sentence had become a paragraph.

---

That is the first thing that went wrong that day and it went wrong in about forty minutes and nothing anybody did caused it.

She had it on the slate in a column. At the fourth house it was on the slate in a column. At the sixth house she wrote it out in the margin with the hour and the door, and at the ninth house a man read it upside down and said, “That is a hard thing to write about people,” and at the eleventh house a woman repeated it back to her and could not remember whether she had heard it or had made it up, and by the second hour of the afternoon it was in a conversation she was not part of, on the market side of the street, in the mouths of people who had never been up the lane.

She had done it in Kells. She had done it in a ward of about four hundred people and she had been proud of it and she had put it in a book and Mercy Slee had had it in a ward's book.

**A sentence gets merged. That is the whole of it. A refusal gets merged and a sentence gets merged and the third question stops the first kind and does nothing at all about the second, and I have been standing in eleven places this month telling people that a refusal is a sentence, and a refusal is a sentence, and I said that to a woman at a door in this town at about the sixth hour today and she agreed with me and it did not stop her saying what she had heard about a family in a lane, and it is not supposed to, and I want it written that I know that it is not supposed to and that I said it anyway because it is the sentence I have.**

---

The second thing that went wrong was the leash, and it went wrong at about the seventh hour at the bottom of a lane with a man and a barrow's handle in her hand.

They were at the end of the yard by the two rooms. Tomas Vale had come up from the street with his book out and there was a woman at a door and the two of them had about nine minutes of her.

“Can I ask you to do the second door.”

Tamsin Rook put her book down on the wall.

“No.”

“It is the same three questions and they are the same words and my name would not be on them.”

“No,” she said. “And I am not going to give you the reason twice, and I have given it four times in a month and I have never improved it, and the reason is not about a lane and it is not about a door. **The reason is that you have a barrow and a frame in a cradle in it and I have a bag, and the moment a person at a door has to decide which of us to believe, that door is going to have a bellmaker in it and a courier in it, and there is going to be a document at that door with two names on it and one keeper in the box.** I have been the second name on a piece of paper four times in eleven years and one of them was about a child and I have never got it back, and I am not doing it in a yard in a town where the district office is a hundred yards away and has just written to a landlord about this lane.”

“I am not asking twice,” Tomas Vale said.

“I know you are not,” said Tamsin Rook. “That is what a leash is, and it is four lines on a slate in a town you cannot get back to in a week, and it cost me one sentence and I have written the hour against it, and you may not ask me again today and I will not be asked again today, and neither of us has said one word of the other's work.”

He went and stood about forty yards off with the barrow and the book.

And that is the third thing that went wrong, and it went wrong because of the leash and not in spite of it, and she has written it in her own book in her own hand with the hour and has not improved it, and it is the finding of the day:

**He had to go himself, because I said no, and going himself put him at a door in a yard where a Crown object was standing at the end of the lane in the open, because a man who has said no to a question will not come and stand in the lane to keep watch, and he stood at the lane mouth where anybody coming out of a door could see nine feet of barrow and a frame and four bolts in it.**

**And it was seen. By the woman at the first door of the two rooms, and by a man carrying a sack in the alley, and by a boy who went up the lane at a run and came back down it at a run and could not be got to say who sent him. And by about the seventh hour of the evening, four people in that lane knew that there was a man with a Crown object standing at the top of it asking questions at the doors, and I could not have stopped it and neither could he, and it is the cost of my no and I do not think I was wrong, and I want both of those in the same sentence in my own hand.**

---

The landlord is called **Rowland Fisk** and he has nine properties in this town and a yard off a lane and four men who work for him and about eleven houses he lets, and he is owed four months' rent by three tenants whose names he would not give her, and he is not owed anything by the three in the two rooms, who have paid to the week.

She had him at about the eighth hour, in his own yard, with a ledger on a bench and a boy copying from it, and he did not ask her who she was and he did not ask her to leave, and he gave her about nine minutes before he said anything at all.

She asked him the three questions, in the same words, in the same order, and the third one to a landlord is: *do you want that written down, and may I write your no as a no and not as nothing.*

He said no to the first two and he said no to the third as well, and then he shut the ledger and put his thumb in the middle of it and said his answer, and it took about four minutes and it is the whole of what a landlord in this town would have said.

“I will tell you the whole of it because you have asked me three questions and you have not told me one story, and I have got about four tenants who will not tell me the truth about a drain and I have not had a conversation with anybody in a yard in a month.”

“All right.”

“There is a woman in a house at the top of that lane and I let her the house on a year and I have not seen her for about two years and the rent comes in at the postmistress's. And there are three people in two rooms over my yard and they are not late and they have not been late and they pay on the day, and I have had the two rooms off them since the spring at a rent I set myself and I have never once put up a notice and I am not going to, and I will tell you why and then you may ask me the three questions again in any words you like and I will give you the same answer twice.

**I moved them because the two rooms are warmer and there are three of them and the house is a house and not three rooms, and I did it because the woman at the top of the lane came to me with a paper and a paper from the district and the district says the man at that address is not at that address, and I said, then who is, and she said she did not know, and the district does not know either because I asked them, and it is their line and it is not my line.**”

“You asked the district.”

“In October, in the ordinary way, on a form any landlord may send, and they answered it in nine days on their own paper with a date on it, and the answer is a good answer and it is the whole of what I have ever been given by that office, and I have got it in a drawer in that house because I keep everything anybody sends me about my own houses, and I will not let you have it and I will not read it to you and I will tell you what it says, because I have read it about nine times and I have shown it to nobody.”

“Please.”

“It says: *the address is in the district. The office does not hold a record of the persons at the address. The office is not able to say who is at the address.*”

The yard was quiet for a moment and a boy copied a figure into a ledger and got it wrong.

“That is nine words of **I do not know** in a Crown hand with a date on it,” said Tamsin Rook, “and a landlord cannot act on a Crown's *I do not know*, and so he acted on the man instead.”

“I did not act on the man,” said Rowland Fisk. “I acted on the paper. **The paper says there is an address and no man at it and I have a woman at the address with two others and I am owed four months by three houses I cannot name and I want that lane quiet.** That is the whole of it and I am not going to be ashamed of it in front of a stranger and I am not going to be praised for saying it either.”

“You should have shown it to her.”

“I have thought about that for about four months and I have not done it, and I know what you are going to tell me and I have told myself,” said Rowland Fisk, “and I am not going to be moved by a woman with a book about a thing I have thought about every day since the spring, and I have got nine houses and a yard and a lad who cannot copy a figure, and the district does not know who is at my address and neither do I, and neither does the woman, and the one thing I am sure of is that I am not going to be the man who got a street wrong about a family because a piece of Crown paper would not tell me who was in a house.”

He picked the ledger up.

“Now the other half, and then I am going to work. **You have been round about nine doors asking three questions and you have not asked me one of the three that matters, and I am going to tell you what it is so that you can go away and write it down, and I am not doing it for you.**”

“All right.”

“Who signed it,” said Rowland Fisk. “The line for that address has got a name on it, and the name is a man's, and the man is not in the house and has not been for two years, and I have never met him and I have never wanted to, and the whole of the difficulty in that lane is that the only person the district has ever heard from about that house is a man who does not live in it. **And he is not living in it because he does not live in it. He is somewhere else. He is alive, I should think, and I have no reason to know that and I have not asked.**”

She wrote it down. She read it back and he made her change one word, and she changed it, and it is the only cooperation she got out of the whole of that day.

---

She did not persuade anybody. She did not use Tomas Vale's name at a single door, not once, in nine hours.

On the fifth door she put the bag down where it could be seen. She did it deliberately, in the lane, in the open, before she said the first question, and she said the reason out loud to the woman in the doorway, and the reason is in her book.

“I am going to put this bag on the ground in front of you, and there is a bell in it, and it is a handbell and it is mine and I am a courier, and I am not going to use any of that to get anything out of you today and I want you to see it while I am asking, because in about four days somebody is going to come up this lane and use it, and I would rather you had seen me put it down than be told afterwards that a courier with a bell had been asking you things.”

“That is new,” said the woman.

“It is about eleven days old and it is a fortnight late and I am not proud of it and I am doing it anyway.”

---

The thing she is good at did not work.

That is the finding and it took her the whole of the second hour of the afternoon to be able to write it, and she wrote it four times and tore up three of them, and what is in her book is this:

*A slate, a road and a witness. That is the whole of what I have and I have said it in four counties in a month as though it were a method and it is not a method, it is me, and it works on a person who is frightened or ashamed or who has never been asked anything in eleven years, and it does not work on a man who has nine houses and a yard and a lad who cannot copy a figure and who has thought about the same thing every day since the spring.*

*He did not want a record. That is the sentence I have not got round to in nine days of doors. Every person I have written down in a month wanted their no on paper, because a no on paper is the only shape a no has got and it is the only thing anybody can be held to. **Rowland Fisk does not want his no on paper. He wants to be left alone, and he is owed four months' rent by people who are not the ones who are leaving, and the district does not know who is at his address, and he has a piece of Crown paper with a date on it that does not say what he needs it to say and cannot be made to say it.** A record does not help a man in that position. A record is a second thing he has to keep.*

*And I went at him with three questions built for a frightened woman at a door and a man who has been forwarded twice, and he answered all three correctly and gave me more than anybody on the lane did, and he gave it to me and not to the lane, and I am going to go back to that yard on another day and ask him the fourth question and I am not going to ask him the first three again, and I do not know whether he will answer the fourth, and I have to write down that I have a plan with a landlord in it, and it is the first plan I have had in a month that involves a person instead of a paper, and I do not know what to do with a person.*

---

At about the sixth hour of the evening the man with the barrow came up the lane and asked her whether she had finished, and she said she had, and he said he had been up the lane at the seventh hour and there were four people at the top of it who had seen a Crown object standing in the open, and that by the second hour of the afternoon it was a Crown carrier that had come to ask the Ilotts about a form, and that by the fifth hour it was the district that had sent it, and that by the sixth hour it was the Crown that had put them out of their house.

“That is not what happened,” Tamsin Rook said.

“That is what it is,” said Tomas Vale. “And I am not going to tell you it is your no, because it is not your no, and it is not my question either, and it is not a fair thing to have happened to a house in a lane, and here is the thing I have got to write down before I do anything else: **four people heard me ask a question in a lane and about a dozen people heard that there was a Crown object in the lane, and by the second hour of the afternoon there was a Crown object and a district and a removal, and nobody in that lane said a single untrue thing.** Every single step of that was somebody being careful and lawful and right. That is the fifth time in twelve days and I am going to stop being surprised by it and start being frightened of it.”

“No,” said Tamsin Rook. “You are not going to be frightened of it, because being frightened of it is how a man makes a story about it in an hour, and there is nobody in it who deserves one. What you are going to do is write the nine words down and find out whether a district office has ever said *I do not know who is at an address* in its own name with a date on it, and if it has, then a landlord in nine towns in four counties has got that paper in a drawer and has done about what Rowland Fisk did, and none of them is a villain and every one of them is short of money in a bad year.”

“There is an office back up the bank road,” said Tomas Vale. “Three days up. There is a man in it and he answers in his own name within the hour and he files what he declines to say in a book where any person may ask for it.”

“Yes,” said Tamsin Rook. “And when you get there, do not ask him whether a landlord has been sent a paper about an address. He will give you a straight answer and it will be about his own office. **Ask him what a district office is, when a landlord writes in and asks who is at an address, and what it is that the office then knows.**”

She shouldered her bag and went down the lane, and the bell was in it, and it was not rung, and nobody asked her for a reason, and a woman at the second of the two rooms called after her to say that the man with the barrow was not from the Crown and had said so himself at the top of the lane, and that he had said it about nine times.
