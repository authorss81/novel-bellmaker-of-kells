# Chapter 0074 — The Clerk Who Is Not Hiding

He had a number for it and the number was five, and he had said four to himself on the way down the lane, and he wrote both figures in the margin of the page and did not pick one, because a man who picks one of his own numbers in a margin is doing arithmetic for the reader.

The five were a deputy at a counter in Helling who had written for eleven minutes without being asked past what he had been asked for; an officer at Fourmile who had refused him in his own name and put the refusal where anybody might ask for it; a master in a room over a saddler's shop who had answered a question in front of six people and would not leave a second copy behind; an auditor in a room over a haberdasher's shop who had read a document twice and said so in a letter before he was asked anything; and a young clerk in a street at about the sixth hour of the evening on the fifty-eighth who had told him to ask him properly and then had answered.

**And the round was in Miremarket on the sixtieth**, which he had not known until the fourth hour of the morning and found out in about ninety seconds from a boy with a barrow, and it explained the town better than the whole of the fifty-eighth had.

---

The boy told him because Tomas Vale asked him a question and then did not wait, which is a thing you can do with a boy and cannot do with a man.

“They’ve come.”

“Who.”

“The round. Came in about the eighth hour of the morning and it’s at the counting house now and there’s money in it and they’re paying out at the yard behind the cross until dark.” The boy had a barrow and about nine of somebody else’s fish in it. “It comes twice and the second one’s the one that matters.”

“Good.”

“It always the second one that matters,” said the boy, and went.

And Tomas Vale stood in a street in a town of two thousand people and understood the whole of it in about four seconds, and it was this: **a town with a round in it is a town with money in it, and the money comes out of a hall, and the hall is the reason the town is not unhappy, and nobody in the town is frightened, and nobody is wrong.**

The round’s cart stood in a yard behind the cross from about the eighth hour of the morning until about the fourth hour of the afternoon, and the paying-out was done at a table under a tarpaulin with a clerk at it, and about two hundred people went through that yard in a day, and Tomas Vale stood at the edge of it for about forty minutes and was not asked to move and was not asked anything at all, and watched about a hundred and forty hands go into a book.

**Not one of them was a mark in a column and not one of them was asked what the mark meant.** He had come five weeks looking for a place where a column and a stamp had swallowed a person, and there were a hundred and forty people putting their own right hands into a book in a yard in the open air, in daylight, in front of a clerk, one at a time, and no face in that yard had any doubt in it at all.

He wrote it down at about the second hour of the afternoon and the line is in the day-book entire and it is the shortest line in the book:

> *A hundred and forty people, a hundred and forty hands, a hundred and forty names, and a clerk at a table. And every one of them chose to be there. I have been trying for five weeks to find a person in a column and I stood in a yard for forty minutes and did not find one, and I would like somebody to explain to me what I am doing.*

---

He asked the front door again at about the fourth hour of the afternoon, and Ivo Trant was at it, and the answer was the same, and it was correct, and Tomas Vale had it in his mouth before the man had finished it.

“There is nothing by which you may ask me for anything. I said it yesterday. There is a form and it has no box for a reason.”

“I am not here to ask you for anything and I have not got the form, I gave it back.”

“That is the first time anybody has given it back,” said Trant. “What is it, then.”

And Tomas Vale, who had a sheet with four questions on it in his own hand and had put it in at four offices, did the thing he has done for five weeks and could not stop doing, and he asked a man a question instead of asking a building a thing.

“**Which of your clerks goes out on the round.**”

Trant looked at him for slightly longer than a man needs to.

“That is a question about the work,” he said. “It is not a question about a document and it is not a question about a column and you have not asked me for anything. There are four. There are always four, and it is not always the same four, and one of the four is the man who fills the answering column and he goes out because the answering column is what the round is for, and his name is Elias Nabb and he has been in this hall about eleven years and he is not a man anybody in this town has ever had a word with.”

“Thank you.”

“Do not thank me. **I will tell you what you have done and I would rather say it than have you find it out.** You have found a hole in my door in about nine seconds and the hole is that I will answer a question about the work, and any man in this kingdom could put a boot through that, and about four men in this kingdom have. And I have not put it there on purpose and I have not got a rule against it, and I have been twenty-two years in this building and I have never once thought about it, and I would like you to go and do your work.”

“Yes,” said Tomas Vale. “I will.”

And then, because it was true and because it is the whole of what the man is, he said the other half of it.

“I am going to say what I am going to do, and then I am going to do something I do not want to do, and I would rather you heard it here than worked out on a step. I am not going to ask that clerk to show me the roll or give me a page or say a word in a hall. **I am going to stand in the street at about the eighth hour of this evening until that building is shut, and when the four come out I am going to ask Elias Nabb one question in his own name, in the street, in front of anybody who is there, and if he says no I will not ask a clerk anything in this town for a week.**”

Trant turned the form round so that the boxes faced him.

“Then I will tell you the half of it that is yours,” he said. “**A man who says he will not ask again is worth about four men who did not say it.** I have had about eleven in this counter in four years and I would take the eleven.”

---

He was still standing in the street at about the fourth hour of the afternoon and he was still standing there at about the eighth hour of the evening, and he wrote that down too, because a man who writes down the hours he spent standing outside a building is doing the only honest thing available to him.

The hall shut at about the seventh hour. Four people came out. One of them was a young man with nothing under his arm who crossed the street at a run, went about nine steps, stopped, came back, and said:

“You stood here four hours.”

“Five,” said Tomas Vale.

“**Five.**” The young man looked at him with something that was not suspicion and was not friendship either. “You are the man with the frame in a yard. I am the one who talked to you in a street. Come round the back, and do not say one word on this side, because Mr Trant has been kind to me and he is not a fool.”

Elias Nabb was in a room behind the hall with a press against one wall and a table and a lamp, and he had a coat still on and a pen behind his ear he had not taken out, and he said, without sitting down:

“Bryn has told me you are not from the district and hold no office and have no standing in any ward in this kingdom, and I have not asked to see anything, because I have been in this building eleven years and I know what a man in a hurry wants and I would rather not be handed anything.”

“You are right on all three and I have brought nothing to hand you and there is nothing in my coat you would want.”

“Then say what you came to say, and say it standing up, and I will write it down if it is a question and I will not if it is not.”

It was a question. Tomas Vale had it in the fourth column of his own page where it has been since the fortieth, in the words a woman gave him on a road in the rain, and he said it exactly:

“**What is the document.**”

And Elias Nabb got a sheet out of a drawer, and asked him the hour, and wrote for about six minutes, and squared the sheet on the table, and turned it round, and pushed it across.

> *The rolls and the schedules in this hall are records made by this office of answers given to questions put to households by this office. A household is asked a question. The office obtains an answer. The office enters the answer in the answering column against the line, in the order the answers are given. The line is answered. The schedule goes on. That is the whole of what the record is and it is not anything else.*
>
> *And I am going to say the second half of it, because you have not asked me the second half and I have decided to say it whether you ask for it or not, and I would like that on the sheet.*
>
> *Where a household is returned as unable to make up its mind, this office does not leave the line open. This office records the hand of one member present and treats the line as answered. That practice is a settlement of a difficulty, and the difficulty is a household, and I did not write that and I did not invent it and I have read it about nine times and **I think it is right.** I have been four years in the answering column and I fill it, and I fill the mark in it, and I do not have to read that sentence to do the work and I read it anyway.*
>
> *Signed, Elias Nabb, clerk, this hall, this town, this date, about the eighth hour of the evening, on a sheet of this office's own paper, at the request of a person, without standing, on a question put in his own words. Nothing in this building is to be altered because of this sheet.*
>
> *And one thing at the bottom, which is mine and not the office's: **I have never been asked what the mark means. In four years. Not once, by anybody, and not by an officer and not by a person.** I have filled it in about nine hundred times a year and I have assumed it means what it says, because it says it, and because I have never had any reason to think otherwise, and I am not sure, and I have not asked, and I am not going to ask, and I have written that down here so that a man cannot say afterwards that nobody ever noticed.*

Tomas Vale read it twice and did not touch it and put it in the day-book flat, between a sheet in an auditor's hand and a sheet in a deputy's hand, and the three of them lay on the table together, and he looked at them for a long time.

“You have written down that you are not sure,” he said.

“Yes.”

“Why.”

“**Because a clerk who says what a column ought to be is a clerk who has put his name to a practice.** And the only thing in that hall that can be used against me is my name in a book. Not the column. The column is the office's. There is one child in this house and there is a house and there are about eleven years. **And if I am wrong, then four years of my hand is wrong, and that is a thing a man does not find out about himself at a counter. He finds it out about somebody else and by then it is a thing other people do with him.**”

He had said that in under a minute and it was the whole of his trade and he was not defending it. He was reporting it.

“That is fair,” said Tomas Vale.

“It is not fair. It is the truth and it is about four parts cowardice to one part arithmetic and I have never been able to get the proportions better than that and I have had about eleven years in this building.”

“Can I ask you the other half.”

“No,” said Elias Nabb. “**You can ask me and I will not answer and I will not be offended, and I am telling you in advance so that you do not stand in this room for a quarter of an hour waiting for it. What the mark ought to be is not a question a clerk can be asked. You may ask a man what a thing is. You may not ask a man what a thing is for, because what a thing is for is a decision and a decision is the district's and I am a clerk and I hold no office, and I have told you the hour I put at the bottom of that sheet and I have not put my office on it, because there is no office.**”

“All right.”

“I am going to sit down now,” said Elias Nabb, “and then I am going to say the last thing and then you are going to leave, and it is going to be a thing about you and not about me, and I have thought about whether to say it for about a minute and a half and I am going to say it.”

---

“**Has anybody ever asked you that before.**”

And Elias Nabb said, “No.”

And Tomas Vale said, “Not in four years.”

“Not in four years. Not in eleven years in this building, which is longer than the four. **Nobody has ever asked me a question in this room.** Bryn has asked me what the paper is for, which is a question a young man asks his own trade about on his first year, and I have answered it and it was a good question and it is not what you asked. You are the first person to come in here and ask me what a thing means, and you have been in this town two days, and this morning you asked the man at the front door which clerk goes out on the round, and it is the first time in four years that anybody has asked me one of those either.”

He put the sheet he had written on top of the other sheets and squared it again.

“Now I am going to say the thing and I want it said once. **You are the only person in this building who has asked a clerk a question.** There are nine clerks here and about nine hundred lines going into that hall from this town this year and there is not one of us who has ever been asked, and that is not because we are forbidden. **Anybody may ask. That is the whole of the arrangement and it is on the wall in the hall and it has been on the wall since before I came.** And in four years not one person has used it on any of us, and I have not told anybody that this morning and I am not going to be the one who tells anybody.

“**So either you have found the one thing this trade has, or you are a man talking to himself in a room and there is no difference between the two and neither of us will know which for about four years.** I do not know which it is. I have four years of a hand and no answer and a sheet of paper with a stranger's question at the bottom of it, and the sheet is in your book and not in mine, and that was your doing and not mine, and I am not going to pretend I did not notice it.”

He got up and put his coat on properly.

“**I will not be asked again before the round goes out again.** I have told you the reason: the round is out again on the sixty-third, and there is a child and a house and about eleven years, and a man stood in a street for five hours and then asked me one question in his own name, and one is the number I can stand. Ask me on the sixty-fourth and I will tell you the same things in the same words, because the same things are true on the sixty-fourth and I am not a man who improves a sentence between a day and a day.”

---

He walked back to the yard behind the draper's shop at about the ninth hour of the evening with the frame bolted in the corner where it had stood since about the sixth hour of the morning of the fifty-eighth, and it was still bolted, and nothing had been rung, and the second clause had held for a second day.

And then he sat down on the step of the draper's with the day-book on his knee and wrote for about forty minutes, in four columns, in the flat hand, and the third column is the longest thing in it and the fourth column is where he is allowed to be wrong, and both of those are on the page and neither was improved.

> *The sixtieth. I have a second written answer in a stranger's own name with the hour at the foot of it, and it is on this office's own paper, and the clerk said out loud that nothing in his building is to be altered because of it, and **I have now got the same sentence in three hands in three counties and it is the same sentence every time, and I have carried it for a month and used it at four counters and it has never once been the wrong sentence in a building. It has only ever been the wrong sentence in a room with a man in it. I do not yet know what that means and I am not going to write down a guess.**
>
> *And I stood in a street for five hours today outside a building I am not allowed to go into, and I want both halves of that written down and they are not the same shape of fact.*
>
> *The first half is the method, and it works: a man will not ask me a question, and a man will answer a question about the work, and the difference between the two took me nine seconds to find and four years for a clerk in there never to have had it looked for. I asked a man which clerk goes out on the round. That is all. **Nine seconds, and it opened a door that is not a door, and a man in a counter told me the hole was there and told me it had been there for twenty-two years and had never been thought about, and told me that about four men in this kingdom have put a boot through it.**
>
> *And the second half is this, and it took five hours of standing and it is the loneliest afternoon of the five weeks and I am not going to dress it up as anything else.*
>
> ***I am the only person in that building who has ever been asked a question.*** Not the only person who could have. The only person who has. Nine clerks and about nine hundred lines a year and a notice on the wall since before a man I have never met came into it, and not one of us has used it. A young man of about four years asked his own trade what the paper was for, which is a different question and a good one, and that is the closest thing in the building to what I did today.*
>
> *I have been told twice now in a week by two careful men that I am the only person asking. The first of them said it with about four words and a number and did not look pleased. The second of them said it standing up with his coat on and told me it was either the one thing this trade has or a man talking to himself, and **he was right to say both halves and he was right to say he could not tell which, and the worst of it is that I cannot tell either, and I have been in five weeks of roads being the only person asking about a column in every single one of them and I have started to think that being the only person asking is not a fact about a town and is a fact about me.***
>
> *And the thing at the bottom of his sheet, in his own hand, about a mark he has put in about nine hundred times a year for four years and has never been asked about: **he is not sure, and he has not asked, and he is not going to ask, and the reason he is not going to ask is that if he is wrong then four years of his hand is wrong.** And I want that in this book in the plainest words available, because I have been at this for twenty-eight days trying to get at a hand and I have had it in front of me twice — a form-writer above a sluice and a clerk in a hall — and both of them told me in their own mouths that nobody had ever asked them what they were writing, and neither of them was lying, and **I have spent five weeks believing that the people who write the things must know what the things are, and there is a man in this town who fills in nine hundred marks a year and cannot tell you what a mark is.***
>
> *That is not a discovery about this town. It is a discovery about myself and it arrived on the sixtieth of the month at about the eighth hour of the evening in a room behind a hall, and it is the first thing in this book I have wanted to hide from a reader of it and I am not going to.*
>
> *He is not a villain and he is not sorry and he is not a coward and he is not brave. **He is a man with one child and a house and about eleven years and a hand that nobody has ever asked the meaning of, and he put it on a sheet for me in a room at the end of a working day and then told me he would not be asked again this week, and I sat in a street for five hours to earn one question from a man I had no right to stop.**
