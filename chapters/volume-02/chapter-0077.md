# Chapter 0077 — What A Name On A Roll Is For

She had a way of asking that she had built over eleven years and had never once been able to explain to anybody who did not do it, and she could not put it in a question, and so on the sixty-third she put it in a street.

**A name on a roll is a mouth that has not been opened yet.**

She said that to nobody, at about the fourth hour of the afternoon, standing at the end of a lane in a town of two thousand people with a slate under her arm and a stub of chalk in the pocket of it, and she said it once and then she went and asked a person.

She had about three hours and she asked nine people and got eight answers and none of them was a fact, and the ninth was at about the fifth hour of the evening in a front room off a street behind the market, and it took about two hours and it is the only account of what a name on a roll is for that any person has ever given her, and it was given to her by a man who was glad to be asked.

---

His name was Reuben Skell and he had a name on a line and was in no difficulty about it whatever.

That was the first thing, and it was about two minutes in, and it was the thing that took the rest of the afternoon off her.

He offered her the front room and a chair and the back of the fire and he did not sit down for the first ten minutes and then he sat down and stayed down for two hours, and he was not a man being helpful and he was not a man being brave. **He was a man who had never once in four years been asked this and had a great deal to say about it and had been keeping it.**

“You are the woman with the board,” he said. “I have been in the square twice and I have seen it. I did not think anybody was going to come.”

“I am going to ask you three questions and they are the same three I ask everybody and I am not going to change a word of them, and I am telling you first that any answer goes on a thing I write on with the hour against it and anybody may read it, and you may shut the door on all three.”

“You are not going to shut me. Go on. And if you put the price at the end instead of the beginning I shall think less of you and not more.”

**First.** *Has any person ever put their name to a paper for this house — a Crown form, or a return, or a certificate — without anybody asking them to?*

“**No,**” said Reuben Skell, “and that is the thing everybody gets wrong, including you, and I can tell you are about to say *well, whose hand is on it* — no. My hand is on it. I put it there. I have put it there four years. The clerk asked at the door for one of us and I said yes and I wrote it out in front of him and it is my hand and it is my name and I did not have to be asked twice and nobody in this building has ever made me feel a fool about it.”

“Second question, then. *Is the person who put their name there living in this house now.*”

“**I am.** That is what makes it a duty and not a piece of paper, and I will come to that, and I am going to come to it properly, because you are the first person who has ever asked me a question about it and I have had four years to get it in the right order.”

He put his hands on his knees and took about a second, and Tamsin Rook, who reads shoulders rather than mouths and had not needed to in this room for an hour, had nothing to do but listen for two hours, which is what she came here for.

---

“**A name on a roll is a duty.** Not a claim. Not a debt. Not a thing anybody has done to us. A duty. There is a line with our name on it and the line comes round, and the line has to be answered, and the answer is us, and it is not a hardship, it is a chore like any other, and the reason I can say that is that my father had the line before I did and **he did not go.**

“He did not go for four years. He was named and the round came and he said no, the same as my wife has said no twice about the front pitch, and nothing happened. Nothing at all. Nobody came. Nobody wrote. Nobody sent anybody. I was in his kitchen the whole of that winter and I remember the whole of it because I was angry about it for about a month, waiting for something, and nothing came, and eventually I asked him why he was so sure nothing was going to come, and he said: *because a thing that is not going to cost anybody anything does not have anybody in it, and the day it costs somebody something is the day I will answer it, and I will answer it to whoever has to be told.*”

He stopped there and it was a real stop and not a dramatic one, and she waited.

“And he was right about one of those two things,” Reuben Skell said. “He was right that nothing came. He was not right about the rest of it and it took me thirty years to work out that he was not right and I have got about four years left to be wrong in.

“**A name on a roll is a duty for about four years and then it is a mouth. I do not mean that as a figure of speech and I do not mean it as a complaint. I mean that the first year or two, it is a chore, and after that it is something you are, and there is no office in this kingdom that ever gave me the second one and I took it out of the room like a man carrying a parcel he was not addressed for.**

“Nine hundred houses in this town have a name on a line. I have asked about eleven of them and every one of them is like this house, and I will put my hand to that, and about four counties behind this one it is the same, and it is a coat and a hat, and my father told me to hold on to it and I have held on to it four years and so would you.”

---

And then Tamsin Rook said the thing that got people hurt and she did it on purpose, and she has written that down and she has not improved it.

“**Then a name on a roll is a mouth.** And a mouth is for a claim. And if a name is a mouth, then a name is the one thing in this kingdom that can be asked a question and can refuse — and that is the whole of my trade and I have done it for eleven years and I am the best reader of documents in four counties, and I have spent a month looking for a boy of eight who was taken six days before any of this started and his only papers are a line on a schedule and a piece of a household paper with his line cut out of it.”

Reuben Skell put his head slightly to one side, the way a man does when he is being fair about somebody who is about to be wrong.

“Yes,” he said. “Go on.”

“**A name is a mouth. A mark is a mouth with nothing behind it.** And I have been looking for the mark and not the mouth, and if a name is a mouth then a name can be asked, and if it can be asked it can be opened, and if it can be opened — ”

“**No,**” said Reuben Skell, and he said it kindly, and he said it the way a man says *mind the step, it is the step and not the door*, which is the exact sentence a man in a room over a saddler's shop in a town four miles back said to her on the fifty-first, and she heard it and did not like it.

“No,” he said. “You are about four sentences from the worst thing you could do in this town and you are going to do it because you are good at this and you have not slept enough and there is a child in it.

“**A name is a mouth. That is what I have just told you and I stand by every word of it. And a mouth is not an opening. A mouth is a mouth. It is a thing that is on the outside of a house and it faces out, and what it says is whatever the person in the house is willing to have it say, and it says it over and over, for years, to whoever reads the page, and the person in the house never once has to open anything.** You have spent eleven years going to doors and getting people to say a thing out loud to you in a room with a person in it, and that is a real thing and it is worth something and it has never once made a person safe, and **it has made about nine hundred people in this town careful, and careful is what you are looking at when you come into a front room like this.**

“A boy of eight. I have four children and none of them is eight and I am not going to ask you which one or tell you a thing about mine. What I am going to tell you is that if I had a boy of eight taken out of this house I would want a woman at my door the same as anybody, and I would also want it written down, and I would not want it written in a line that everybody in the county can read, and I would not want it in a book with a stamp, and **I would not want anybody to be able to come to my door in nine years with the page and say *this is what you said about your son.* You have been trying to build a thing that is safe and you have built a thing that is permanent, and those are not the same, and you know they are not the same, because you were at that stall in Bramden telling a square about a mouth that belongs to nobody.**”

---

And then he said the thing that beat her, and he said it as a man talking about his own house and not at all as a man talking about a form.

“I am going to tell you what this house is and then you can decide what to do with it, and I am telling you because you did not ask and I have been waiting four years for somebody to be in this room who was not going to ask.

“**A child of ours died of a fever in the spring of the year before.** That is all I am going to say about it and I am not going to say more and it is not for a slate. He was ill nine days and there was a doctor at Helling and the road was out and then the road was in and then the road was not, and there was a cart from the district in this street on the second of the days with about nine other carts in it, and a man I did not know came to the door and said a district cart goes to the low end at about the fourth hour and would carry anybody that wanted to go, and **nobody in this house wanted to go, because nobody in this house had wanted to go for a week, and the road was in.**

“And the cart went, and it took two of the men from the row, and it came back about the ninth hour. **And I have thought about that cart every day since and here is what I have thought: that cart was not for us. That cart was for houses that had not answered. It was in this street because our street is a street with houses in it that have not answered, and we were in the way of it and I am not a fool, I know that, and I am not going to stand in a room and say that a cart killed my child, because the fever would have gone to the low end or it would not have gone anywhere.**

“**What I have thought, every day, is that the district came to my door and offered me a cart, and that I was owed a doctor, and that nobody has ever said one word to me about which of those two things I am entitled to be angry about.** And I have been angry for a year and I have got it in the right place, and it has taken me a year, and I have had a person asking me a question about my own handwriting for about a minute, and I have not once been asked anything about the cart.

“So when you come in here and ask me what a name on a roll is *for*, in your own words, at my own table, I am going to tell you the whole of it, and then I am going to tell you that **you are asking me as though the round is the worst thing that has ever happened to this house, and you are standing in a room where a district came to the door with a cart and I have been owed something for a year and nobody in this kingdom has asked me what it is.**”

He sat back.

“That is the whole of what I have,” he said. “There is not a fourth question. I have had it in a drawer for a year not being said to anybody and it is out of the drawer now and it is about a cart, and you are a stranger with a board, and I would like you to sit down for a minute before you write it.”

---

**And Tamsin Rook had no answer to it.**

That is the whole of the sixty-third and she put it in her own book in her own hand at about the seventh hour of the evening, in a lane, on a step, with the slate on her knee, and she did not improve it and she has not improved it since, and it is eleven words and they are the only eleven words in this whole month that she has not been able to argue with.

> *I went to ask a man what a name is for. He told me. I have no answer. I have been wrong twice in one afternoon and both times in the same direction.*

And then, because the third question had not been asked and because it was the only part of the method she still had, she asked it, and it is the twelfth time.

“**Do you want that written down, and may I write your no as a no and not as nothing.**”

And Reuben Skell said, “**Yes. And put the cart in it. I do not care about the line, put the line in and put the cart in, and if there is room for one of them and not the other then put the cart in.**”

“It is a slate and there is room for both and I am not going to choose.”

She wrote it in three columns and ruled them apart and did not merge them, and she read all three back, one at a time, and he heard his own words and nobody else's.

He changed one of them. He had said *nobody in this kingdom has asked me what it is*, and when she read it back he said, “Take the kingdom out. **Take the kingdom out of it. A kingdom is a word a man uses when he wants a thing to be bigger than the room it is in, and this happened in a street with nine carts in it.**” And she took it out, and she wrote the hour against it, and it is a slate bought for a penny and a half and it is on a table in a front room in a town of two thousand people and it is the second name in this month that she has written down on purpose.

And she told him the price before she left, because she had promised a number and a market and she was not going to break it in a front room.

“**Fourpence,**” she said. “It was fourpence eight days ago in a market a day up the water. And I have not got fourpence’s worth out of this room and I am not going to pretend I have.”

“What would it have been worth.”

“Two days ago it was fourpence and it had doubled and I could not tell you why, and I have not worked it out and I am not going to work it out in a front room at about the seventh hour of the evening with a man who has just been owed a doctor for a year.” She put the slate under her arm. “**You have got nine hundred households in this town and about eleven thousand in the district behind it, and about a name on a line each, and a name on a line is a mouth, and a mouth is for a claim, and I have spent eleven years refusing to be one and every person in this town has become one without anybody asking them, and that is not my discovery and it is not a fact and it is the best thing I have got, and I have not got a single thing I can do with it.**”

Then she went out into the street and down it about nine feet and stopped, because there was a cart standing in it.

There was a cart standing in that street because the round was out again that day, and it was standing at the end of a row of about nine carts, and two men were lifting a crate off it, and Tamsin Rook stood in the road and looked at about nine of them and could not say, standing in the middle of a town of about two thousand people, with a slate and a book and eleven years, **whether she had just been given the trade by a man who does it, or had been told, politely, by a man who is owed something, that she has been doing the wrong thing for a month and should stop.**
