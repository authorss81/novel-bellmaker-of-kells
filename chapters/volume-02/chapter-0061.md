# Chapter 0061 — The Boat

The boat was lying at the stage with the frame already in it when Tomas Vale came down at about the sixth hour on the forty-fifth, and the water was making, and Abel Rundle was standing on the boards with his hands behind him looking at it.

It had been making since before light. That was the whole of the news and he gave it to Tomas Vale in nine words at the head of the stage.

“Going nowhere today,” Abel Rundle said.

---

He had been on a river all his life and he had a way of saying a thing that made a man's next question unnecessary, and he used it twice before Tomas Vale had got his coat open.

The tide on this reach was not a clock. It was a clock about half the time and a argument the other half, and what decided it was not the moon and not the calendar and not a man with a rule about it. It was how much water had come down in the night, and there had been a great deal, and a reach that has had a great deal in the night does not go down for two days afterward. It lies there and it swells and it stands up in the morning with a sort of sulk on it and the current under the stage is going the wrong way for everything anybody wants.

“You said the forty-fifth at the top of the light,” Tomas Vale said.

“I said the tide turns at about half past four,” said Abel Rundle, “and it does, and I said the boat goes on the tide, and it does. And the tide that turns at about half past four is not going to be the tide that carries four days of water out of this reach, and I have been wrong about a tide twice in forty years and both times it cost me a day and a half of pay, and I am telling you now so that the day and a half is not a surprise this afternoon.”

Tomas Vale stood on the boards with the barrow behind him and did not say the seven things he had ready, because there were not seven, there were two, and the first was that he had been told the date and had written it down as a date that was not his, and the second was that the water had just taken it off him.

He wrote the hour in his book instead, and wrote under it: *The tide turns at about half past four and the tide is not going. Fifth day, about the sixth hour. The date I was given was the forty-fifth at the top of the light and I wrote it down as not mine and it turns out the tide had a different opinion and the tide was not wrong and I was.*

---

The frame had been on the boards since about the fourth hour of the afternoon on the forty-fourth, and it had not been touched since, and the four bolts had been made fast on the boards at about the seventh hour on the evening of the forty-fourth and had not been looked at since by anybody, and the barrow was on the barge with the wheels chocked and a stone under the near wheel that Tamsin Rook had found herself.

He went and looked at all of it anyway, because he was a man who looked, and because on the forty-fourth he had looked at it eleven times between the fourth hour of the afternoon and the seventh hour of the evening, and on the forty-fifth he looked at it four more times before the eighth hour and wrote none of them down.

Then he did the thing he had come down to do, which was to get a locked frame in a cradle off a landing stage and onto a boat without breaking a receipt he had written himself at about the fifth hour of the morning of the thirty-third in a watch house in a town he had not been back to.

It was already on the boat.

And that was the first problem, and it was a small one, and it was the only easy thing that happened to him in two days.

---

The second clause of the receipt says: *that the frame stays in its reinforced padded cradle for the carriage.*

Tomas Vale had read that clause eleven times in eleven days and had not once had a question about it, and he had a question about it at about the seventh hour of the forty-fifth with his hand on the edge of the cradle and a ferry-man watching him from the boards.

“A carriage is a coach, or a cart, or a barrow,” he said. “A thing with wheels that a person takes a place in and does not steer. That is what the word means. And a boat is not a carriage, and there is no wheel on this, and I have been saying *the boat is not a carriage* to myself for two days as though that settled something.”

Abel Rundle did not say anything for a moment.

Then he came one step up the boards and put his hand on the near frame of the cradle, and did not touch the bell, and did not touch the frame, and put his hand on the cradle, which is not the object.

“Say the rest of it,” he said.

“The clause binds the carriage. A boat is not a carriage. Therefore on a boat the frame may stand on the boards with the cradle under it, and the bolts made fast, and that is what the clause permits, because the clause has nothing to say about a boat.”

“It has plenty to say about a boat,” said Abel Rundle. “It is going to say it in about nine seconds and then I am going to be late, and I am going to say it anyway.”

He took his hand off the cradle and turned it over, palm up, the way a man does when he is about to count on his own hand and is annoyed about it.

“A carriage goes where you take it,” he said. “Water goes where it likes. That is the whole of it, and it is the whole of it because I have got a man in a barge who is going to be sick, and he is sick at the third hour of the ebb, and on a good ebb he is sick at the third hour, and on a bad day he is sick at the first hour. And I have got about ninety feet of barge and no rail on the off side, and I am not going to be the man who put a Crown object on ninety feet of open boards at the third hour because he read the word *carriage* and decided a river was not a coach.”

He put his hand down.

“Your first clause says the bolts are made fast at every place the frame is set down. A boat deck is a place the frame is set down. You make the bolts fast. And then the boat works. Water works a boat in three directions and not one, and a bolt is not a hand, and a bell in a cradle with a bolt through it is a bell in a cradle with a bolt through it, and a bell on ninety feet of boards with a bolt through it is a bell in the water at about the third hour, and then it is a bell on the bottom, and then somebody on this reach has got a Crown object in a net for nine years and no receipt at all.”

---

Tomas Vale had his hand on the edge of the cradle and he took it off it.

“You are right,” he said.

“I am right about water,” said Abel Rundle. “I am not right about paper and I have not read your paper and I am not going to.”

“May I ask you to enter it.”

“Ask Hanna.”

“I am asking you to say it again in about nine words so that Hanna Grieve can put it in the book, and I am asking her to put it in the book, and I am asking that neither of you improves mine.”

Abel Rundle looked at him for slightly longer than a man needs to.

“There is a thing about that,” he said, “and I will come to it. Get her.”

---

Hanna Grieve came out of the lean-to with her sleeves turned up because it was the eighth hour and she had been doing something to a net, and she has kept a tally at the head of this stage for about thirty years and the book is a ruled book with four columns and it is the day, the thing, whose, and the hour, and it is kept with a stub of pencil and not a pen, and it is the same shape as a yard book at an inn and it is on a bench under the lean-to about nine feet from the water.

She came and stood at the end of the bench and did not sit down.

“Enter it,” Tomas Vale said, “in your own hand and in your own columns, and read it back to me, and I will copy it into my book exactly and neither of us will improve it.”

“Whose account is it.”

“Yours of what I said, and Abel Rundle’s of what he said, and mine of what I have just been told, and they are three accounts and they do not agree and I want all three of them in one line of your book with the hour on it, because I have been wrong twice in two days about things that are not about a river and I would like there to be one page in this county where a thing is entered by the man who was wrong and by the man who was right and by the man who keeps the book, and all three in the same hand of pencil.”

Hanna Grieve wrote it. It took her a little over two minutes and she wrote it in the column for *the thing* and she did not use the column for *whose* and she was not asked to.

Then she read it back.

> *Forty-fifth day, at about the seventh hour. The holder of a frame in a cradle on this boat asked the ferry-man whether the word carriage in the second clause of a custody receipt of the thirty-third includes a boat. The ferry-man said no. The holder said that therefore the frame might stand on the boards with the cradle beneath it. The ferry-man said no again, and gave the ground that a carriage goes where it is taken and water goes where it likes, and that the water works a boat in three directions and not one, and that a bolt is not a hand, and that the frame stays in its cradle. Entered at the request of the holder, in the hand of the keeper of this tally, at about the seventh hour of the forty-fifth. The holder of the frame says he was wrong twice in two days and does not say about what.*

Tomas Vale copied it into his book word for word, including *does not say about what*, and read it back to her, and she said it was right, and he thanked her, and she said:

“I will thank you when you have been on the water four days and told me nothing has happened.”

---

Then she went back into the lean-to, and Abel Rundle got his rope, and it was at about the eighth hour and the stage was empty except for the three of them and a man coiling a net on the boards at the head.

And Tomas Vale did the thing he had come down to do, which was to tell a man who was not going to be asked anything what he was carrying, without lying about it.

He did it in a way he had not planned and did not afterwards improve, and the words were these and they are in his book in his own hand with the hour against them.

“Abel. I am going to tell you what is in the frame and I am not going to tell you the whole of it, and I want to say both halves of that out loud before I start, because if I do not you will hear a half of it later and think I hid the other half, and I would rather you had the whole shape of what I am doing now than a complete lie.”

“All right,” said Abel Rundle.

“It is Crown property,” said Tomas Vale. “It is a survey carrier, and it is stripped, and it has no clapper in it and there is not going to be one. And I am carrying it under a receipt, and the receipt says that the object may be produced at the request of the district, or the watch, or the market court, and that **a request may be made by any person and not only by an officer.** And you are a person and not only an officer. And so you may ask for it now, or in four days, or when we have landed, and I will produce it in its cradle, in daylight, wherever you like, and I would rather you asked now than in four days, and I am saying that because I would rather it were done and over and I am not going to pretend that is generosity.”

“Why would you rather.”

“Because a request that has not been made is a thing that is still owed, and a thing that is still owed is a thing that can be made at about the second hour in the morning by a man in a bad mood, and I would like it made now, in the light, in front of a woman with a tally book, and entered.”

Abel Rundle stood on the boards and looked at him, and then went and looked at the frame for about as long as it takes to walk round a thing twice, and did not go round the far side of it.

“No,” he said.

“That is a complete answer and I am not going to argue with it.”

“It is not an answer,” said Abel Rundle. “If it was an answer I would have written it in the book and I have not got a pencil. I have not asked you for the thing and I am not going to. Now sit down, because I am going to say the one thing I have been keeping for two days, and I have been keeping it since about the seventh hour yesterday, and I have thought about it all night and I have decided to say it.”

“All right.”

---

**And this is the thing Tomas Vale wrote down, and he wrote it down at about the seventh hour of the evening of the forty-fifth at the head of the stage on the boards with the tally book shut in the lean-to nine feet off, and it is in his book in his own flat hand and it is the first thing in a month he has written about himself that he has not improved in the morning:**

*Abel Rundle, ferry-man, this stage, about the eighth hour of the forty-fifth. Asked for nothing. Said no to asking, which is not an answer and is not in the book. Booked the frame's place anyway, which I did not expect, and said:*

*“Your receipt says anybody may ask for that. It does not say anybody may ask for it twice, and it does not say I have to forget it, and I am telling you now that I am not going to forget it, and I am not going to ask you for it, and those are two different things and one of them is free and the other one is not, and you have written a piece of paper that costs a man nothing to use and it costs me something every hour that I have got it, and I would like that written down somewhere, because you have come down here two days with the idea that you are a man holding a door shut, and you are not holding anything shut, you have hung a hook in the open air on a reach that four boats a week go past, and any one of them may pick it up at any hour, and you will not know which one, and that is not a fault in your paper, it is what you asked for.”*

*And then he said: “And I will tell you the other half, because I am not going to be a man who says a hard thing and stops. In about nine years something will come up this reach with a bell in a cradle and a piece of paper, and the man carrying it will be a decent man who thinks the receipt is a kind of passport, and the woman behind him will be the one who reads the fifth clause out loud, and that is how it will be done, and it will be done to somebody who did nothing, and I will be on a boat at the time and I will hear about it in a week.”*

*And I asked him what he meant and he said he meant that I have handed a thing to a stranger and told him it was his right, and a right with no cost on it and no limit on it is a thing that gets used, and that is not an argument against me and he was not making one, and I have no answer to it and I did not try to make one in front of him because he would have known.*

*And here is what I got wrong for two days and wrote down wrongly in a day-book at about the sixth hour, and the man who has been on a river for forty years had it in nine words at about the seventh: a carriage goes where you take it and water goes where it likes. I have been carrying a wheel and a barrow and a lane and a yard and a market court in my head for eleven days, and every one of those has a place a man can be got at, and a river has no place. There is no lane to go round on a river. There is a boat. There is four days of it with a barrow and a frame and two of us in the same line of sight in front of everybody on the whole reach, and the fifth clause in my coat is the only thing I have got, and it does not stop anybody asking. It stops them doing. Those are not the same and I have had them the wrong way round for eleven days and a man on a landing stage has fixed it with nine words about water.*

*And the other thing on the same page, and I put it here because it is the finding of the whole day and it is not about a bell:*

*The district withdrew a finding on the thirty-third and nobody has asked for the withdrawal. Not one person in four counties knows it happened. And a boat is a place where a man who is not going to be asked anything at all says what he is carrying, out loud, to a stranger, because a man who has been silent for two days cannot stay silent for four, and because being silent on a boat is a different act from being silent in a lane, and I did not know that until about the eighth hour this morning and I know it now.*

*He has not asked. He said the word* not going to *twice and he wrote the boat's place in the book anyway, at about the seventh hour, and the frame's place, and a line for the barrow, and he wrote the fare, which is the fare, and he would not talk it down, and I have paid it.*

---

Tamsin Rook had been in the lean-to with Hanna Grieve for about an hour and had come out with the slate and a stub of chalk and had sat down on the bench with her back to the water and had not asked him a single question about the ferry-man, which was hers to give and she gave it, and what she gave it was this, and she said it before he had got to the end of it:

“You told him on purpose and you offered him the right and you did not offer him the whole of it, and both halves of that were yours and neither of them was mine, and I am not going to come at you for either of them, and I am going to say the one thing that was mine to say, and it is this. **On a river, the road is not a road. There is no shoulder. There is no door you can go round. And I have been carrying a bag with a bell in it for eleven days to get to a place where I can ask a question of a building, and I have just watched you ask a question of a man at a landing stage and get the same answer you would get in a lane, and that is because he is a man and not a building, and a man on a river has nowhere else to be.**”

She stopped. He waited for the end of it and there was not one.

“Take that out,” Tomas Vale said.

“No,” said Tamsin Rook. “I said a day of the month and it was a weekday and it is not a date, and there are four people on that stage and one of them is a woman with a pencil who writes down the day and the thing and whose and the hour, and I have said it out loud in front of her, and I am not going to take it back, and you may enter it in your book as the ninth of the month and I will not correct you, and that is the whole of the leash and it has cost me one sentence and I have decided it is worth one sentence.”

He wrote it down. She watched him write it, over his shoulder, and did not say anything, which she has done four times now and which is the highest thing anybody has been given in this batch.

---

The water stood up in the stage all through the second hour of the afternoon and through the seventh hour of the evening and it did not go down, and Abel Rundle went home at about the eighth hour of the evening and came back at about the fifth hour of the morning of the forty-sixth and looked at it and said two words that were the whole of a professional opinion.

“Still up,” he said.

---

And then, at about the fourth hour of the afternoon of the forty-sixth, the water went the other way.

It went the other way all at once and fast, the way a reach does when there is nothing left to come down and a spring tide underneath it, and Tomas Vale was on the boards with his book out when Abel Rundle put the boat's nose up against the landing stage at Ocklebridge and held her there with a pole and got a line over the post, and the whole thing had taken two hours and had gone the wrong way up the water for thirteen miles.

“That is the highest this has been this year,” Abel Rundle said. “It will not do it twice this month and I have told you so in advance so that you cannot say afterwards that I did not tell you.”

The stage at Ocklebridge is a stone stage with a slope of worn stone down to the water, and there are about two hundred persons in the village, and the water bell is on a post at the head of the water above the stage, and there is a post and a small iron headstock and a rope on it, and a woman was standing at the bottom of the lane with a cloth in her hand and the cloth was wet.

She had been doing what she does every morning and the hour was about half past five.

Tomas Vale got his barrow's prop out of his hand and stood at the bottom of the stage and did not go up the lane, and had said to himself on the water on the way up that he would not go up the lane, and had known before he got here that he would not.

The flood would begin to turn at about the seventh hour. He had about an hour and a half.

---

She came down to the bottom of the stage, and she saw him, and she did not go up.

“Mrs Vell,” Tomas Vale said. “It is the forty-sixth. You said on the thirty-fifth that the first name on that page was not mine and was not going to be, and that I might ask you in nine days and you would tell me then, and that if I asked before nine days you would not tell me, and that the nine days were yours to ask for and you had asked for them.”

“I did.”

“**The nine days were the forty-fourth.** I did not come on the forty-fourth. I did not write on the forty-fourth. I did not come on the forty-fifth. I came yesterday on a tide that was not going and I stood on a stage for a day with a frame in a cradle, and I had a day in which I could have walked up this lane and I did not walk up it, and I want that in a book before you say one more word, because you are entitled to know the whole of what I did with the day you gave me and I did not give you the whole of it in the nine minutes I have got before this water turns.”

Her cloth stopped moving in her hand.

“And the whole of what I am owed is that I say it before you have to,” Tomas Vale said. “Not the name. You do not owe me the name. You have not owed me the name since the thirty-fifth and you do not owe it now and I am not going to ask you for it, and if you never give it to me then the thirty-fifth was a thing a woman did for herself and I was a man who was in the way of it.”

“All right,” said Marta Vell.

“**May I ask you one thing, and you may say no, and a no is a thing I will write down with the hour and will not improve in the morning.**”

“You may ask me.”

“Did the week stand.”

The water came up the slope of the stage and went back off it and came up again.

“No,” said Marta Vell. “It stood until the forty-fourth and it does not stand now, and that is not a thing I have done to you, it is a thing that has happened, and I want you to hear how I am saying it, because you have come down a slip with a flood tide under you and you are going to write this down and I would like the shape of it to be right. **You did not come on the day I named. That is the whole of what you did. Nothing else. I have not withdrawn it, because there is nothing in it to withdraw, and I have not kept it, because there is nothing in it to keep.**”

“Yes,” said Tomas Vale. “May I write that.”

“You may write that.”

He wrote it standing up on the slope with the book against the post, in his own hand, and the hour against it, and he read it back and she made him change one word, and he changed it, and both the word and the correction are in the book.

He read the whole thing out to her then, which he had not planned, and she stood and listened to it with the cloth in her hands, and when he had finished she said:

“That is the whole of what I have from you and it is nine lines and there is not one of them that is about me.”

“No.”

“It is about you,” said Marta Vell. “You have written down the lateness of a man who is owed nothing, on a stage, in a flood, where anybody coming off a boat can read it over your shoulder, and there are four people on that boat, and one of them is a ferry-man who is going to be four days away in a week.”

Tomas Vale looked up. Abel Rundle was standing on the boards with a coil in his hands and had been for about a minute.

“All right,” said Tomas Vale.

“Turn round and look at him,” said Marta Vell. “**Look at him, because I am going to say a name and I am not going to say it to a man in a lane at four in the afternoon with a Crown bell behind him. I have kept that page thirty years and I have never given it to anybody, and I am not going to give it to you, and I am not going to tell you that is the end of it.**”

“Mrs Vell.”

“**I am going to give you a different name.** And I am going to say it out loud and on a stage with a ferry-man in it, and I am going to say why, and the why is that a name you have written in a book is a thing that can be looked at by the next man and by the man after him, and I have not got a book and I have got a bell, and a bell is the only kind of record in this kingdom that a person can be *in* instead of *on*, and I would like one name of mine in the water instead of thirty years of them in a drawer.” She turned and looked at Abel Rundle, who had stopped moving the coil. “And because you are standing there, and because you are a stranger who will be four days gone, and because a thing said in front of a stranger cannot be taken back afterwards by either of us.”

“That is a very good reason,” said Abel Rundle, “and I would rather you had not used it, and I am not going to pretend I did not hear it.”

“There was a man at this water in the spring nine years ago,” said Marta Vell. “He had a satchel and he was very quick and he was not unkind and he asked me to ring four times, and he told me what he wanted repeated, which nobody has ever done to me before or since, and I rang it four times. **Hesk Rann.** That is the name on the second line of that page and I have said it because I have said it and I cannot un-say it, and I do not know why I have said it to you and I have thought about it for two minutes and I do not know.”

Tomas Vale did not write it down.

He stood on the slope of a stage with a book in his hand and a barrow behind him and a ferry-man four feet off and a flood tide making under his feet, and he did not write it down, and Marta Vell watched him not write it down.

“Do not write it,” she said.

“I am not going to write it.”

“You are not going to write it because if you write it, it is in a book, and you are the man who has spent a month putting things in books, and you want it, and I have watched you want things all morning.” She put the cloth over her shoulder. “And when you are next in a room with a person who fills in forms and you have to say a name, you will have to remember that a woman said it out loud on a stage and could not take it back, and that is the whole of what I have given you and it is not a page and it is not in a book and there is no hour against it, and if you want it in a book you will have to go and ask him whether he remembers a woman at a water bell.”

“Yes,” said Tomas Vale.

“I am going to ring now,” said Marta Vell, “and I am going to ring as I ring every morning, which is four times, and that is all, and you will stand there and you will not count them out loud, and you will not say what they were for, and if you do I will stop.”

The bell is small and it is in an iron headstock and there is a rope on it and it is not joined to anything, and she rang it four times, unhurried, and the water under the stage was making the whole time and she waited for the water between each of them the way a person does who has been doing it for thirty years and has never once had to hurry because of a river.

The flood began to turn at about the seventh hour.

---

Abel Rundle got the line off the post at about the eighth hour and they went down the reach in about an hour and a half and the water was falling behind them the whole way and he did not speak for twenty minutes and then he said one thing.

“You did not ask her for it.”

“No.”

“You had about an hour and a half and a water going out and you did not ask her for it, and I have been on this reach forty years and I have watched about nine men go at a thing with a book, and every one of them asked, and you are the first one who went away without, and I have been thinking about it for the last mile and I have not worked out whether that was respect or whether you were frightened.”

“I was frightened,” said Tomas Vale.

“All right,” said Abel Rundle. “That is the more useful one.”

---

The water turned at about the eleventh hour of the evening of the forty-sixth and the ebb began and the boat went out of the stage on it, and the frame stayed in its cradle and the four bolts were made fast on the boards where the frame was set down and the barrow's wheels were chocked and the stone was under the near wheel, and nothing was rung, and the receipt was not broken and the fifth clause was not used on anybody.

And Tomas Vale wrote one line at the head of the stage at about the eleventh hour of the evening of the forty-sixth, standing on the boards with the tally book shut in the lean-to, and Hanna Grieve wrote it in her columns and read it back and did not improve it, and the hour was against it:

*Frame in cradle, cradle on boards, four bolts made fast, barrow chocked, nothing rung, receipt whole, all five clauses. The boat went on the ebb at about the eleventh hour of the evening. I have on this water for one day and a half and there are four days of it in front of me if the water is kind, and Abel Rundle says the water is kind, and a boat puts a barrow and a frame and a man in the same line of sight for every hour of all of it, in front of everybody on the whole reach, and there is no lane to go round and there is no shoulder and there is no door, and the fifth clause in my coat is the only thing I have got, and it does not stop a man asking me for a thing. It stops him doing it. A man on a river is the easiest man in the kingdom to ask, because he has nowhere else to be. I did not know that this morning and I know it now, and I have written it down in a woman's book on a landing stage and in my own and I am not going to improve either.*
