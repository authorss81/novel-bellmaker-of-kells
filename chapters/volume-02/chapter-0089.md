# Chapter 0089 — The Inheritance That Cannot Be Claimed

He found it because of the arithmetic and not because of anything else.

On the morning of the seventy-fifth he was at a gate on the water at the west end of this town, counting loads of timber going into a yard, and there were about nine men on the yard books and a counting house at the back of it with a door standing open because it was about the seventh hour of the morning and a counting house in a yard in a river town is open all day and is not a building anybody thinks about.

And a man came out of that door carrying a book, and stopped, and turned round, and went back in, and shut the door, and stood behind it for about nine seconds, and then came out again and shut it behind him properly, and said, to nobody:

“**Four months.**”

And Tomas Vale, who has a way of standing near a thing like that, stood about eleven feet off with a barrow he could not park anywhere, and the man came down the yard and looked at him and said, “You are not from this yard.”

“No.”

“**Then you will not mind if I do not say the rest of it.**”

---

**The house was Vey and Lock.** Timber and freight off the water, three boats of its own in a season and about nine men on the books and a yard with a slip in it. The senior partner was **Marden Vey**, and the other three were partners, and the counting-house clerk was a man called **Sander Fisk** who had been in that room for about nine years and was pleasant about it in the way of a man who has nothing to be pleasant about and is working anyway.

And the thing that was standing in that counting room, on a table, in a leather bag with a seal on it, had been standing there for about four months.

“**Ivor Salter died in the winter,**” said Sander Fisk, before anybody had asked him anything, “and he left about three hundred pounds, and there are six households in this town with a claim on it, and the total of the six is about fifty-one pounds, and it has been in this room for four months and it is going to be in this room at the end of the year.**

“**And I am going to tell you the whole of it now in about four minutes and then I am going to go back to the book, and I am telling you because you are standing in a yard with a barrow and you have not asked me for anything, and because Mr Vey is forty miles up the water and is coming back this morning on the carrier, and I would rather have said it at a gate to a stranger than have said it to him in a room with a door shut.**”

He said it in the yard, standing at a gate, in about four minutes, and it was the whole of it, and none of it was a complaint.

**A claim has to be shown.** That is the first thing and it is the oldest thing and there is no other way of doing it. Not proved — *shown.* Shown to a district, or shown in a counting house, or shown at a door, by a hand against a line. **A hand against a line is the only record in this kingdom that a person stood in front of somebody and said something, and everything else in this county is a copy of that or a summary of it.**

Ivor Salter had kept a yard-book, as everybody with a yard keeps a book, and in it for thirty-one years he had written down, against a day, the name of any person who stood in his yard and said a thing about their own line and had him put it in. Six households in this town have that hand in that book. Fourteen are dead. **About nine thousand people in four counties have never heard of it, because it is a book in a yard and not a form in a hall, and Salter never thought of it as anything.**

And the money to pay the six was in the bag, and the bag could not be moved, and here is the whole of why, and Fisk said it in the yard without any expression at all:

“**A house may hold money for a dead man only if the house can show its own answering.** That is a rule and it is about four hundred years old or nobody knows how old it is, and it is the same rule as the one that says a man cannot sign for his wife. **You must be able to show a hand of your own before you may be entered in a book as handling a hand of somebody else's. That is the whole of the law of this subject and there is no other part of it.**

“**And this firm has a line in a hall in this town. It has had one for about nine years. It is in the name of the trade, and it is not in the name of a man, and it has not been answered since the spring before last.**”

---

Tomas Vale stood at a gate in a yard and found that he had stopped being surprised, and that this was new, and that it was a thing to be frightened of in a different way from everything else.

“How long has it been open.”

“**Since the spring before last.** Four years, near enough. I have the figure on a slip and I am not going to read you the slip.”

“Why.”

And Sander Fisk said, with a fairness that Tomas Vale found he could not argue with for about nine minutes afterwards:

“**Because nobody has ever asked me why, and because I have looked, twice, and because I am not going to look a third time unless somebody makes me, and I am telling you that as a fact and not as a complaint. A line in a trade's name is not a line in a man's name, and nobody in this firm has ever agreed what the mark means, and the practice in that hall — and I have read the practice, it is printed, it is an approving return from a district office and it is a good paper — says that where a household cannot make up its mind, one hand is put against the line and the line is treated as answered.**

“**There are four of us in this firm and we cannot make up our minds, and so a hand ought to go down about twice a year and for four years not one has gone down, and there is no way to make one go down that I have found, and I am not a partner and it is not my line.**”

“Whose line is it.”

“**The firm's. That is the whole of the difficulty and it has been the whole of the difficulty for four years and you are the first person from outside this yard to ask me a question about it in nine years, and I would like it recorded that you asked me and not Mr Vey, and I would like it recorded more that Mr Vey asked me the same question in the spring and I gave him the same answer and he did not put a hand down either.**”

---

He came back to it at about the second hour of the afternoon in a room with a long table in it, and he asked for her to be in the room first, which is the seventh time in a month that he has put anything on a slate.

He wrote it in the four columns and turned the slate round, and the question was one line, and it was about four counties, and it was the ordinary question.

She read it and she said no, in one word, the way she has said it six times, and then she gave him a reason that was hers and was not a grown, and it took about forty seconds.

“**No.**

“**Because a woman with a slate goes into a timber merchant's counting room in this town, and by the fourth hour of the afternoon there is not one person in Miremarket who does not know it, and by the end of the week the four families that owe that firm money will know it, and they will not be told why. They will be told that there is a woman in it. And a firm that has been in this town four generations does not get a quarter back for a quarter year because a woman was seen going through a door.**

“**I have watched it happen twice. I priced it once, in a smith's shop, and I priced it wrong and it is a penny and a half for a slate and there is no price on a sentence in a lane.**”

He did not ask again. He has not asked again about that and he is not going to, and he wrote in the book that night that she was right for the seventh time in a month and that he is the one who keeps finding this out.

**So he went into a counting room alone, with a barrow he could not park anywhere, and he could not read a hand.**

That is the cost and it is a small one and it is on the page in about four seconds at about the sixth hour. Fisk read him a line out of the firm's book — a hand, a day, a column — and Tomas Vale looked at it for about a minute and a half and could not tell him whose hand it was, and did not say so for another twenty minutes, and when he did say so Fisk said, “**That is all right. That is not a thing a man can do. I have been in this room nine years and I could not tell you whose hand that is either and I have never told anybody and there is a reason and the reason is that if I cannot tell you then nobody can, and if nobody can then it does not matter, and I do not know which of those two is true and I have not gone and found out.**”

He had wanted her in that room for nine minutes and he had not got it, and what he had wanted her in it for was a thing he could not have done anyway, and he wrote both of those down.

---

**Marden Vey had been forty miles up the water since before the round came in and came back on the morning of the seventy-fifth on the carrier, and he was in the counting house from about the first hour of the afternoon until about the ninth hour of the evening**, which is longer than his business is, and the reason is that a man with a barrow had been standing in his yard for a day and a half and he wanted to know what a man with a barrow was.

He was not angry. That is the first thing about him and it is in the yard, in his own words, before anybody has said anything.

“**I am not angry. I have been angry about this for four years and it has cost me four families and a yard and about a third of the carrying, and I have got the anger down to about the size of a bad morning, and I have got it down there by myself, in this yard, at about two in the morning, and I have not had to do it with anybody's help and I would have taken help if it had been offered and it was not.**”

He was owed money by four families. He said the four figures without being asked and they were **about ninety pounds, about forty, about eleven and four, and about six**, and he said which family owed which and how long, and he did it in a way that Tomas Vale understood afterwards had been done about nine thousand times in four counties by men who were owed money.

“**Nobody is going to lend to us.** That is the second thing and I will not dress it. A house that cannot show its own answering is a house that cannot be entered in a book as handling anybody's money, and a man with money in a town of two thousand people will not lend it to us and says why in one sentence and is right in the sentence. **I have tried it four times in four months and I have been told no four times and the reason has been the same four times and it has never once been said unkindly.**”

“Because of the mark in the column.”

“**Because of the mark in the column.** And I want to be exact about the size of it, because I have had a year to be exact about it and nobody has ever asked me to be. **The mark is not what is ruining me. A mark in a column is a mark in a column and there are about eleven thousand of them a year in this county and none of them is a secret and none of them has ever cost anybody a night’s sleep that I know of. What is ruining me is that the line has been open four years, and the line being open is not the mark, it is the four of us not putting a hand down, and I have known that since the spring and I have not done it.**”

“Why not.”

And Marden Vey, who is owed ninety pounds by one household and who is going to lose a yard, said the sentence that is the best sentence anybody says in this town in six weeks, and he said it standing up, in a counting house, in his own name, about his own trade, **and about the practice in the hall, which he has read, and of which he said first that it is a good paper and that it is right, and that he has never in thirty-one years been able to improve it or wanted to.** He said it once and he would not say it a second time in a different room to a different man, and Tomas Vale wrote it down in his own book on a step of a draper's shop at about the ninth hour of the evening, and **writing it down is not the same as having it entered anywhere, and he wrote that under it and it is the only line in that entry that is about him.**

“**Because a claim cannot be shown, and a claim that cannot be shown cannot be paid, and that is not a hardship, and I want it written down that I said that and that nobody is to think I do not mean it.**

“**That rule is the reason Ivor Salter has not been robbed.** He has been dead four months and there are about nine hundred people in this town who could each remember a thing he said to them at a gate, and a good many of them could say it better than the six who have it in his book, because a gate is where people say the true things. **And not one of those nine hundred can be paid, and the reason they cannot is that this firm cannot be entered in a book, and the reason for that is four men who have never once discussed a mark in a column in eleven years.**

“**I am not going to have that in my head for a fortnight while we are open. I have a rule and the rule is that a thing has to be shown. If I bend it for six households today then I have bent it, and the next man to want it will say that a merchant of this town did it for six households and it is only fair, and in about four years there will be nine hundred claims in this county that are all fair.**

“**So the six households wait. And they are owed about fifty-one pounds and they have waited four months and they will wait until the end of the year, and I am not going to tell them I cannot help them, because I can help them, in about nine seconds, by writing something down. And I am not going to. And I would like it noticed that the thing I am refusing to do is a small thing and that I am refusing it on purpose, and that nobody in this room is going to persuade me, and that I am not a villain about it and I am not frightened of it, I am just a man who has worked out what a thing is for.**”

Nobody said anything for a while. A boat came up past the slip.

“And the mark,” said Tomas Vale. “You said it was not the mark.”

“**The mark is a hand against a line and there is no box for a reason on that column and it is four inches wide and it is the best box on the paper and I have read that too. And the mark is not what is ruining me.**

“**The mark was put in a building about forty years ago by a man whose name I do not know, who is dead, and who was doing his job on a morning, and there is nothing anybody could do about it then and there is nothing anybody could do about it now. I have looked for the man. I asked a man in a hall in this town in the spring and he was about four years in the building and he was not there then and he was not there when I asked. That is the whole of the hunt and it took me an afternoon.**”

---

And then Marden Vey said the other thing, and it is the thing that is going to happen, and he said it as though it were a matter of freight.

“**You will want to know what is actually wrong with me and I am going to tell you, because you have not built anything out of it and you have not put a paper in front of me, and about four people have done that in four years and every one of them wanted the same four things and I have given none of them.**

“**I have four partners. Two of them are my nephews and one of them has been in this yard since he was a boy. And every one of the four of us reads that open line as a debt.**

“Not as a chore. **As a debt.** A debt is a thing that is owed to somebody, and the somebody is a hall in this town, and the four of us have looked at that line for four years and every one of us has thought, in his own words, and we have said it to each other at about nine separate times: *that is the firm's, and it is in arrears, and it will want paying for, and it is a thing the firm is in for, and it is not a thing I am in for.*”

He put both hands flat on the table, which is a thing a man does when he is about to say the part he has not said.

“**And there is nobody in this firm to tell any of us that it is the other thing. Because it is not a debt, it is a chore, and a chore is a thing you do for yourself in your own yard on your own day, and every one of the four of us would do it for ourselves in about nine seconds, and not one of the four of us will do it for the firm, because the firm is not us. It never has been. We have been a firm for thirty-one years and we have never once in thirty-one years been able to tell each other that the firm is us.**

“**And that is the whole of my ruin and it is about eleven words long and it is not in a hall and it is not on any paper and there is no column for it and no clerk can enter it, and you cannot certify it, and nobody is going to stop me saying it in about four years when I am not in this yard any more.**”

---

Tomas Vale stood in a counting house in a yard on the water in a town of about two thousand people, at about the sixth hour, and did not build a case, and did not denounce anything, and could not stop any of it, and did not comfort himself by calling it necessary.

He wrote at about the ninth hour of the evening, on the step of the draper's, in the flat hand, with the hours, and he filled all four columns and merged nothing:

> *The seventy-fifth. I asked a woman to be in a counting room for the seventh time in a month and she said no in one word and gave me a reason about a quarter a year, and she was right, and the reason it was a reason and not a preference is that the Veys have four families on a book and a quarter taken off a quarter is about ninety pounds that is already owed and cannot be lost. **I did not ask again. She has now refused me seven times in a month and not one of the seven has cost her anything, and every one of the seven has cost me something, and I have written that down and I am not going to be grateful to her for it because she would not want me to be.***
>
> *Let it be written in the plainest words I have and then I will shut this book.*
>
> ***A name on a roll carries every person who has ever been in the trade. A line is answered by one hand, and the hand belongs to whoever is at the counter. A firm that cannot show that its line was answered cannot be entered in a book as holding anything for anybody else. And therefore a man who has been dead four months has not been robbed and six households are owed about fifty-one pounds and none of them can be paid, and the whole of the reason is that four men in a yard have never once told each other that a firm is them.***
>
> *That is the engine of this business and it is in a counting house and not in a hall. **I have been in this town since the fifty-seventh learning that a name becomes a duty in about four years in a queue, and it turns out that the same thing happens to a firm in a yard with nine men on its books and a rule about forty years old, and that the two are the same shape, and that nobody has to stand in a lane to ring it in either case.***
>
> *And the cost. **About ninety pounds and about forty and about eleven and four and about six, owed to him by four families, and a third of the carrying, and a slip, and four refusals in four months from four men with money in a town of two thousand, all of them right and none of them unkind. About fifty-one pounds owed to six households that have waited four months and are going to wait to the end of the year. And a yard with three boats in it in a season. Those are the figures and there is nobody in them who is wicked and I have looked at all of them.***
>
> *And the sentence. **A claim cannot be shown and a claim that cannot be shown cannot be paid, and it is the only reason a dead man has not been robbed by nine hundred people with a memory, and he will not bend it for six households, and he wants it written down that he refused and that nobody is to persuade him, and he is not a villain and he is not frightened of it.***
>
> *I came into this town wanting a person to be angry at who was not kind to me. I have got a merchant who is owed money, who is right, who is going to be ruined, and who said the best thing anybody has said to me in six weeks on his own trade, and it is true, and I cannot get at it and I cannot argue with it and it will be on a page in a district office in about four years whether I like it or not. **And the man who put the mark in the column is dead, or retired, or asleep, and I have looked, and there is nobody to be angry at at all, and I have been unable to be angry at anything in this town for ten days and this is the tenth day and I have stopped looking.***
>
> *And what I can see coming and cannot stop, and I am writing it down because it is the last day I can write anything down without it being a plan: **when this comes out, six households in this town find out that a mark in a column is why they have not been paid, and a firm of nine people finds out that the same mark is why nobody will lend to it. Those two things point in the same direction, and they are about a hundred yards apart, and they do not know about each other, and there is nobody in this county who could put them together except a man with no standing and eleven documents who has been asked not to.***
>
> *Nothing was rung in this town today. The frame is four streets off with four bolts in it. The crate is in a smith's yard with the sacking off and there is no claim in it and I have not touched it since the fifty-third. All five clauses stand and I wrote the fifth one at the door of a timber yard at about the seventh hour of the morning because I have no standing to ask a merchant a question about a claim in a dead man's name.*
