# Chapter 0084 — A Name In A Market Queue

There is a market on Miremarket about four days in ten and there were about nine hundred people in the square on the seventieth, and Tamsin Rook stood in it for most of a day and wrote one thing on a slate, and the thing took her an hour and a half and is in the fourth column, and it is the first time in eleven years she has put anything in the fourth column at all.

She did not know she was going to do it at about the fourth hour and she had no idea why she did it at about the sixth.

---

The market is not a queue.

That is the thing she had to unlearn first and it took her about two hours. She came into a market of about nine hundred people with a slate under her arm and a method in her head that has worked at ten doors in four counties, and she was standing inside about nine thousand separate queues, and nobody in any of them knew there was a queue.

**A queue is a thing with a shape.** People in a queue can see each other. Two women in a line of six at a pitch will look at each other's faces for four minutes and will not remember one word of what the other said, and will carry away one thing, and the one thing is almost always a name.

---

**First.**

It was at a pitch of needles and thread and the woman behind the counter had about nine of her own on the board and the woman in front of Tamsin Rook was **Nan Pyle**, and the woman behind her was a woman Tamsin did not know and did not get a name for.

Nan Pyle was saying:

“— and it is not a debt the way a shop is a debt. I want that said, because I have heard you say it and I have said it, and it is not a shop. **A shop is a thing you owe money for. That is a line with a name on it and your name has been on it four years and you have never once been asked what it is for, and I am telling you the name is yours, and it is not mine and it is not hers and it is nobody's that I have borrowed, so you can stop looking round this queue for who you are going to owe it to.**”

“I have not said it was a shop.”

“You said the sum. **You said the sum and you said the day and you did not say who was to blame, and I am telling you there is nobody to blame, and I have been in this market for about eleven years and I know that there is nobody to blame.**”

“That is worse.”

“It is worse,” said Nan Pyle, “and I am telling you the day as well because you would otherwise go home and only have the sum. **The twenty-ninth of the first month. That is a day I have had in my head for four years and I have never once written it down, and there is not one person in this square who could tell you where I got it, and neither could I.**”

Nobody in the queue wrote anything down. Nobody in the queue had a slate. **Two women said a name and a day and a sum at a pitch of needles and thread and about forty people walked past it and not one of them knew that anything had been said.**

---

**Second, at about the fifth hour,** at a fish pitch, and this one was worse because it had a place in it.

**Bel Amsel** was at the front of a line of four and **Hilary Croy** was behind her, and what Hilary Croy said was:

“— and I am not the one that has to do anything about it, that is the whole of what I am telling you. **It is at Ashlade. It is four counties. It has not been answered this year and it was not answered the year before and I would not swear to you that it was ever answered at all, and there is nobody at that house now to answer it with, so it has gone about four years without a hand and I have not asked anybody about it because I am not going to be the one that carries somebody else's line.**”

“You have told me whose name it is.”

“I have not. **I have told you a name in a market the way a woman tells a thing in a market, and I have told you a town, and I could not tell you this afternoon whether that name is a man or a house or a child, and neither could you, and that is the honest end of it.**”

And Bel Amsel said, “Well, it is a name and it is on a line, and what are we going to do about a name and a line.”

“We are going to do nothing about it,” said Hilary Croy, “and that is the correct thing to do, and I would like it written down in a book that I am the one that said we are going to do nothing about it.”

---

Tamsin Rook wrote nothing down for an hour and a half.

That is the whole of the middle of the seventieth and it is the hardest thing she has done on a road and it is not a hard thing to anybody who has not done it, and it went in her own hand afterwards with the hour against it:

> *I stood in about nine hundred people for an hour and a half and I wrote nothing. **Eleven years of being careful in public means you do not write where a person can see you writing. That is the whole of what careful is and I have never once been able to put it down, and a careful courier is a courier who looks busy.** And a busy-looking woman at a market is the one thing in this trade that is worth more than money, and there is nobody in a market of nine hundred who is frightened of a woman with a slate because she is not doing anything.*
>
> *And writing nothing down is the one thing my trade has never once let me do. Nine doors, four counties, eleven years, four columns, and the fourth column has been empty every single time, and the whole of the reason it has been empty is that I have never once been in a room where the thing in the fourth column was going past.*

---

**Third, and this is the one that finished it, at about the sixth hour, at a stall of nothing in particular, and there were about five of them in a line.**

**Joss Munn** had a barrow of onions and **Oda Renn** was in front of him and **Peth Bram** was behind Oda Renn, and Tamsin Rook was behind Peth Bram, and this is the whole of what she heard:

“That is the fourth year.”

“It is the fourth year.”

“**It is not the same fourth year as the last one. That is what nobody says. In the first year it is a thing somebody has done and in the fourth year it is a thing you are.** And I do not mind. I have got a brother in it and he is glad of it, he says it is the only thing in the week that is the same every year. **It is a chore. You do it because the line comes round and the line has to be answered and the answer is us.**”

“It is a debt.”

“No it is not.”

“It is a debt if it is owed.”

“**It is not owed. It is answered. There is a difference and you know there is a difference, and the difference is that a thing that is owed is owed to somebody and there is nobody, and a thing that is answered is answered to nobody and that is fine and it does not keep you up.**”

“That is not how it is. My mother says it is how it is.”

“Your mother has not been to a hall about it in about four years and I have been to about nine. **Ask your mother. Do not ask me. I am a man with onions.**”

Nobody knew it was a roll.

That is the sentence the whole of this is built on and she wrote it at about the ninth hour and it is the finding of the seventieth:

**Nobody in any of those lines knew that the thing they were talking about was a document.**

Not concealment. There is nothing to conceal, there is no mark on it, no form, no counter, no district on it, and **the woman telling you your name is yours is not an agent of a thing and holds no office and is owed nothing and is right about who you are.** Joss Munn has put his own hand on his own line four years running. Nan Pyle has carried a date in her head for four years that she cannot source. Hilary Croy has decided to do nothing about a name four counties away and has said so out loud in a market and would say it again tomorrow. **They will all go home and forget it. That is the whole of what makes it work, and it is the whole of what makes it impossible to touch, and it is nine minutes a year.**

---

She filled the fourth column.

It is in a slate that cost a penny and a half off a stallholder at a landing nine days ago and it is the first entry the fourth column has ever had and she wrote it standing up, at about the ninth hour, at the back of a market, and she read it back to nobody, and she has not improved one word of it since, and she wrote it long because it was long:

> *Fourth column. The first one in eleven years. The seventieth, this square, at about the ninth hour, standing at the back of it.*
>
> ***The list is made in queues. About nine minutes a year. About eleven thousand of us a year in this district and every one of us does our nine minutes and none of us is a clerk and none of us is paid and none of us is an agent. A name on a roll is read as a debt roughly four years before anybody in an office knows it is a duty, and by then there is no office involved at all, and no document, and no mark, and no hand but a neighbour's, and the claim is between two people who live near each other.***
>
> *And I have got to write the second half of it and I have been putting it off for an hour.*
>
> ***Nobody is to blame and there is nothing to correct, because there is nothing to correct. Two women said each other's names in a market. That is the whole of the mechanism. There is no villain in it and there is no office in it and there is no order in it and there is nobody in it to be angry with, and it cannot be stopped, because to stop it you would have to get nine thousand people to stop saying a thing to each other in a queue, and you cannot do that, and there is no form for asking anybody to stop, and I have looked.***
>
> *And it cannot be corrected, either, which is the part I have not got round. **You cannot go back into a market and unsay a name. You cannot go back into a kitchen and unhear one. The whole of my trade is built on the fact that a thing said out loud to a person with a slate in a room is a thing that can be read afterwards, and that is true, and it has just stopped being true, because that is a claim and a claim goes sideways, and I have spent eleven years learning to make claims and I have never once had to think about what a claim does to the person who pays it.***
>
> *And the last of it, and I am putting it here because it is the only true thing I have and I will not improve it: **this is the same skill. This is the same skill exactly. I am the best-trained reader of documents in four counties and I have been standing in about nine of them all day, and the reason I can read a market queue is the reason I can read a return, and it is the same reason, and it has taken me eleven years and a month to work out that a thing I was proud of was the thing.***
>
> *I am not going to try and stop it. I have thought about it for an hour and a half and that is what I have got and I am not going to write down any other answer than that one, because I have written down four other answers this month and improved all of them.*

---

And then she did the one thing her whole method is, and she did it in a market of about nine hundred people, and it was not asked for and it was not going to cost anybody anything.

She said a name back.

Nan Pyle was at the pitch of needles and thread, packing up, and Tamsin Rook stood ten feet off, which is the distance she uses and has used for eleven years, and she said, in the woman's own words, out loud, so that about nine people heard it:

“**It is a line with a name on it and your name has been on it four years and you have never once been asked what it is for, and I am telling you the name is yours, and it is not mine and it is not hers and it is nobody's that I have borrowed.**”

Nan Pyle put her hand down on her board.

“Did I say that.”

“You said that.”

“I did. **And I have never said it twice in my life and I did not know this morning that it was a thing I said.**”

“That is what I am here for. **You have said it now, twice, and I have heard it twice, and that is the only thing I have got and I am telling you what it is and it is not worth fourpence and I am not going to sell it to you.**”

“Right,” said Nan Pyle. “Then it is a thing I have said twice and I shall go and think about a line.”

And that is the whole of what Tamsin Rook's method reached on the seventieth, and it is nine people in a market saying a name out loud to another person, and about nine minutes a year, and eleven thousand of them, and she wrote down at about the ninth hour of the evening, in a lane, on a step, with the price of the slate against the fourpence and the fourpence in her hand and the fourpence not spent:

> *Nine people said a name out loud in a queue in a market today and none of them knew they had said a thing and every one of them was told, by somebody, in their own words, that the name was theirs. I have the nine and their words and I have not got a single one of them to do anything with, and I am not going to, and I have said so on the page above in my own hand and I am not going to take it back in the morning because I always take things back in the morning and I have decided about this one.*
>
> *And I did not ring anything. Nobody asked me anything. And I am not going to write the rest of this entry in a market, and I am going to bed.*
