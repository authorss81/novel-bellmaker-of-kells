# Chapter 0090 — A List, And A Thing Nobody Could Answer

He began at about the fourth hour in the room over the draper's, with the slate flat on the table and the chalk in his hand, and he did it the way he does everything, which is to say he began by ruling the columns, and then he ruled them again because the first lot was not straight, and then he sat looking at them.

The four columns. The question and the hour against it. The answer, in the speaker's own words. The refusal, ruled apart. And the fourth, which is a thing nobody could answer, which he had filled once since he came to this town, and which was not Tamsin Rook's and had never been anybody's but his own.

He put the chalk down and picked it up and put it down again and went to stand at the window for about ten minutes.

**There is no list.** That is the whole of the first half hour and it is not a discovery, it is a thing he worked out on the seventy-third in about an hour and three quarters and has not got round to since. There is no book of names in this kingdom. There is no form for asking for one, and no version of the question that a clerk's counter, a district office, a man in a chair or a woman on a landing is permitted to answer, and a man with no standing is not permitted to ask it anyway. **A name is in about nine thousand kitchens in four counties and it is in about nine thousand heads and there is nowhere at all that it is written down all together, and there never has been, and the whole of the arrangement is about nine minutes a year each.**

And he had been about to write it out. On a slate. In four columns. With the hours.

---

He did the sum, because he is a man who does sums, and he did it in the margin of the second column where nobody would ever read it, and the sum is this.

About eleven thousand lines a year go into halls like this one across this district. Four years of them is about forty-four thousand. The names in them are not written anywhere, and to find one out he would have to go to the house and ask, and asking costs a question and a door and a day, and there are four counties, and he is one man, and a barrow will not go at the speed of a question.

**And he did nine.** Not in a month. In one day, in a market of about nine hundred people, on the seventieth, standing in a line at a stall of nothing in particular behind a man with onions, and nine people said a name out loud to another person in about four hours and every one of them did it for nothing and not one of them knew they had done it.

Forty-four thousand, at nine a day, is a figure he wrote down and then struck out, and then he wrote it down again underneath, because he wanted to see it twice.

> *About forty-four thousand names and nine a day and one man on foot. I am not going to write what that is. I have written it and struck it out and I am writing it down a second time because I have never in my life struck out a sum and I want to look at it twice, and a man who strikes out a sum is a man who does not like the answer and not a man who does not believe it.*
>
> *And the other half, and it is the half that matters, and I have got it exactly: **it is being made about eleven thousand a year faster than anybody is unrolling it, and there is nobody unrolling it, and there has never been anybody unrolling it, and the reason there is not is that it does not need unrolling. It is a mouth and it is open and it is not owed to anybody and nothing is asked of it and it costs about nine minutes a year each and it works.***

He shut the slate and went down the street.

---

The market was on and about nine hundred people were in the square, and he stood at the top of it for about two hours and he did not ask anybody anything.

**That is the sentence and it is the whole of what he did, and it took two hours, and he wrote it down at about the sixth hour, standing at a trough, and it is one line and he has not improved it: *I stood in about nine hundred people for two hours and I did not ask one of them a question, and I have written that down as a piece of work and not as a failure, and I do not know yet whether it is one and I have not got anybody to tell me.***

He watched three of the nine work.

He did not know they were three of the nine. He knew the names because a woman with a slate had said them out loud once in a lane and had not been writing them down at the time, and he had nine in his head and could not put a county to two of them and could not be certain of a first name for one, and that is what about two hours in a market will do to a list.

**Nan Pyle** was at a pitch of needles and thread with about nine of her own on the board, and a line of about six, and she was saying nothing to anybody and was not the same as on the seventieth and would not be tomorrow.

**Joss Munn** was two pitches down with a barrow of onions and was talking about the price of a thing and not about a line and had no idea a man with a slate had ever written a word he said near her.

And a woman at a fish pitch said a name — he heard it, he heard the shape of it, he was eleven feet off and he caught about two words and the day — **and the whole of the exchange took about eleven seconds, and the woman said the name and then said that it was not her name, it was the name of the house, and the house owed it and she was owed by it, and that she had said so at a fish pitch before and would say so again and did not mind who heard.**

And he stood there with a chalk in his hand and about nine hundred people going past and he did not write it down, because she had said it to a stranger and not to him, and because **the moment he writes a name that a person has said in a market on a slate, that name is a thing he is carrying, and a thing he is carrying is a thing somebody may read, and there are four columns in this world and he knows all four of them and he has not got a fifth.**

He wrote the sum in the margin instead. He wrote *eleven seconds* and *a queue of four* and he wrote *nobody asked*.

---

He was on the stone landing at about the ninth hour with the slate under his arm and there is a crane on it and about nine boats and a road up off the water.

The cart had gone up that road four days ago with a child on it, and there was nothing on the landing, and there was no reason for anybody to be on a landing at the ninth hour except that there is nothing else to do at a landing, and she was sitting on the bollard with her boots off and her feet in the water, which is a thing she does and has done for eleven years and which he has never once commented on.

“Read it back to me,” he said.

“That is my line and not yours.”

“**It is the whole thing and it is the fourth column, and I have never read a fourth column back to anybody in my life because there has only ever been one and it is about a paper and a building, and this one is about nine people, and I want it read back by the only person in this county who has read eleven thousand things back to the people who said them.**”

She took the slate.

She read all of it standing up, and slowly, and it took her about four minutes because there were nine lines and each one had a name and a day and a pitch and about nine words, and she did not stop it once and she did not speed up once, and when she came to the end she did not hand it back.

“**Nine.**”

“Nine.”

“**You have got nine and there are about forty-four thousand and you have written the sum down and struck it out and written it down again, and I have watched you do that, and you did it because you wanted to see it twice, and you are a man who is frightened of a figure and it is the first thing about you I have ever found that I did not expect.**”

“I am.”

“**Say the rest of it. Not the part about the columns. The part after.**”

He did not want to and did it.

“**And I am writing down a list of nine people who have said a name out loud in a market and did not know they had done it. And every one of those nine was told, by somebody, in their own words, that the name was theirs.**”

She handed the slate back.

“**Then there are two lists and you have written one, and the reason there are two is that on the seventieth I stood ten feet off a woman at a pitch of needles and thread and said a sentence back to her in her own words, out loud, so that about nine people heard it.**”

“Yes.”

“**And that is not the same act as a woman saying her neighbour's name in a line.**”

“It is not.”

“**No. It is the opposite act and it took eleven years and I have never once had it named and I have written about four hundred things in four columns in this kingdom and nobody has ever asked me what it is for.**”

He had the chalk out.

“Then it is a thing that could be answered, and the fourth column is for a thing nobody could answer, and so it goes in the first one, and you have had your foot in the water for about four minutes and you have still not told me what to do about it.”

“**No. I have told you. You have not been listening, and I will say it in a sentence and I am not going to say it twice, because I have said it to you about four things this month and I am not going to make a habit of it in a county where I cannot hear half of what anybody says anyway.**”

And she took the chalk off him, which she has done once, at a landing, about a year ago, and wrote one word in the ninth line, and gave it back, and did not explain it.

He looked at it.

The ninth line had read: *Nine. A courier with a slate, who said a **name** out loud in the ordinary way in a market, at ten feet, so that about nine people heard it, and who has never once been asked what it was for.*

The ninth line now read: *Nine. A courier with a slate, who said a **sentence** out loud in the ordinary way in a market, at ten feet, so that about nine people heard it, and who has never once been asked what it was for.*

“All right,” he said. “That is the whole of it and it is one word and I am going to make the change and I am not going to ask you a second thing about it.”

“**You are going to ask me whether it was worth it.**”

“No,” he said. “**I am not, and you are not going to tell me, and if I asked it you would be right to say that I had asked a thing that has no answer, and I have got one of those this month already and I did not know what it was until four days ago.**”

He made the change. He wrote the line out again clean with the hours flat against it. And then he did the other thing, which is the thing his method actually is, and he did it in about four seconds and did not enjoy it.

**He ruled the ninth line off from the other eight and moved it down the page, and he put it in the third column, and he did not merge it, and he wrote the hour against it, and the reason is the whole of the rule and it is the only rule he has: the third column is for words a person has said in their own mouth, and a name is not a thing a person says, it is a thing a person owes, and a sentence is a thing a person says, and that is the entire difference, and it has been in his hand for six days and it was handed back to him at the ninth hour of the evening by the only person it belonged to.**

“And that is the third time,” he said, “that anybody has read a thing back to me and changed one word. A woman in a market twelve days ago changed one word in a sentence that is now in a book in a hall, and a woman in a kitchen two days ago changed one word of mine and it went from *asked* to *sent for*, and it has happened twice on the page in twelve days and **nobody in this county has ever once heard of a thing being got right by a person changing one word of it, and I have watched it three times and I have never once seen it in a building.**”

“**It does not happen in a building because in a building a word costs something,**” said Tamsin Rook, and put her boots on, and that is the whole of what she said about it and she has not improved it since.

---

He wrote the last entry of the seventy-sixth at about the eleventh hour of the evening, in the room over the draper's, in the flat hand, with the hours, and he filled all four columns and merged nothing and it is the longest thing he has written in six weeks and the plainest.

> *The seventy-sixth. I tried to write the list out and I got nine names in four days and about forty-four thousand of them and I have written the sum down twice, and there is nothing to be done with a sum.*
>
> *Here is the whole of the finding and I am putting it in the third column because it is a thing a person said to me on a landing four days ago and not a thing I found out.*
>
> ***An answer is a person and may be withdrawn. A duty is a mouth and cannot. The roll does not need anybody's answer, and the list does not need anybody's answer either, and that is the whole of what has happened in this town since the fifty-seventh, and it did not happen in a hall and it did not happen to an office. It happened in a queue, at a pitch, in about eleven seconds, for nothing, by a person who is owed nothing and is not an agent of anything and is usually right about who she is.***
>
> *And here is the thing I have got to write down and I have been putting it off since about the sixth hour of the afternoon, in a room, alone, with a fire I did not light.*
>
> ***I have been building a method for six weeks and it is not a way of protecting anybody. It is a way of making certain that there is a document.***
>
> *I have been careful all my life. I have thought careful was a habit about hands and about ink and about a person. **It is not. It is a way of writing things down, and I have found that out by writing a list of nine people down, and the reason I could not stop is that a list of nine people is a list of nine people who are safe. They have said a name in a queue and gone home and forgotten it. If it is on a slate in a room over a draper's shop then it is not forgotten, and then somebody may read it, and then it is a fact about them, and it is a fact about them because I wrote it, and I did not ask them.***
>
> *And a record is a sentence other people may read. I had that on the sixty-sixth, standing against a wall, and I thought it was a discovery about a clerk. **It is a discovery about me. There is one kind of sentence in this world and it is the one that can be read afterwards, and I have been manufacturing them for six weeks because a man who has nothing to carry makes a document, and a document is the only thing he can be certain of.***
>
> *And the second thing, and it is worse and it is the thing I am not going to be able to put down cleanly: **I said one true sentence in a room in this town seven days ago and it cost nine pitches and about thirty-six pounds a quarter and twenty years and nine years and a handcart, and it cost a household its front of a row, and the sentence was true, and nobody has been forgiven for anything and nobody has apologised for anything and I am not going to be told it was worth it.***
>
> *And I am about to write a list that will make it possible for a person to say a second one.*
>
> *I know that. I have known it since about the sixth hour of the afternoon and I have not been able to put the slate down and I have not been able to leave the room, and I have written the nine down anyway, and about nine people in this town are not safe because of a man who cannot stop writing. **A list of nine names is nine more mouths and I am making them and I am going to go on making them, because I do not have another thing, and I am writing it down here at the eleventh hour of the evening so that there is one place in this book where it is written that I knew and did it, and if anybody ever asks me what I was doing in this town since the fifty-seventh that is the sentence they get and not the other ones.***
>
> *I am not going to call it necessary. Nobody is going to come and tell me it was necessary. And I am not going to be a man who has only been a door.*
>
> *Third column, ruled apart, the hours flat against them. A woman at a fish pitch said a name and said it was the house's and not hers, in about eleven seconds, to a stranger, and I did not write it down. **A woman in a doorway said a hand against a line is the answer to a question, and I wrote it down, and I have not improved it, and it is a true sentence and it is in a book in a house and not in a hall and no clerk will ever read it and that is the only good thing that has happened to me in six weeks.***
>
> *Fourth column, one line, and it is the second time in my life, and the first one was about a paper and a building, and this one is about nine people in a market:*
>
> ***The list cannot be unrolled. It is in the memory of about eleven thousand people and it was made in about nine minutes a year and it can be walked out of, house by house, by one person on foot, in about four years, and it will take longer than that, and it is being made about eleven thousand a year faster than anybody is unrolling it, and there is nobody unrolling it, and there is no form for a list and there never has been, and I have not got a place to take a slate to and I am not going to look for one, because a slate that goes into a building is a column and a stamp and I know what those are and I have had thirty-six days of learning what a stamp is and it is not me.***
>
> *And the last of it, and I am writing it flat and I am not going to improve it, and it is the whole of what I have left and it is not a stage and it is not a power and I have reached for nothing in this town and I am not going to write about reaching:*
>
> ***A column a clerk can certify and a list that cannot be certified at all, and a name on a roll that begins, in about four years, to be a duty.***
>
> *Nothing was rung in this town on the seventy-sixth and nothing has been rung in it since the fifty-third in another county. The frame is four streets off in a yard behind a draper's shop with four bolts made fast in it and no clapper and a receipt that forbids anybody to furnish one. The crate is in a smith's yard in this street with the sacking off and there is no claim of any kind in it and I have not put a hand on it in twenty-three days. **All five clauses stand. I wrote the fifth one twice this week, once at a counter and once at a gate, and both times it cost me a question and both times it was the correct price.***
>
> *And there is a woman who has said nine words on a landing four days ago and has not asked me for anything and is not going to be thanked by me for them, and she is asleep in a room over a draper's shop with a slate under the head of the bed, and I am going to shut this book.*
