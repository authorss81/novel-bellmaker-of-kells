# Chapter 0068 — Whose Mouth

She was up at about the fourth hour on the morning of the fifty-third and she let him sleep, and she did not lie there doing arithmetic about the four o'clock, which she has done every morning for eleven years and which on that morning came out the same as it had come out on the other five mornings since they had been on the water.

The third day of a market is the day the town comes in. She had known that before she got to Bramden and she had heard it twice more in two days from people who had no reason to tell her anything, and the square at about the seventh hour was fuller than a square of about eleven hundred ought to hold, and there were barrows in it that had come up the landing road and a row of booths down the far side that nobody had mentioned and a fiddle on the steps of the cross.

And at about the eighth hour of the morning, in the room over the draper's, with his hands still cold, Tomas Vale said:

“I am going to ring it at the top of the hour in the square. I have thought about it all night and I have not thought of a better hour and I have not thought of a better place, and the parish thing at the low end wants somebody to ring a bell for it and I am going to be the somebody.

“**I want you standing where they can see you.**”

She was sitting on the end of the bed with her stockings in her hand and she did not stop what she was doing, and when she had finished the row she looked up.

“No,” she said.

He waited, which he had been warned about in four counties and which he did anyway.

“I am not going to explain it,” she said, “and I am not going to be asked twice today, and if you ask me a second time I will say the same word and then I will go down the stairs and you will not be able to find me for about an hour, and we both know that is true and neither of us knows where I would go.”

“All right.”

“That is the whole of it. That is four words and I have used them and I am not going to give you a reason, and I want it noticed that I have given you one thing, which is where I will be, and I have given you a *place* and not a *want*, and there is a difference and you are the only man in this kingdom who could not tell it.”

“All right,” he said.

“It also has to be written down, because we wrote it down the three times we used it. Mercy Slee put it on a slate in this county on the thirty-first and that was the first time it was used on anybody, including us. Then the flat fork on the thirty-fourth, where I said no to you and the ground was a carrier's book. Then the shop on the thirty-eighth, where you stayed out while I asked about paper. Then the ninth door on the forty-third, where I said no once and you did not ask me again. That is four and this is the fifth, and there is a slate in a town four miles behind us with the word *leash* on it written by a woman called Mercy Slee in her own hand and underlined twice, and neither of us wrote it and neither of us wrote it first, and I have kept a copy of it in the front of my bag since the day she put it in my hand, and I have not looked at it in about nine days.”

“I have not asked you for anything,” he said. “I have asked you to stand in a square.”

“**That is asking me to stand in a square.** And I want you to hear how it sounds, because you are a careful man and you have not heard it. If I stand next to a bell in that square with a bag on my shoulder, then the square decides what the two of us are, and it will decide it in about four seconds, and it will decide it right, because I am a courier with a bell in her bag and you are a man with a bell in a crate and neither of us has ever been in this county before. And people will say afterwards that the bell was rung by two people, and that one of them could not hear it, and that is a sentence about me and it will be said at the low end of that square within a day and it will be said in Fourmile inside a month. **So: no.** Not because of the bell. Because of the standing next to it. And I have not asked you for anything either and if you had asked me for that instead I would have said yes before you finished the sentence, and I want that in the book as well, and I want you to write both halves or neither.”

He wrote both halves. It took him about ninety seconds and she watched him do it and did not read it over his shoulder, which she had done three times in a month and which she had stopped doing on the fortieth without saying that she had stopped doing it.

“All right,” he said. “Then where will you be.”

“Down at the landing, and then up at the market cross, and then wherever you are, about ten feet off, for as long as it takes.”

“Ten feet off.”

“Ten feet is not near enough to be part of it and it is near enough to write down what it is.”

---

The bell came out of the crate on the sill at about the half hour and she carried the crate down the outside stair to the square on her own shoulder because he was in the road with the receipt in his coat checking the bolts on a Crown object in a yard four streets away, and she set it down on the kerb by the cross and took the sacking off it there in the open, and a boy of about nine asked her what it was and she said, “A bell,” and he said, “Whose,” and she said, “Nobody’s,” and he went away, and she thought about the answer for about a minute and a half.

At about the quarter to the hour she got the slate.

It was not hers. She had given the last one away on a boat on the forty-seventh to a woman keeping a landing tally who had asked for something to write on, and she had not asked for it back, and she had said why, and the reason had been true. What she got on the morning of the fifty-third was the back of a fishmonger’s slate, and on the front of it, which she could see because it was lying face up on a trestle, there was a price and a place and a date in chalk, and the fishmonger looked at her when she turned it over and said, “That side’s the price.”

“I know. I’m leaving it as it is. I want the side nobody writes prices on.”

“That’s a fair thing to say. **It is the back of a slate, mind, and not a licence.**”

“Yes,” said Tamsin Rook. “That is the whole of what it is and I will say so out loud in a minute and they can all write it down.”

She drew four columns on it with a stub of chalk out of the fishmonger’s tray, and the columns took her about ninety seconds, and she was watched the whole time and nobody helped her, and when she had them she put four marks in the bottom of them, one for each, and a line under each mark, and then she went and stood by the kerb at about a quarter past the hour with the slate under her arm and her bag on her shoulder and the bell on a low block with a folded coat under it.

She used two of the four. The other two she left empty all afternoon and the fishmonger asked her about them twice, and she told him the same thing both times, which was that a man from Kells keeps four columns in a book and that the third of the four is a column for a thing somebody refused and the fourth is a column for a thing nobody could answer, and that there was no such thing in the square this afternoon and she was not going to invent one to fill a column, and that if she filled it she would be as bad as the man she was writing down.

Nobody in that square had been told what it was.

That was not an accident and it was not cunning either. He had wanted to say something first. He had written out four sentences in the room over the draper's at about the ninth hour and had read them to her and had asked her whether the third one was too much, and she had said the third one was too much, and he had cut it, and what was left was a thing about a bell and a thing about himself and a thing about standing next to it, and she had said, “**You have written a thing where a man is telling a square what a bell is for, and the minute you open your mouth you are the man who says what it is for, and the square will agree with you because you are the one with the bell and it is easier.**”

“And if I say nothing.”

“Then the square decides, and you will find out what the square thinks a bell with no claim in it is for, and that is worth knowing and it is worth more to you than anything you have said to me in a month.” She had put the four sentences on the sill. “And you will not be able to complain about it afterwards, and that is the price of it, and the price is the reason I am letting you do it.”

So he said nothing for about four minutes after the top of the hour, which she counted, and the fiddle stopped, because a fiddle in a market square with about nine hundred people in it stops for nothing except a thing somebody thinks is going to be an event, and then Tomas Vale picked up a bell of fourteen pounds two ounces that somebody else had made out of metal a man had sold with a record behind it, and rang it once.

---

She had thought she would write the words. It was not words. It was a single stroke about two seconds long, a plain carrier with no claim in it, on a block on a market square with nine hundred people in it, and the whole square heard the same thing, and the whole square understood the same thing, and about a second after the sound stopped there were about nine hundred different things in nine hundred people.

That was the finding. It was a bad finding and it was the true one and she wrote it at the top of the slate before she wrote anything else, and then she filled the columns, and the columns took about forty minutes because four people would not stand still and one of them wanted to be quoted exactly and one of them wanted to be quoted as near as he could get without being a liar.

**Column one**, in his words, and she had asked him for them at the kerb at the top of the hour and he had given them in about four sentences and she had written them down and she had not improved them and she read them back to him once and he said they were right:

> *Rung once at the top of the hour at the market cross in this town on the fifty-third day of the month, by Tomas Vale of Kells, a bellmaker, who says: this bell has no claim in it and no keeper in it and no name in it but the caster's mark and the day of the casting. It says only what I say while it is moving. I am not a keeper of anything. I am not speaking for any household in this town or in any other. I have no household to speak for. I rang it because I have bought it and I have not yet found out what it is for.*

**Column two** held four people, and she wrote them one under the other with a line ruled between each, and she did not put a heading over them that said *what the crowd said it was for*, because a heading like that is a sentence and a sentence is the thing the whole month has been against. She put a name at the head of each entry and the same name again at the foot of it with the hour against that, and she did not shorten anybody's entry to make it fit, and she wrote one of them in twice because the chalk went and she rubbed it out and it is legible and there is a place in it where it is not.

**Mab Quiller, a chandler, at the top of the hour:** *He has said it is a warning and he has not said of what. That is the oldest thing there is and he has done it in a market on a market day and a market day is the last place on earth for it, because everything in a market is for sale and a warning is not for sale.*

**Harl Gant, a carter, at about the fifth minute past:** *It is for the river. A bell on a wharf with nothing in it and one stroke on it is a river thing, that is how the wharves do it, and he has come off a boat and he has the look of a man who has come off a boat.*

**Wil Trotter, a dyer, at about the ninth minute past:** *It is for nothing. He said himself it is not for anything yet. I have been in this square thirty years and I have never heard a bell rung on purpose for nothing and I do not think nine hundred people are going to hear one again.*

And **Nesta Bray**, who had a barrow and had come up off the landing that morning and had put the barrow against the cross and had not moved it for forty minutes, and who would not give a sentence at all until the fourth time she asked, and who gave it then in a form that took her about nine seconds:

*Whatever he says it says. That is what a bell is when there is nothing written in it. A bell is a mouth and a mouth belongs to the person whose claim is in it, and there is no claim in this one, so it belongs to whoever is standing there when it stops.*

And there was a fifth voice in that square, and it was the loudest one, and it was not one of the four and she did not write it down, and that was a decision and she made it at the time and it was the right one and she has never been sure.

It came from a man with a stall of cutlery about nine feet along, and she did not know his name then and did not learn it, and what he said was this, and he said it twice, and the second time he said it to her:

“**A bell is a mouth, and a mouth belongs to the person whose claim is in it, and there is no claim in that one, so it belongs to whoever is standing there when it stops. Sell him a knife while he is at it, master, he is the only man in this square who can hear what he is saying.**”

And the square laughed, and about nine hundred people did not, and she wrote the four and not the fifth, and the reason she wrote four and not five is that the four had given her a sentence in their own words and had stood there while she wrote it, and the fifth had given her a sentence for a stall and had said it twice to a crowd, and one of those is a record and the other is a performance, and she has carried parcels for eleven years and she knows which of the two she has been paid for.

She read the slate back. That was the part she was most careful about and it took about eleven minutes and it is the whole of what she learned on the fifty-third of the month, and it is the thing she was good at, and it cost her.

She read the four columns back to the four people, one at a time, and each of them heard their own words and nobody else's, and **two of them changed their own words and nobody changed anybody else's.** Quiller wanted hers to say *warning, of the river, at the low end*, and it did not say that, and she would not let it. Trotter wanted his to say *nothing at all, nothing, forever*, and when he heard the first version he said, “That is not what I said, I said I do not think I will hear one again, and the difference is everything, because the first is a thing I am claiming and the second is a thing I am saying.” So his went in again as he gave it. Gant did not change his, and gave a reason, and the reason was that he would be going to be asked in a week by a man off a boat what a bell on a wharf was for and he wanted to be able to say he had said it in a market square and not be ashamed of it.

And Bray would not have hers written at all, and then she did, and when she heard it she was quiet for a second and then said: “That is what I said.”

“It is what you said.”

“It is what I said and I have never said it before and I did not know I thought it,” said Nesta Bray, “and I have got a barrow and I am not a wise woman and you have written down that I am.”

“You are. That is what a name under a line is.”

---

The crowd did not go back to the market.

That is the thing about a bell with nothing in it and she had been right about it and it cost more than she had thought it would. People came and stood about four deep and waited for the second one, and when the second one did not come, about a third of them left, and about a third of them stayed to argue about what the first one had been, and the last third drifted about the square all afternoon saying the sentence to each other in their own words, and by about the second hour of the afternoon there were four distinct stories in that square about a bell, and about the fourth hour there were nine, and she heard two of them being invented, and both of the inventions were better than anything that had actually been said, and one of them had a child in it.

Nobody asked her for a reason. She wants that in whatever is written down. Not one person in that square, of about nine hundred, asked her why she had written a slate down, or what she was, or where her road was, or why the man with the bell had got a woman with a bag to write for him, and nobody put a hand on the slate, and nobody asked to hold it, and nobody said *that is a Crown thing* or *are you with the district* or *is that his wife*.

The whole of the credit for that belongs to a man who said four sentences at the top of the hour that said nothing about a claim and everything about himself, and to a bell that had no wax in it, and to about nine hundred people who had never seen a thing like it and had no reason to be afraid of it yet.

And it goes in the other column of the ledger, which is the one she has never had to write in before, and which is this: for eleven years she has done the work of being careful in public and not one single person has ever come to her afterwards to say that they had noticed she was there. And on the fifty-third of the month, in a market square, in about nine hundred people's hearing, a man asked her to stand next to a bell, and she said no, and the reason she said no was that the *standing next to it* was the whole of the value and the only way to have the value is to be the one who is visible, and she chose the four columns over the standing next to it, and about eleven people in that square said out loud that they had seen the woman write the four lines down, and eleven is eleven, and it is the first time anything she has done has been noticed by anybody, and she was not in the middle of the square when any of them noticed it. She was four columns and ten feet off.

And she was right about the hearing as well, and she had been right about it for a month without once being thanked for it, and the reason she was right is the plainest thing about her and it is the only thing in this chapter that anybody in another county could use.

**A note is a thing one person hears and a column is a thing nine hundred people can read, and a person who has lost the first one is not the worse person for reading, and everybody in every parish in this kingdom has a book.**

---

She got to the landing at about the sixth hour of the evening and sat on the crane steps with the slate flat on her knees, and there was the tide up and about eleven boats, and the post was not due back until the fourth, and the whole of the market was still up the hill being wrong about a bell.

There was a man in the boat with his hand on the rope who had been watching her come down, and he asked her whether she would take a parcel up to the high end of the town for him, and she said no, and he said, “There is nothing in it that you need to know,” and she said, “I did not ask, and it is not that, and the answer is no and I am not taking another one today,” and he took the rope and did not argue.

That was the first work she had turned down in three days, and she noticed the shape of it, because it was the shape of the fourth use of the leash, and the leash does not touch it, and it never has. The slate says: *neither uses the other's road without asking first, an answer may be no, and the person who asked may not ask again that day.* It is about the two of them and it has no third person in it and it has no boatman in it and it has no parcel in it, and if he had asked her to take that parcel she would have said yes or no on the merits and not once on the slate. And when she turned it over the other way it was just as true: nobody on the water had asked her for anything, and she was not standing next to anything, and the bell she was carrying had not been rung and would not be, and there was no leash on that at all, because the leash is not the whole of the rules and never was. It is two rules, and one of them is for him and one of them is for her, and the fourth use of it this morning cost him her standing in a square and cost her a square where eleven people said out loud that they had seen her, and both of those are hers and not his, and the slate says so, and she has never once wished the slate was not there, and she has thought about it about nine times this week.

And she said the thing, because there was nobody within twenty feet of her and because the water was there and because she had been carrying a road in her head since the fourth day of the month and it had gone stale.

“The road is not for him,” she said, out loud, to the river. “The road was never for him, and it will not be for him after him, and if he is killed on it tomorrow the road does not stop.

“**A name on a roll is not a place.** That is the whole of it and I have said it four times since the boat and this is the first time I have said it to something that cannot answer me. A name on a roll in a building four counties from here is on a road somewhere, and a roll is not a road, and no clerk in this kingdom has ever walked one, and the only thing that has ever gone looking is a person on foot with a bag. Jory Fenn was eight and was taken six days before the day this all started and his only papers are a line on a schedule and a piece of a household paper with his line cut out of it. He is not a cause. He is not even the reason any more, because I have been on this road for a month and there are about nine thousand names in this county and I could walk to every one of them.

“I am on the road because a roll is a list of places that people are not, and the road is the only thing that has ever gone to a place to find out whether a person is in it, and I have been eleven years learning how to be that, and I cannot hear the note of a bell any more and I do not need to, because a road does not need a note. A road needs a person who turns up at the door and says her name out loud and lets the door decide.”

She put her hand flat on the slate.

“And that is the whole of what the road is for and it is not a man with a barrow, and it was not a man with a barrow on the thirty-fourth when I said I would go as far as he goes, and it will not be one in a year, and the day somebody offers me a road that runs through him I am going to say no to it in about four seconds and I am not going to be asked to explain.”

And then she wrote on the slate, in the margin, where it did not touch the four columns, one line, and it is the only line on it that is hers:

> *I wrote these four down in the square at the market cross in this town on the fifty-third day of the month, at the top of the hour and for about forty minutes after it, at about ten feet off. I am not a person in this square. I am the hand that wrote down what four other people said and what the man with the bell said. He did not choose the words for me and I did not choose his. The slate is the fishmonger's and I have asked him for it to be kept and he has put it on the trestle with the price side up and anybody may read it and nobody has to give anything to anybody to read it. I have not been asked to hand it to a district office and there is no form for that and I have not looked for one.*

---

He came down about the eighth hour of the evening with the crate under his arm and the sacking over it, and she could see from twenty feet that he had been in the square, because a man who has stood in a square for eight hours has a particular way of putting the crate down.

“That was a bad afternoon,” he said.

“It was the last of a three-day market,” said Tamsin Rook. “There were nine hundred people in that square and four of them wrote a column and one of them invented a child. You rang a bell with nothing in it in a square on the day a town comes into a market. I want it in the book that the hour was the top of the hour and the day was the third of the three, and both of those were chosen before either of us got up.”

He put the crate on the crane steps and sat down on it, which he had never done.

“I did not say anything,” he said.

“You said four sentences and then you did not say anything for four minutes, and the four minutes were the worst four minutes of my life, and I want you to understand what happened in them. About nine hundred people were waiting for you to tell them what it meant and they were not angry, they were *waiting*, and every second you did not speak, somebody in that square filled in the gap with the best thing they had, and by the fourth minute there were about nine different answers in nine hundred people and you were not one of them, and you had not the smallest part of the credit for any of them, and I wrote four of them down.”

“That is the worst sentence anybody has said to me in a month.”

“It is the second worst,” she said. “The worst one was in an inn yard on the forty-first and it was about your fifth clause, and you were not in the room, and I have not forgotten it either.”

He looked at the slate on her knees.

“Then price it,” he said. “You have said you can price a thing. Price that. I have been asking you since the thirty-fourth whether you will stand next to something and you have said no three times and I have got nothing and no trouble, and this morning I asked you a fourth time and I have got the four columns and the whole of the day, and I want a number, and I want it now, and I want it to be a number and not a kind face.”

“Fourpence,” said Tamsin Rook.

“That is a shilling.”

“No. It is **fourpence**, and I have not said what I am pricing.” She turned the slate so he could see it. “Look at the four columns and tell me what they are worth. And then look at me and tell me what it is worth that the man who rang the bell did not have a woman standing next to him saying what it meant. That is a bell and four columns and about nine hundred people, and the bell is fourteen pounds two ounces and the four columns are the back of a fishmonger's slate, and it cost a pound and fourteen shillings and about forty years of somebody's fees.

“Now tell me what it is worth to the man in the crate. **It is worth fourpence, and here is the fourpence: nobody in that square can say a single thing about that bell that is not a thing they were told, and the only things they were told are four sentences said by the man swinging it, and one of those four said that he does not know what it is for.** Everything else in that square this afternoon is a rumour with a child in it, and none of it is yours, and none of it is anybody's, and you could stand in a market square every day for the rest of your life and not one thing in the market would be yours.

“That is what standing next to it would have bought you. It would have bought you about nine hundred people saying *that is the man and the woman says it is his*, and it would have cost you every hour of it, and it would have been worth it for about three weeks, and after that the fourpence would have been the only thing left in the market and you would not have been able to get it back, because **a mouth that belongs to somebody is a mouth, and a mouth that belongs to nobody is a mouth, and the second one costs a man fourpence a day forever and it is the only kind of mouth that can be used on a new thing every single morning of its life.**”

He sat on the crate and did not say anything for long enough that a boat came in and went out again.

“That is not about your hearing,” he said.

“No,” said Tamsin Rook. “**And I want that said out loud, because you have been careful with me about it for a month and I have watched you not use it, and I have also watched you use it about nine times by not using it, which is worse and is a thing you do not know you are doing.** I cannot hear the note. I have not been able to since the fourth day of the month and I am not going to be able to again, and I have said that out loud in a lane in a town four miles behind us to about a hundred people and I put it on a record on purpose, and nothing in that square this afternoon turned on it, and it did not turn on it because I was ten feet off and writing down four other people, and that is a thing I did with my hands and not with my ears, and it cost me a square in which eleven people said out loud that they had seen me, and I would do it again at the same price and I am not going to be thanked for it by you in a room, and if you say one word about the fourth of last month I will put this slate in the water and it is not deep there and I will lose the four columns of a man I have never met being angry about a thing he was not in the room for.”

“I am not going to say a word about it,” he said.

“Good. Write that down too.”

---

At about the ninth hour of the evening the slate was on a trestle at the top of the landing with the price side up, and about forty people had stood in front of it, and a man had asked whether he might copy the fifth column, which does not exist, and Nesta Bray had told him there were four and that the fifth was his own mouth and that he could put it down himself if he had a slate.

And the fishmonger, whose name is Wyn and which is on his sign, moved the trestle to the front of his pitch on the strength of it, and by the next morning the slate was leaning against a barrel of herrings with the price side out, and on the fourth day of the month it was still there, and on the day after that it was not, and nobody in that town ever established who took it, and Tamsin Rook did not ask, because she had said in the margin of it that anybody might read it and nobody had to give anything to anybody to read it, and a thing you put where anybody may take it is a thing you have not given to anybody, and that is the difference between a slate and a leash and it is the only difference there has ever been.

And the four columns were in his day-book that night, copied out in her hand at his request, with the hour, in four columns, not merged, and beside them he wrote:

> *I rang a bell with no claim in it once, in a market square, at the top of the hour, on the last day of a three-day market, and I said four sentences and then I was quiet for four minutes, and in the four minutes a town made up nine things, and four of them were written down by a woman at ten feet off, and eight of them were not, and there is no form anywhere in this kingdom for the eight of them and there never will be, and that is not an injustice. That is a bell working.*
>
> *And I have not got a household, and I am not going to have one, and my receipt says in five places that I have no standing in any ward, and I wrote the fifth one, and on the fifty-third of this month I stood in a square and the square gave me a thing for nothing and I did not take it, and the reason I did not take it is that a woman I travel with refused to stand next to it and told me a number, and the number was fourpence.*
>
> *I do not know yet what any of it is for. I said so in a square, out loud, in my own name, to about nine hundred people, and it is the only sentence I have said in a month that I have not taken back.*
