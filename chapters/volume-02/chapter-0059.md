# Chapter 0059 — Nine Households, One Signature

She started at the fourth hour and she did nine doors between the fourth hour and the fourth hour of the afternoon, and they are not nine houses in a street because there is no street, they are nine doors between a sluice and a bridge along about a mile and a half of towpath and two lanes off it, and she did them in the order the water goes.

And she did not ring her bell, and it was in her bag the whole time, and it was the heaviest thing she carried.

The first one took nine minutes and it went exactly as a door goes when a woman with a slate stands on the step and asks three questions in the same words.

The three questions, and she used the same words nine times and did not improve one of them, which took her about a mile of walking to be certain of:

*First: has any person ever put their name to a paper for this house — a Crown form, or a return, or a certificate — without anybody asking them to?*

*Second: is the person who put their name there living in this house now?*

*Third: do you want that written down, and may I write your no as a no and not as nothing?*

The third one is Nell Fenn's. It is not her words and it is not her idea; it is a thing a girl of sixteen stood in a lane in front of about a hundred people and said out loud in a market court in a different month, and it is on a page in a box on a board in a town four days behind them, and Tamsin Rook had been in that room and had heard a Crown clerk read a sentence out three times, and she had gone up the towpath afterwards and worked out that the whole of what she had seen was a refusal being kept in a column of its own, and she had thought at the time that it was a girl being careful.

It is not a girl being careful. It is the only thing she knows now that actually works.

---

The first house said no to the first question, and would not have the second asked, and said yes to the third, and then would not write anything, and Tamsin Rook wrote the word *no* on her slate and turned it round and showed it, and the woman at the door read it upside down and said, "That is not my no," and Tamsin Rook said, "It is the only shape a no has got, and I will put down the hour and the door and the fact that you read it and did not agree with it, and there is no version of this where your no is on a piece of paper in my bag."

Which is the whole of the argument, and she said it nine times, and it is in a lane nine times, and it is the only thing she taught anybody that day.

The second house was a refusal at the door, in about four words, and the third was a refusal and a door shut, and the fourth was a refusal and a man who came out into the lane to say it to her in the open, at more length than anybody else had, and none of which length was an answer.

Eight of the nine said no.

The ninth, which was the second door above the sluice and had a green door and about nine people in it, was a man who said no to the first question and then said, "I have had every form for eleven years and I have never once been asked anything by anybody, and I want that written down, and I want my name on it, and I do not care in the least what happens to me after today."

She wrote it down with the hour and read it back and he made her change one word, and she changed it, and then he signed his own refusal-of-the-question in his own hand at the bottom of it, and kept it himself, and that is the only one of the nine that is in a house tonight as an answer.

And Tamsin Rook wrote in her own book — in pencil, on a page of her own, not on the slate — *Eight refusals in eight hands. One answer in a ninth. Nobody's name is in my bag. Nobody's number is in my bag. I have got the doors in the order the water goes and a slate, and the slate goes in the water at this sluice before I am out of the parish, because a slate is the one thing a courier carries that anybody can look at over her shoulder and it is not paper and it is not nothing.*

She put the slate in the water at the sluice at about the fifth hour and watched it go under the hatch and stand on its edge in the eddy for about a minute and then go.

---

The seventh door from the bridge is the one that went wrong, and it went wrong because of the leash, exactly as she had told him at about nine o'clock outside a duck's-egg door in a real town, and she had told him so and he had written it down and neither of them had pretended it was a formality.

He came up the lane from the water side at about the fourth hour of the afternoon with a barrow and a sheet of paper on a board, and stopped four streets off, and said:

“I am going to ask you to take the ninth door.”

“No,” Tamsin Rook said.

“It is on your way. It is a hundred and forty yards from where you are standing. It is the one I have not been to and I have been to eight others and I cannot get to a ninth because I have got a barrow and a frame in it and I cannot leave the barrow. That is all. It is one hundred and forty yards and the same three questions and my name on them and not yours.”

“No,” Tamsin Rook said, “and I am not going to give you the reason in a lane twice, and you asked me this morning and I said no, and it is not a no about a lane. It is a no about your name.”

“It is your question as much as mine. You put nine doors this morning and you have been saying the same three words at every one of them and there is no door on this river where the words are not the district's words any more, they are words you learned in a market court in a town four days behind us, and you have made them yours by saying them nine times, and I have made them mine by writing them in my own hand on a board at nine o'clock, and there is nothing of yours in them and nothing of mine in them and they belong to the person who asks them.”

“Then I will go myself in the morning.”

“You will go yourself in the morning and you will take the barrow and put it at the head of the lane and you will not take the frame in, and you will say the three questions in the same words I said them, and you will not say one word of what I said at the eight before it, because if I am at a door and you are at a door there is one door in this mile that has got a courier and a bellmaker on it, and that door is the door somebody will remember in nine years.” Tamsin Rook shifted her bag. “And I am not asking you to take the ninth door. That is what the rule is. The rule is not that I do not help you. The rule is that I am not the reason your question is at a door. **A man who cannot get to a door alone will find a woman, and a woman with a road is always available, and that is how one person’s answer ends up in a document with two names on it and one keeper in the box.** I have been the second name on a piece of paper four times in eleven years and one of them was about a child and I have never got it back.”

“I am not asking twice,” Tomas Vale said.

“I know you are not. That is what a leash is and it is four lines on a slate in a woman's hand in a town you cannot get back to in a week,” Tamsin Rook said. “Write it down that I said no at the seventh door on the forty-third day, at about the fourth hour of the afternoon, and that you did not ask again, and that neither of us said one word of the other’s.”

“I will write it down.”

“Good. Now go away about forty yards and let me finish this one, because the man in there is watching you and he has decided about you and not about me, and I have about four minutes left of his wife in the doorway and I am not going to spend them explaining you.”

He went away forty yards and stood at the head of the lane with a barrow, and the frame stayed in the cradle and the four bolts were made fast and nothing was rung, and at about the sixth hour he went away up the road and left the barrow in a field against a wall with the cradle down and the bolts made fast, and he wrote the hour and the place, and he wrote that he had asked her to take a door and been refused and had not asked again, and under it he wrote the thing he actually thought, which was: *She was right and I knew she would be and I asked her anyway, and the reason I asked her anyway is that I did not want to go to a door by myself, and that is my whole flaw and it has taken me four counties to find out that it is the reason the market lane happened and it is the reason the archive burned and it is the reason I could not get out of a room in Kells without somebody to hand a page to, and I have asked a woman to take a door off me because I was frightened of a door.*

---

The seventh door is where the man is.

He is a form-writer, and he has been a form-writer for long enough that the woman at the door called him *the writer* and not by anything else, and he was standing in the lane with a satchel on his hip and a fold of paper in his hand about two inches deep, and he was about to go down the lane, and Tamsin Rook asked the first question and he answered it.

“No,” he said.

She asked the second.

“No,” he said, and then, before she could ask the third, he said, in an entirely different voice: “You have been round the eight.”

“I have.”

“My name is Hesk Rann,” he said, “and I am the one who fills them in, and I have been on this river for about nine years, and I do not get paid, and I will take your no and I will not write it, and I am going to tell you something and then I am going to go down to the bridge, and I would like it noticed that I am telling you and not writing it.”

“All right.”

“There is a man two counties over,” Hesk Rann said, “who signed a thing for his family before he went away, six years back, in a room at a table, because his sister-in-law asked him to and he was the only one of the six who could write. And he has been signing that form every year since and he has not been in the house once, and he does not know he is signing, because I have taken it to him twice a year since the year before the first one and he has never once asked me what it was for.”

Hesk Rann put the fold of paper down on the gate.

“He asked me on the second year,” he said. “He said, *does this want me to say something* — and I said no, it does not want anything from you, it wants the answer you gave last year, and there is a line to confirm it on and I will put you on it. And he wrote his name and the date and gave it me back. And that is the whole of how it has happened and it has happened about six times and he has never once asked me to do it twice and he has never once asked me to stop and he cannot remember agreeing to any of it, and I know that because the last time I saw him I asked him what the paper was and he said *a thing about a gate*.”

Somebody inside the house was standing at the second window and neither of them looked at it.

“Now the rest of it,” Hesk Rann said, “and then I am going. There is a woman four doors up this lane who cannot write and there are two more between here and the bridge and there are about forty in a year between here and the lower water, and I go to them, and they tell me what the house says, and I put it down. And I have been asked by nobody. Not once, in nine years, has anybody said to me *would you put your name to this as the house*. I put it down because the form has one line for a name and I am the one in the room, and I have signed about nine hundred of them, and every one of them is a true answer from a true household, and every one of them is in a form that says *the answer of this house*, and I have never in my life lied to anybody about anything, and I want that written down in whatever you write it in, because I am about to be the man in your nine doors and I would like there to be one true sentence about me in a book before the rest of it starts.”

“Why are you telling me?”

“Because you have been round eight doors with three questions and you have not once told me a story,” Hesk Rann said, “and because in about four days a man is going to come up this road who has got a Crown bell in a cradle, and I have heard about that, and I have a family, and I would like there to be a record of this conversation with a date on it in case anybody comes looking for a reason.”

He picked the fold of paper up off the gate.

“I am not going to sign your no,” he said. “You can write *refused* in your own hand and I will witness it with the hour and go down to the bridge. And I am not going to be one of your eight hands, and I would rather be one, and I am telling you that I would rather be one, and I am not going to ask you to write that down either.”

“I am not writing down anything you do not ask me to,” Tamsin Rook said. “That is not mine to decide. That is a rule and I did not write it and I cannot break it.”

“Then write down that I asked you to write it down and you did not.”

She wrote that down.

---

She got the hand at about the sixth hour, sitting on the parapet at the low drain gate with a slate she had fished out of the eddy above the sluice four hours earlier and dried.

Hesk Rann had not written his refusal, and she had asked him for nothing, and she had spent an hour on it and got it in the correct way, which is that she asked a question whose answer is a hand.

*“You write for the trade as well,” she said. “Do you.”*

And he had said, “I write for anybody who cannot write. It is not a trade, it is a favour, and I am bad at refusing it.”

*“You wrote a bill for a smith past the county line.”*

And he had said, “I wrote a bill for Amos Trewin’s wife in the spring, because he asked me and he was in a state about it, and I have not been in that shop nine months.”

*“Have you got it.”*

And he had said, “He keeps his bills. He has to. It is a legal paper. It is in a bundle at the back of a book and it is the only thing in that shop I have ever been paid for, about nine and six for a day’s writing in the spring and eleven in the autumn.”

He wrote his refusal for her on the back of a form out of his own satchel, and he wrote the hour beside it, and he wrote it in the same hand he uses for a household and the same hand he uses for a tradesman's bill, and the descender of the *g* hesitates, and the downstrokes lean forward, and there is a pause before the *g* that is in every one of them.

Not a copy. Not a traced thing. Nine hundred households and a smith's bill and a form at a door in a lane two days back, and the same hesitation in all of them.

And the form at the household of six — the current year's, the one that was on the table with nothing in the box but a name, the careful hand that was not one of the six — is the same hand.

**The same hand is in three documents in three counties and in two kinds of paper, and the two kinds of paper are a Crown form and a tradesman's bill, and the two are not the same kind of document, and there is no law anywhere in this kingdom that says a man may not be the hand on both.**

She wrote the six words she has written in her own book about a hand before, because it is a courier's shorthand and a courier is the only person who will read it: *forward slope, hesitating descender, same pause before g.* And under it she wrote: *nine hundred houses and one bill. He is not stealing forms. He is a man who can write and there is no one else.*

---

At about the fourth hour of the afternoon she was at the ninth door and Tomas Vale was at the tenth, four streets off, with a barrow he had left in a field, and he had one door in an hour and she had nine in a day, and at about the seventh hour of the evening she walked up the road to where the barrow was and he was sitting on the shaft with a board on his knee and one line on the board, and she said:

“How many.”

“One,” said Tomas Vale. “One door and one refusal and the man read it upside down like yours, and he kept it, and I did not get a hand out of him, and I sat on a wall afterwards for about an hour feeling like a man who has come to a river with a bell in a cradle and has learned nothing.”

“Nine,” said Tamsin Rook. “Eight refusals in eight hands and one answer in a ninth and one hand that is on a Crown form in a lane two days back and on a smith's bill past the county line and on nine hundred doors along this water.”

“That is the same hand?”

“It is the same hand,” Tamsin Rook said, “and the man who has it is called Hesk Rann, and he is a form-writer, and he fills in Crown forms for people who cannot write, and he does it for nothing, and he has never been asked once, and he cannot remember agreeing to it, and he told me all of that at a gate in about four minutes because I asked him nine questions and never told him a story.”

Tomas Vale looked at the board on his knee with one line on it for a while.

“Then it is not a conspiracy,” he said. “It is a man.”

“It is a man and about nine hundred doors,” Tamsin Rook said, “and the man is the door, and the doors are a habit, and the habit is the thing, and that is exactly what you wrote in your book on the night of the fortieth and you did not believe it until a man with a satchel told you in a lane at six o'clock in the evening.” She shouldered her bag. “And there is one more thing and it is the thing you will not like, and I have had it since the seventh door and I have been carrying it down this road for three hours.”

“Say it.”

“He has been to that family in a lane two days back. Twice a year. For six years.” Tamsin Rook said it without any weight at all. “Mr Vale, that household has had six certified answers in a drawer and every one of them has a different date in the same hand on it, and they told you the man has not been in that house for six years, and they were not lying, and it is all true at once, and I have not got a way to hold a thing being all true at once, and I have had three hours on a parapet.”

The towpath ran south under them and the water went down to the lower river and the lower river went on for a long way through four counties, and somewhere at the end of it, downstream, where the water is wide and there is a market and a clerk's hall with a great many books in it, there was a town called Miremarket, and it was four days away, and there was a boat on the tide at about eleven miles.

“Downstream,” Tamsin Rook said, and stopped, and waited to see whether he was going to say it or whether she was going to have to.

He was quiet for the length of a cart going past, which is long for a man who has two questions in a book.
