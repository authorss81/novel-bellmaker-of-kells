# Chapter 0062 — The Fifth Panel On Water

The river does not have a shoulder and it does not have a lane and it does not have a place a man can stand out of sight in, and Tamsin Rook worked that out on the ebb of the forty-sixth, in about an hour, sitting on the boards with her back against a coil of rope with the bell in her bag and the bag between her boots.

She had known it before they got on. That is the thing about knowing a thing in advance: it does not stop it arriving.

What arrived, in the first hour, was that there were nine people on a boat of about ninety feet and every one of them had looked at the frame in the cradle in the cradle's place on the boards, and not one of them had asked, and that the not-asking was the loudest thing about the boat.

She had been looked at four times in eleven days by people who then said nothing to her about what they had looked at. She had been looked at about nine times since the fourth hour of the afternoon of the forty-fifth and she had counted them, because counting is what she does with anything she cannot do anything else about.

---

She asked three people before the light went entirely, in the same words, at the same hour, and did not improve them for anybody.

The words were: *Is there a map of this water. Not a road sheet. A map of the reach, with the landings and the stopping places and where the water will not let you.*

The first was a man going down to a market with forty feet of line and a basket and he said no, and said it kindly, and added that a man with forty feet of line does not need a map, he needs a woman who has been down it, and that was the whole of his trade and it is a map that cannot be printed and does not go out of date, and he had got about eleven years of it in his head and it had been wrong twice.

The second was a woman with a crate of fowls and she said the reach has a book and it is not a printed thing, and it is kept at the head of it, and it has a day and a thing and whose and an hour in it, and she said the name of the woman who keeps it without being asked, because everybody on that water knows it, the way everybody on a road knows the name of the woman at the second cottage.

The third was a boy at the tiller with a hand on it that was older than the rest of him, and he said, without being asked either, that the reach was printed, and that there was a sheet for it, and that it was in the sheet-house at the town they were going to and it cost a farthing, and that it was not any use because the water in it was four years old and the upper reach had changed.

---

That is the finding and it took her about nine minutes to get it right, and she got it right on her own, sitting on the boards with the coil against her back, and it is the first finding of this batch that nobody handed her:

**On water, the routes are not roads. On water the routes are tides, and landings, and stopping places, and a place a boat can lie all night with a bank behind it. And a return does not go up this water to an office at all. A return goes up a bank to a building, and a person who has never been carried in a boat does not know that there is any difference, and I have not known it until about the second hour of this morning.**

---

The sheet was on the thwart all morning of the forty-seventh and it belonged to a man called **Marek Osgood** and he had bought it eleven years before in a town two hundred miles up this water because he had a shilling and there was a board in the inn, and he was going down to a town where his daughter was, and he had carried it ever since.

He had it out because it had got wet in a squall in the night and he was drying it on the boards against the sun, and he had it open face up and it was the front of the sheet that was up, which is what a man dries.

He was opposite her at a folding table and had been opposite her at a folding table since about the seventh hour of the previous evening, and he was a man who had a way of not talking that she recognized at about a minute because she had one herself.

At about the ninth hour she turned her head and looked at the sheet from where she was sitting, across the table, upside down, at about four feet, without standing up and without asking to be shown it.

She read it in about two minutes. It is what a courier reads. Wear is random and it does not care what a thing is for. The thumb corner, the white creases, the fold across the coaching times.

And the rectangle.

In the fifth panel on the back — and the back was down against the boards, and she was reading it through the paper, upside down, and the sun was behind it and the light came through the cheaper stock in exactly the way that the light always comes through the cheaper stock, and it is why carriers dry road sheets with the printed side against the boards and not the other way — the fifth panel had four rules in it and a printer's name and the head of two columns.

It had no column rules.

The other four panels had them. The fourth panel, which was the next one along and which she had read at a counter in a coaching inn six days ago and said out loud *that is a good panel* to a man who was looking at the tide table, had three columns and three column rules and the office name and the day it was received and the day it went out.

The fifth panel had the office column and the header and the print and no rules at all, and the paper in that rectangle was smooth where the other paper was not, and the ink was a slightly different black, and the tear at its left edge was the tear of a sheet that had been handled and not of a sheet that had been worn.

She did not ask to be shown it. She did not ask him to turn it over. She did not lean forward. She sat with her hands in her lap and read four feet of paper upside down through the light and then she looked at the water for about two minutes.

Marek Osgood dried his sheet for a while and then said, to the water:

“You have been reading my sheet for about four minutes and you have not asked me for it.”

“No.”

“That is a strange thing to do with a stranger.”

“It is the opposite,” Tamsin Rook said. “It is what you do with a stranger. If I had asked, you would have had to decide something about me in about four seconds, and a person deciding about a stranger at four seconds decides about the stranger and not about the sheet, and then I would have got whichever of the two I happened to be unlucky about, and I wanted the sheet.”

“You have wanted the sheet for a week?”

“I have wanted the sheet for six days. I have had one and I have had a look at it and I did not know what I was looking at for about two hours.”

“All right,” said Marek Osgood. “I am going to say a thing and it is not a question, and if it is a question you may tell me it is one and I will go on being dry.”

“All right.”

“You are the courier,” he said. “The one with the bag and the bell in it, and the one that the ferry-man on the stage would not carry on his road, and everybody on this boat has worked that out by the second hour and nobody has worked out the other thing, which is that you have been the one asking the questions for six days and nobody on this boat has been told your name, and there is not one of us going to be told it, and I have been on this water since the fourth hour this morning thinking about that and about how a person runs six days of their life like that.”

Tamsin Rook put her hands flat on her knees.

“Say your part,” she said.

“There is a woman in this town I am going down to who has a girl in my daughter’s school and I have not seen her in nine years, and if I come down that landing with a courier on the boat who is not anybody’s servant, I am a man who has picked up a story on the river, and I have picked up stories on the river for eleven years and they are all about somebody else.” He turned the sheet over and dried the back. “So I am not going to ask you anything and I am not going to be asked anything, and I would like to have said it out loud in front of you and not in front of nine people.”

“That is fair and I am not going to argue with it.”

“I know you are not. That is not why I said it.”

---

**And here is the part of this day that she got wrong, and she wrote it down that evening in her own book with the hour against it, and she did not improve it, and the sentence is the sentence:**

*About the eleventh hour I decided that I was going to be credited. That is the whole of it. I have spent six days being careful with paper in public and being right about it and nobody has written my name against one single thing I have found, and being right and not being credited turns out after six days into exactly the same thing as being wrong and not being credited, and I did not want to write that in anybody's book, so I made a decision about it in about nine seconds, on a boat, and the decision was that I would put my name to what I had found.*

*And I did, in a book of my own, at the top of the page, in my own hand, with the hour: Tamsin Rook, courier, of no office and no district, at the fourth hour and the fifth hour of this day, upon the water.*

*And it was the wrong decision and I made it knowingly and I would make it again, and I have written the reason down here so that I have to look at it.*

*The moment my name was on a page about a road sheet, about eleven people on this boat knew what I was. Not knew me. Knew what I was. A courier's business is contracts and badges and who owes a fare and a person with a name on a page about a sheet is a person who works, and it took about an hour and I watched it go round the boat, and what went round the boat was not a woman who cannot hear a bell.*

*Two of the nine told me things they had not told anybody. Seven of the nine stopped saying a sentence halfway.*

*A paper with a name on it is a paper a person can be refused. That is what I have been saying to nine doors for six days and it goes both ways and I had not turned it round, and I have turned it round, and I have had six days of it the other way and I would not go back.*

*And the thing I am not going to do is burn it. It is my own book, my own hand, my own name, and the word* courier *is the only word on that page that is true, and I put it there.*

---

The boat put in at a landing at about the second hour of the afternoon of the forty-seventh for water, and there was a shop with a board nailed up outside it, and the board had eleven road sheets on it and a print of a hare and the words *by the sheet or the four*, and the sheets were a farthing.

She bought one.

It was a different year. It was a different shop's edition and it was printed in a town sixty miles off by a printer who put his own name at the foot instead of a Crown distribution mark, and the paper was better and the ink was better and the coaching times had been brought up to date and the tide table in the bottom corner had four more entries in it than the one in Marek Osgood's hand.

The fifth panel was gone.

Not faded. Not blank. Gone in the same rectangle, in the middle of the run, with the header and the office column and the printer's name and no column rules and paper that had not been near a week of anybody's hands.

She held it up next to Marek Osgood's across the thwart, and the two rectangles were the same rectangle, and it was the same cut, and the print around them was four years apart in its line rules and nine years in the tide table, and both of them were made from one plate.

“Ah,” said Marek Osgood.

“Yes,” said Tamsin Rook.

---

She worked the rest of it out on the boards between that landing and the next one, and it is in her book, and it is eight lines, and it is the whole of what the fifth panel is:

*The cut is in the plate.*

*Not in a copy. Not in an edition. In the plate, with a knife, once, and every sheet printed from that plate since has come out of the press with the same rectangle missing in it, and there are about eleven editions of this sheet in this country and I have seen two and they are four years apart in type and both have it.*

*And a thing that is cut out of a copy has a date. Somebody took a knife to that particular sheet on that particular day, and the paper and the ink and the wear of the edge would give it up in a year to anybody who wanted it.*

*A thing that is cut out of a plate has no date.*

*That is the whole of it and it is the worst thing I have found in a month. There is no day on this that anybody did it. There is a hand, and a knife, and a plate, and then eleven years of it, and it is not an edition and it is not a fire and there is no hour anywhere in the world at which anybody did this, and that means it can be asked about, and there is no answer, and it will be like that in a hundred years and I have to write that down while I still have it.*

---

At about the sixth hour of the evening of the forty-seventh she gave the slate away.

She had fished it out of the eddy above the sluice four days ago and dried it against a wall and had put nine doors on it and the hour and the door and the word *no* and the fact that a man had read it upside down and had not agreed with it, and there was nothing on it that belonged to anybody but herself, and that was the argument for giving it away and not an argument against.

She wrote on it in her own hand, in chalk, standing on the boards, in front of nine people, and then she turned it round and gave it to Marek Osgood and did not ask for it back.

*This is a description of a part of a road sheet that is not on it. I read it upside down at four feet through the paper this morning and I did not ask to be shown it and I am not going to tell you how I know what I know. I am going to tell you where it came from because you have been dry in front of me for two days and a man who has been dry in front of a person for two days has been given a thing he did not ask for and does not know what to do with.*

*In the fifth panel on the back of any sheet printed from that plate, in the same rectangle in every copy, the two column rules are not there. The other four panels have them. The paper in that rectangle is smooth. The ink is a different black. The edge of it is a cut and not a tear.*

*It was done to the plate. It is not an edition and it is not a fire. There is no date on it anywhere in the world, and that is not a comfort, and I want it on a slate in somebody's hands and not only in mine, because a courier carries a slate on her belt and anybody can look at it over her shoulder, and a slate in a bag is a slate nobody checks, and a slate in another person's hands on a boat going down a river is a page that has to be accounted for to two people instead of one.*

*You may throw it in. It is a slate.*

Marek Osgood read it twice and put it inside his coat against his chest and said, “That is the first thing anybody has handed me on this river that was not about money.”

“It is a slate,” said Tamsin Rook.

“It is a slate and you are not going to get it back.”

“No,” she said. “I am not going to get it back and I am not going to ask you for it and I have written that in my own book with the hour, and I want to say the reason out loud because I have made two mistakes in one day and I would like one of them to be on the page: **a thing in one pair of hands is a thing that can be lost in a way nobody has to account for, and I have been carrying one in a bag for eleven days and I did not know that was a weakness until about an hour ago.**”

---

And then the water opened out and there was a town on it, and it took Tomas Vale and herself and a barrow and a frame in a cradle in its own line of sight all the way down to the stage, and there was no lane and no shoulder and no door, and it had been four days' worth of a reach and they had done one and a half of it and the plate was cut and the slate was gone and the bell was in the bag and it was not rung.

The stage at Helling is a stone stage with two sets of steps and a crane and a tally board, and it is a market town of about nine hundred, and there is a market cross at the top of it and there is a Crown district renewal office in it with an address on the door, and there is a notice nailed by the door, and the notice is about the receiving and the going out of returns, and the notice has a number on it and the number is the number of a desk.

Tamsin Rook read the notice from the boards, at about the seventh hour of the evening of the forty-seventh, with a man who cannot hear her standing four feet off her and looking at the same notice upside down, and neither of them asked the other anything.

“Mr Vale,” she said.

“I have read it.”

“That is a building with an address,” said Tamsin Rook. “And a building with an address has papers in it, and papers have names on them, and there are four of us in this boat and one of us is going to have to go in there in the morning with four questions in the same words, and it is not going to be me, and I have known that since about the fourth hour of the afternoon of the thirty-eighth, and you may write it down with the hour if you like and I will not correct you.”

She shouldered her bag and went up the steps, and the bell was in it, and nothing was rung, and nobody asked her for a reason.
