# Chapter 0055 — A Bell Somebody Else Made

The smithy is a long stone shop with two doors and a yard behind it and a hundred and forty feet of frontage with a sign board on it that has been repainted about six times and never in the same colour twice, and the sign says *AMOS TREWIN — SMITH* and under it, in letters half the size, *BELLMETAL, FITTINGS, WIRE, AND HONEST WORK.*

The forges were going at about the seventh hour and there were three men in the shop and a fourth at the anvil who was not the smith, and the smith was a lean man with a leather apron and a burn on the inside of his left wrist about the size of a coin.

He looked at the barrow come into the yard and at the frame in the cradle on it and did not stop working for about forty seconds, which Tomas Vale understood perfectly, because he had watched people look at a Crown carrier in a cradle for a month and forty seconds was a good result.

Then he put the hammer down and came out.

“I have two things to say before you say anything,” Amos Trewin said. “The first is that I am not a master of the Bellwrights' Measure and I have never been in that house and I cannot certify anything at all, not a bell, not a staple, not a hinge, and if you have come to me to have something certified you have come a long way to be disappointed and I would rather you had it now.”

“I have not come to have anything certified.”

“The second,” Amos Trewin said, “is that I know what is in that frame, because there is a notice about it in this county and everybody in this county has read it by now, and I want you to hear me say the rest of it before you get off the barrow, because if you get off the barrow I will be talking to a man and I would rather be talking to a man standing where I put him.”

Tomas Vale did not get off the barrow.

“All right.”

“A bell that cannot be traced is not a bell of unknown origin,” Amos Trewin said. “That is a phrase that gets people into trouble and it is not mine, I heard it at a gate at a market in this county about a month ago with about nine people standing in the rain, and it has been in me ever since and I have not been able to get it out and I have not been able to get it checked, because the man who said it was in a lane with a barrow and a lockbox and a bell and I did not get his name.”

“Sabin Fell,” Tomas Vale said.

“Then I have his name and I have said it and I have not got it from you,” Amos Trewin said, “and I would like that to be the arrangement, because I have a shop and a book and eleven customers who cannot write and I am not going to be a man somebody says a thing to and then remembers it.”

He turned his head and looked at the barrow again.

“There is a bell in that frame and it is not yours,” he said, “and the reason I know is the signboard. Four months ago a man from the north came down this road with a book of paper and a boy with a ladder and they nailed a notice on my board about a Crown instrument being carried through this county, and they nailed it on the good part of the board, and I have not taken it down and I am not going to, and it says the thing may be produced at the request of any person, and I have read it about forty times, and there is not one line in it that stops me selling you metal.”

“There is a bar on it,” Tomas Vale said. “From my trade. And the bar is on the bell and not on me, and it is not to be rung in a public place in this county, and this is the next county, and I have come over that line on a barrow with a barrow wheel and a bell in it and no clapper in the bell, and I am telling you all of that before you ask, because you are entitled to know what you are talking to before you decide.”

“Why did you come over the line?”

“Because the bar stops at the county stone and the bar is on the bell and not on me,” Tomas Vale said, “and because I have no metal.”

---

He stood at the anvil for a moment with his hand flat on it and then said, “That is not a reason to come to me. I would have been the wrong man for it. Half the smiths in this county would have sold you metal out of a hearth and never asked you a question about a bar, and you would have got it cheaper, and it would have been worse.”

“I know it would have been worse. That is the whole of the month.”

“Then get down,” Amos Trewin said. “Not the barrow. You can leave the barrow in the yard and I will put a man on the door and nobody touches that frame and nobody puts a clapper in it, because I have read the notice and I am a man who reads notices, and I will tell you the two things I do and the two things I will not do, and I am telling you both halves before you have asked me for either.”

“Go on.”

“I will not cast anything for you,” Amos Trewin said. “Not a handbell, not a wheel bell, not a strap, not a staple for a gate. I have not cast a bell in nineteen years and I am not going to start, and there is no fire in this shop that could do it, and I have said that to four Crown men and two guildsmen and one man who wanted a ship's bell and the answer does not change with the man.”

“And the two things you do do.”

“I sell metal, and I keep a book.” He said it as though it were one fact and not two. “That is the same thing and people do not believe it. I sell copper, I sell bellmetal, I sell wire, I sell iron. And every time I sell a thing I write down what it was, what it was made of, how much of it, what day, and who bought it. And that is not because I am a good man. It is because the third time in my life I sold somebody a load of metal and it was not what I said it was, and the man who bought it made a thing that failed in a child's hand, and I had to stand in a room and be told about it by a woman who did not raise her voice, and I have kept a book every day since for a reason I have never told anybody until about eleven minutes ago.”

“Eleven minutes ago?”

“You have been on my barrow since the seventh hour,” Amos Trewin said, “and I have been waiting to see whether you were going to ask me the question or ask me for the thing. You have asked me the question twice and I have not got a customer at seven in the morning who does that.”

---

He was allowed to read it, and that was the whole of the day.

The book is not large. It is a ruled book with a column head for each thing and it is the same book that has been running since the shop had a different name over the door, and it goes back a very long way, and Amos Trewin turned it to about nine years back and then left it open on the anvil bench with the page spread and the two of them standing over it for the rest of the day.

It is a record of a sale. It is not a record of a person. That distinction is the first thing a courier understands and the last thing an office understands, and it is nine columns and it is this:

*The day. The thing. What it was made of. The weight. The price to me. The price out. The buyer. Where it was to. And a ninth column, which is the only one that is not about money, and which Amos Trewin calls the where-from, and in which he writes the name of the place the metal came out of and the date he had it and the name of the man he had it from.*

The ninth column is why the book is a book.

A man may buy copper from four sources in this county. He may buy it from a Crown dealer, out of a district store, on a Crown document with a date and a place of purchase on it, and a man may keep that document and show it. He may buy it from another smith, and the other smith's book is the record, and it is one shop vouching for another. He may buy it from a chandler who sells odds out of a locked hut, and there is no record and there never was. Or he may buy it out of a hearth, off a man who found it or melted it himself, and there is nothing whatever, and there never was, and it is not a crime, and it is the ordinary way most bellmetal in this country has ever been bought.

**A bell is traceable when the metal of it can be shown to have come from a source that keeps a record of what it sold and to whom.**

Tomas Vale stood over a ruled book in a stone shop for about five hours and read a sentence out loud three times, and the third time Amos Trewin stopped what he was doing.

“Who said that.”

“A man called Sabin Fell said a longer version of it in a room over a fish market in Kells about a month ago, in front of about eleven people and a clerk with a wax tablet, and he was called the master of the guild, and he was sixty-one, and he was angry with me at the time and he is entitled to be, and he has never heard of you and he has never read this book and you have never read his.” Tomas Vale put his finger on the ninth column without touching it. “And he said it to a room of his own trade, because I was in the room and I had made a bell I could not trace, and he is right and I have spent a month being right about it in the only way I know how, which is saying it in a lane.”

Amos Trewin looked at the book, and then at the yard, and then at the bell in the cradle, and he said the thing that turned the afternoon.

“Say that again with a word changed,” he said. “Say *the man who sold the metal* instead of *the source that keeps a record*, and then say the whole thing, and then tell me what a man does on an ordinary working day who has to be able to show where his metal came from.”

Tomas Vale said it again.

“Then he asks his supplier, and the supplier shows him the ninth column, and the supplier’s supplier shows him his, and there is a chain of shops going back as far as anybody can remember, and the chain is made of men who wrote a day down.” Amos Trewin put his hand on the open page. “That is the whole trade. That is all of it. There is no other thing a bellmaker can do about it and nobody has ever put it in a book, because everybody who has ever written about it has wanted a law and the law is not needed, you need the ninth column.”

He shut the book, which he had not been asked to do, and opened it again, which he had not been asked to do either.

“No,” he said.

“I have not asked you for anything.”

“You have been asking me for about five hours,” Amos Trewin said, “and you have been doing it the way a man does it when he is too well brought up to say it. You want the page. You want the page of the book with the households on it — the people who buy, and where the metal goes, nine years of it, and you would like to buy that page off me, and you have been standing at my anvil for five hours not asking because you have not found the right way to ask, and I have watched you not ask eleven times.”

“All right,” Tomas Vale said. “I would like to buy a page of the book. The page with the households on it. Nine years of who bought metal from you and where it went. And I will pay you the ordinary price for a page of a book, and I will give you a receipt for it in my own hand, and I will not copy it and give it away without saying so.”

“No.”

“That is a complete answer and I am not going to argue with it.”

“I am not refusing you because you are a Crown man in a barrow,” Amos Trewin said, “because you are not one, and I said so to a man from the district in the spring. I am refusing you because the ninth column and the eighth column together make a list of houses. A list of houses that have bought metal is a list of houses, and it is a good list, and it is the best list in this county, and I have never sold it and I am not going to sell it to a stranger on a road. There is nothing sinister in my not selling it to you. There is a hundred and forty feet of frontage here and forty people in this village and nine years of my handwriting in it, and I am the only copy, and the day that book leaves this shop there is no second one, and you cannot make a second one out of a shop that has been here for a hundred and forty years.”

“That is the answer I would have given too, about a bench-book,” Tomas Vale said, “and I gave a page of one away in a yard in front of a hundred and forty people about two weeks ago and I have thought about it every day since, so I am not going to tell a man I understand the cost of it, because I do not understand the cost of it.”

“You gave a page away?”

“I tore a leaf out of my own shop’s running book in my own yard in daylight and gave it to a man who is on no list, because a Crown officer’s armful came down on top of it,” Tomas Vale said, “and I have not regretted it and I would do it again and I would also do it differently, and I have not worked out how, and I have had a month.”

Amos Trewin thought about that for about as long as it took him to put a shoe back on the anvil.

“Then come back tomorrow morning,” he said, “and bring me a reason in writing, and I will read it, and I will not promise you the page, and I will tell you now that you have about one chance in four of it, and the reason is not about the law and not about you and it is that I have never read a piece of paper in my life that was not a bill.”

---

They did not get an inch further than that in the rest of the day, and the price was the last thing.

Tomas Vale asked, on the way out, what the metal was a penny — a hundredweight of good bellmetal, out of a Crown store with a Crown document against it, and Amos Trewin told him, and told him without any particular expression, and it was a great deal more than Tomas Vale had been used to paying.

“Eleven and six,” Amos Trewin said. “And that is a fair price and I make about eleven on it and that is the whole of the trade, and I will tell you why it is eleven and six and not four and six for a load out of a hearth: because I can show you the ninth column, and the man with the hearth cannot show you anything, and the difference between us is not the metal and I want that written on the board.”

“You cannot sell me a Crown-dealer load. I have no Crown document with a date and a place of purchase on it, and I could not show a district where it came from, and if I could I should not, because my shop is shut and my stock is gone.”

“I know why your shop is shut,” Amos Trewin said, “and I have read about that too, and I am going to tell you the one thing about a Crown dealer that nobody tells you. Everyone tells you a Crown dealer is expensive. A Crown dealer is **available.** A Crown store holds metal for anybody with a document, and it does not care what you made, and it does not ask, and a man who wants a bell and has a Crown document can have metal in a week, and that is why there is a Crown document.”

“Then I would be able to cast a bell on the first of next month.”

“You would be able to buy metal on the first of next month, from a Crown store, on a Crown document, in this county, where there is a bar on a bell in a frame in a yard,” Amos Trewin said. “And you may notice, because I notice things for a living and it is the only trade I have, that a bar on a bell in this county is a bar on a bell in this county, and that nobody in this county is going to ask a Crown store what the metal is for, because the Crown store is not a bellwright and has never in its life been asked about a bell.”

He said it without any weight and went and shut the other door against the weather.

---

The last hour of the day, about the seventh hour of the evening, Tomas Vale wrote up what he had bought, and there are four lines of it in his book and one of them is the price and one of them is the county line and one of them is a method and the fourth is this:

*Nothing. I have bought nothing. There is no metal in this barrow and there is no metal in my hands and the half-barrel in Kells has been empty since the nineteenth, and I have a supplier and a price and a county stone and a ninth column, and that is everything I have, and I have not cast anything and I am not going to cast anything on this road, and I would not have wanted to.*

Then, underneath, in the same hand and the same hour, and this is the second half of the fortieth day in the day-book of a bellmaker of Kells:

*What he said is the second time. The first was Sabin Fell, in a room, in front of his own trade, reading a tablet, and angry. The second was a smith past a county stone, at his own anvil, in his own shop, with a book he has kept for nineteen years because a woman once told him in a room what his metal did. Neither of them knows the other exists. Neither of them is talking to me. Both of them said the same sentence about the same column, and Sabin Fell has never seen a sheet of that book and Amos Trewin has never heard of Sabin Fell, and I am the only man in this kingdom who has heard both of them say it, and I have no right in either trade, and I am going to be able to use it four counties from here and nowhere nearer than here.*

And at the top of the page, before either of those, because he had found it and had not been able to leave it out:

*A loose leaf, between the pages for the year nine years back and the year after it, folded in four, not pasted, not sewn, and it fell out when the shop moved the book and not before, which means it went in between two days of trading and not all at once. A narrow strip of the same paper as a Crown form. Two lines copied out in a copying hand, carefully, the way a person copies a thing they have read off somebody else's shoulder:*

*Line — . Persons — . Response — . Returned — .*

*And under it five words in the same hand, and the five words are:* I have not taken this.

*There is a wax mark at the fold about the size of a thumbnail, and it is copying wax, and my sister has used copying wax in the corner of every letter she has ever left me since she was twenty-one, and I have seen it on four of them, and I have had a workshop and an archive and a mother who is gone and a trade I have lost in a month, and I am thirty-two, and I got that number out of a man who was angry with me when he said it, and he said it in a room, and it is the only time in my life anybody has said it.*

*And I cannot tell whether that is her hand or a copyist's, and I am not going to put the leaf in the day-book, and I am not going to burn it, and I am not going to give it to Tamsin Rook this evening.*

*Why not, and I am writing the reason down because she is the only person on this road who would be told about it if I do not tell her, and it is this, and it is not a good reason and it is the reason: because if I hand a woman I have known a month a piece of paper I have found and cannot place, then the first thing I will have done on this road is put a page of somebody's in her hands, and the last time I did that it was my own and I chose who got it, and I have been thinking about that for a month and I have not worked out whether I chose well.*

*And she has a handbell whose note she has not been able to hear since the fourth day of the month, and a road she is not obliged to walk with me, and a reason of her own that has nothing to do with either of us, and she does not need my sister's wax.*

He put the leaf back where it had been, between the two pages, and wrote nothing else, and went out and harnessed the barrow, and the bolts were made fast at the place it was set down, and the frame never came out of the cradle, and nothing in this chapter was rung.
