# Chapter 0051 — The Book and the Roll

The towpath out of Kells runs north along the quiet side of the water and the barrow wheel had been in the mud of it to the axle by the sixth hour, and Tamsin Rook walked in front of the barrow with her shoulder against the shaft because that is where a person walks when they are not pulling and have decided not to be asked to.

The frame sat in its cradle on the boards and the cradle sat in the barrow and the four retaining bolts were made fast at the two places a barrow has for the purpose, and she had watched him do it, and she had counted, and she had not said anything about the counting.

Her own bag was on her hip with the bell in it and she had not taken it out.

Ahead of them the path came off the low turn and went out flat along a field that was somebody's second-best ground, and there was a man coming up it with a barrow of lime who had been coming up it every day for longer than she had been on this road, and he stopped, because a barrow with a bell-frame in it is a thing that gets stopped.

“Morning,” he said.

“Morning,” she said, and let the two of them go past him, and heard him start again behind them and not ask, which was the correct thing and which she had expected.

---

The first carrier she asked properly was a man with a handcart of shingle going up from the second landing, and she asked him on a stretch where there was nowhere for the two of them to stop, which was deliberate, because a man with a full handcart will answer the question you asked rather than the one he would have preferred.

“There is a difference between your book and a Crown roll,” she said. “I want to know the difference and I am asking you because you have a book.”

He put the cart's prop down. “You are the courier that Kells had.”

“I am a courier that Kells had,” she agreed. “And I am asking about paper.”

“All right. A carrier's book says what I carried, from where, to where, how many miles, and my name against it, and the day.” He said it the way a man says the four things he has been asked most. “That is a book of carriage. If I carried a sack of lime to a house and a sack of lime to a weir, that is two lines and two houses, and the book is right.”

“And what are you not allowed to write in it.”

He looked at her properly for the first time.

“The owner,” he said. “If a person sends a thing by me, the book does not have their name on it, and I could not put it on it if I wanted to, and there is a fine for putting it on it and the fine is not about the person, it is about the book. And I do not write what they paid. That is between them and whoever they paid. And I do not write what was inside, because I did not open it, and a man who writes what he did not open has written a rumour with his own name on it.”

“So a carrier's book knows where a thing went and not who decided it should go.”

“A carrier's book knows where a thing went,” the man said. “That is the whole trade. Eleven years on this road, and that is the whole trade, and if you want to know who decided, you want a different book, and there is a different book, and it is in a building, and you cannot have it.”

She thanked him and took the shingle-cart's prop out of the mud for him, which cost her nothing and which she did not offer to do.

---

The second was a woman with a bolt of cloth in a sling, going down, who slowed when she saw the frame and then did not slow further, which is a professional judgement and not rudeness.

“I am not going to tell you where a child went,” Tamsin said, before she could ask anything else. “I have not got one with me and I am not going to say whose. If you have not got anything to say about paper, I will let you go down.”

“You have not even said a child’s name.”

“No.”

The woman went four or five paces and then turned round, which is what carriers do, and came back.

“There is one thing and it is the only thing and it is not about children,” she said. “A clerk came down this road once. Not an officer — a clerk, with a satchel and a list, and a copyist behind him writing as he went. That was a while back. He did not stop at one house. He stopped at four, and at each one he read out a list of names, and the people at the doors did not know him, and he asked nobody anything. The copyist wrote. That is the part I have never been able to put anywhere. He asked nobody anything and the book filled up anyway.”

“How do you know the book filled up.”

“Because he came back down the road about nine days later with the same satchel and the same list, and he read out the same names at the same four doors, and at the third door a woman said *that is not us any more, we have been at the low end of the water since the flood*, and he said *thank you*, and the copyist scratched something out and wrote something, and he did not stop at the fifth door, because there was no fifth door.” She shifted the sling. “Carriers carry parcels. People are not parcels and always were not. A clerk with a list is not a carrier and he is not bound by the carrier's rule, and the carrier's rule is the only rule on this road that keeps a person out of a book.”

“Tell me what a carrier's rule is.”

“You do not carry a name and you do not carry a debt and you do not carry a price,” she said. “That is three. Everything else on this road is a man being sensible.”

She went down. Tamsin Rook stood on the towpath above the second landing and watched a bolt of cloth go away from her down the quiet side of the water, and then she went on up, because there was one more to ask and she wanted to ask it properly and she had about a mile of path to do it in.

---

The third was an old man with a dog and a barrow of nothing in particular, at a gate where the path goes up to a farm, and he was the one who told her the thing she wrote down.

“A clerk came down this road with a list and a copyist,” he said, before she had asked him anything at all, when he saw the frame. “They were let through. The carriers were not asked anything, and I will tell you why there is nothing to say about that, and it is because there is nothing to say about it. A carrier is asked about a parcel. Nobody has ever asked a carrier about a form. And when a clerk comes down a road with a list and a copyist and does not ask anybody anything, and the list fills up anyway, then the road has learned the one thing it is bad at, which is that a road full of people who will tell you things is a road you can get anything out of by not asking.”

Tamsin Rook put her bag down on the parapet.

“Which is what.”

“Which is that it did not need the road,” the old man said. “It needed the list. If you have got a list, you have already got the answer to a question you have not asked, and the asking is the part the road is good at, and it is the only part that costs anything, and it was left out.”

He took his dog by the collar and went up to the farm.

---

She said the reason out loud to herself before she said it to him, which is a thing she does and which she has done for eleven years, and it is the reason she can carry a bell.

“There is a boy called Jory Fenn,” she said. “He is eight. He went in the round that came before a Crown notice was put up in his ward, and nobody has him, and the only paper about him is a line on a schedule and a piece of a household list with the fourth line cut away out of it.”

Tomas Vale did not look up from the shaft. She had said it to his shoulder and he had heard all of it, and he had not asked, and she had watched him not ask, and she went on.

“That is the reason and not him,” she said. “It is not him. I would like that in whatever you are writing in, in the plainest words either of us can manage: it is not him and it is not his bell and it is not a warning he can use. It is a boy of eight who went off this kind of road with a list, and a trade that has never been able to find out where a list goes, and a woman of sixteen in a town four miles behind us who has a piece of paper with a line cut out of it, and none of those three can go and ask.”

“You could go and ask.”

“I could go and ask Kells,” she said. “I have been on this road eleven years and a person who carries for a living is a person everybody tells things to, and I have spent all of it learning that the only road in this kingdom nobody writes on is the one everybody actually walks, and there is no list of it anywhere, and if I stop walking it and start asking, then I am a woman in a room with a slate, and I have watched a woman with a slate do this for a month and I have never once seen her be thanked for it.”

She shouldered her bag.

“A name that is on a roll in a building four counties from here is on a road somewhere,” she said, “and the road is the only place anybody looks, and looking is what I am for.”

---

At the top of a short rise where the path divides, the left fork went up the steps to Ocklebridge and the right fork went on flat to a ferry two miles below it, and the right fork was hers and she had walked it eleven times a year and it was the one she had walked this morning before he caught up with her at the washing stones, and she was going to take it at the rise, and she said so.

He put the barrow's prop down.

“Can I ask you a thing about your road.”

“You may ask me one thing and I may say no, and if I say no you may not ask me again today.” She waited. “Say it, and be quick, because the words *ask me* are in the slate and there is a limit on them and I would like us to find out what the limit is while we are both in a good humour about it.”

“Take me on the flat.”

“No.”

He did not say anything else. He took the prop out of the mud and put the barrow up the steps.

That was the whole of it. She wrote the hour and the two words in the back of a paper she had folded into four and carried since the fourth day of the month, and under them she wrote the ground of her own refusal, because Mercy Slee had put *leash* on a slate in a town four miles behind them and had underlined it, and a leash with nothing written on it about why is a leash that becomes a grudge inside a week, and Tamsin Rook had watched a great many things become that.

*Flat fork, at the top of the rise, Day 34, about the ninth hour. He asked me to take him on the flat to the ferry. I said no. Ground, for the book and not for him: the flat fork is a carrier's road and there is a carrier's book on every barrow that uses it, and he has a frame in a cradle that is Crown property, and if a Crown object goes down a carrier's road then a carrier's book has a Crown object in it, and there are men in this county who would give a great deal for a page with a Crown object on it, and he would not know and I would. And there is a second thing, which is that I have not walked up those steps as myself since the eighth of the month, and I would like one road this week that I walk as myself, and that is the whole of the ground and he does not need it and is not getting it.*

He read it over her shoulder while she wrote it, which she had known he would, and did not say he was sorry, and did not ask again.

---

They came into Ocklebridge at the fourth hour of the afternoon, from the low turn side, and the village is a river village of about two hundred persons with a landing stage and a smithy and a bell on a post at the head of the water.

And the woman at the bell is called Marta Vell and is about sixty on Tamsin Rook's word and no other person's word, because Tamsin Rook is the only person who has ever asked her, and she has no surname on any page anybody has ever seen, and she has rung that bell every morning for thirty years, and at about twenty past seven on the morning of the thirty-third she rang it four times, unhurried, because somebody at a landing had asked her to and had named what they wanted repeated, and nobody had told her why anybody wanted anything.

“Do not tell her,” Tamsin Rook said, at the top of the rise above the village, before the barrow came down. “Not about the schedule. Not today. Not at four in the afternoon with a Crown bell in a cradle behind you.”

“You told me on the thirty-third that telling her was not yours to do,” Tomas Vale said. “I know. I have not forgotten it and I am not going to argue with you about whose it is. I am telling you that it is going to be mine by tomorrow morning whether you help me or not, and that I would rather you were standing in the same room when I do it badly than somewhere else afterwards telling me how I did it.”

“That is fair,” Tamsin Rook said, “and it is not me going to help you. It is me going to be there. Those are different and you have had the second one out of me about nine times this month and I would like it noticed that I have not given you the first one once.”

They went down into Ocklebridge with the frame in the cradle on the barrow, the bolts made fast, and nothing rung.
