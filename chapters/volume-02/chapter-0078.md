# Chapter 0078 — The Household That Says No Twice

The pitch is at the end of the row now and it is at the end of the row because a woman with a book of her own came down a row on the sixty-first at about the second hour of the afternoon and said four pounds a quarter, over a season, if the weather held.

Tomas Vale came up the row at about the fourth hour of the afternoon of the sixty-fourth and there were four stalls between him and the cheese and a woman at the front of it who had never seen him in her life.

“**No,**” said Dory Larkin.

“I have not asked you anything yet.”

“You have got a face on you like a man who is about to and I have had three days to get used to it,” she said, “and I am not going to be unkind about it, and I would like that said first, and then I am going to tell you the rest of it standing here, and you are going to stand there and hear it, and then you are going to go away.”

“All right.”

“**And you were not in this street two nights ago.**”

He did not say anything.

“There were nine carts at the end of this street and two men lifting a crate off one of them, and a woman with a board stood in the road and looked at the lot of them for about a minute and a half and wrote something down on a slate and went off up the hill with it. **My brother saw her and told me at about the seventh hour and I have had three days to think about it and I have not slept a great deal, and I have got to the bottom of it, which is that she was doing something and you were not, and that you are the one standing in front of me now.**”

“She is not with me today.”

“Then that is the first useful thing anybody from your road has said to me in three days,” said Dory Larkin, “and I am not going to pretend I am glad of it, because what I want from you this afternoon is not company.”

He waited, which he had been warned about in four counties and which he did anyway.

“If you had come to me about those carts, either of you, I would have told you the whole of it,” she said, “and I am not going to, and that is the first thing I am refusing you and I want it counted.

“**It is not a secret. It is not about the district and it is not about anybody in this street. It is a thing that happened to this house about a year ago and I have said it out loud once and it was to the woman with the board, and I have been thinking for three days about whether that was the right day to say it and I have not got to the bottom of it and I have not slept.** And I am not going to say it a second time to a man I have met four minutes ago in a row, and when you go up that hill tonight you will not be able to put it in a book, and that is not cruelty and it is the only thing I have got this afternoon.”

“I am not going to ask you for it,” said Tomas Vale.

“I know you are not. **That is the part I have not been able to get round, and I have had three days.** A man who does not ask twice is a thing I have never had in this row and I have had about four landlords. **I do not know what to do with you and I have told you that and it is the first honest thing I have said to a stranger in a year.**”

He stood there. It is in the day-book and it is one line and the line is in the third column:

> *She was correct and it was not a wound and I did not treat it as one and it is the first time in five weeks that anybody has refused me in a way that was not a matter of standing.*

---

And then she said the other thing, and she got it out flat and without any help from him, which she had clearly practised.

“You are not here about the cart, are you.”

“No.”

“Then you are here about the no. **And the no is the whole of why you are in this row and I have known it since about the fourth minute.** A woman with a board wrote down that I said no to a man with a book on a stool, in nine hundred people's hearing, and it has my name at the head of it and the hour on it, and I have read it four times because there is nothing else in the house to read, and **it cost us the front of this row, and I want you to say out loud that you know it cost us.**”

“It cost you the front of the row. About four pounds a quarter over a season. The market keeper said it at about the second hour of the afternoon on the sixty-first and your mother said the number and I did not hear your mother and I have wished I had.”

“Why do you wish you had.”

“**Because the woman with the board was nine feet off and wrote that down in her own book in a lane that night, and told me there is a class of thing a person in a crowd can miss entirely and it is not the loud thing, and I have not been able to stop thinking about it since that night.** And I was not nine feet off, because I was in a yard four streets off with four bolts in it, and I did not know any of it had happened until the third day.”

Dory Larkin put a hand on the cheese and left it there.

“Then here is the second thing,” she said, “and then you are going to go away, and I have been three days getting it in the right order and I am going to get it in the right order or not at all.

“**You have not asked me for anything. Do not.** And I say that before you ask, because you have got a face on you like a man who is about to ask, and there is a woman on this road who asked me a question on the sixty-first in front of the whole market and did not ask it twice, and I have not been able to get past that either, and I have not got a good reason for that one either, and I have stopped expecting good reasons from either of you.

“**You are not going to be able to do anything for us.** That is not unkind and it is not a refusal of you, it is a refusal of the whole of what you are, and I want it said in a row and not in a room. You are a man with a barrow and a thing in a yard you cannot ring and a sheet of paper with a man’s hand on it, and you have been in this town three days, and there is a hall in this town with nine clerks in it and every one of them is right, and **there is not one thing in this kingdom you can do for this house and you have not asked and I would like to keep it that way, because the last person who asked me to let him help was my landlord and he had my rent.**”

“I am not going to do that.”

“I know you are not. **That is what makes it worse and I am going to say why.** If you were a bad man I would have said no in nine words and shut the pitch and been done. You are not a bad man. You are a man who does a thing well, and if I let you do it here then in about four days there is a person in this town who says *that is the family on the cheese pitch at the end of the row*, and then there is a person in an office who says the same, and then there is a person who comes with a book. And I do not know what a person with a book wants. **I have had one in this house before.**”

She stopped and there was a market going on nine stalls away and neither of them looked at it.

“So that is the second refusal of this afternoon and it is not about the no,” she said. “**It is about the two rooms.** I am not going to have a page. And the day there is a page about my house that is not in this house then this house is a thing that is *about*, and I have read about nine families in this town in about four years, and every single one of them had somebody come in with a book and a good hand and a face like yours, and every one of them has got less now than they had and about four of them have got a great deal less, and I have never once seen it go the other way and I have looked.”

---

He did not use the leash. He had it in his mouth for about a second and a half and he put it down and it went in the fourth column with the hour on it, and it is in the day-book entire:

> *I had the leash ready at about the fourth hour of the afternoon on the sixty-fourth and I did not use it, and I am writing down that I did not use it, and I want the reason given in my own words and not hers, because she has given hers and hers is better.*
>
> *The reason is that she is refusing to be helped and that is the first time in five weeks that anybody has refused me that, and I have four documents and a certificate and a day-book and a man in a hall who would answer a question, and there is a thing I could try, and **I did not try it, and the reason I did not try it is that she asked me not to, and she asked me not to in a form that was reasonable, and the whole of what I have learned in five weeks is that a person saying no in a reasonable form is a person who has already done the work, and turning up with a method after that is not a method. It is a man who cannot tell the difference between being useful and being wanted.***

Then he said the thing, and it was the thing he came for, and he had not known it was the thing he came for until about the fourth hour and it came out of the refusal rather than in front of it.

“What would you want written down.”

Dory Larkin did not answer for long enough that a woman went past with a basket.

“**Nothing. I have told you.**

“No. You have told me what you will not have. I am asking the other one, and there is a difference and I am not going to be a man who cannot tell it in a cheese row. **If a room in this town with a clerk in it was going to write one line about your house, in a column of your own choosing, in your own words, out loud, with the hour on it — what would you want the line to say.**”

And she told him. It took about four seconds and she did not look for it and she did not build it.

“**We are five and we do not agree, and we are not going to be asked again, and we mean it, and any hand on that line is one of the five of us and not the house.**”

He wrote it down. He wrote it exactly as she said it and he did not put the hour at the top of it and he did not put his own name at the bottom of it, and he wrote it on a page of his own because the slate was in her bag four streets off with the woman carrying a frame in a yard and because a page could be got to a room and a slate could not.

And then he read it back to her, which he does, and which is the only thing about him that has never once been a mistake.

“Any hand on *this* line,” she said. “It is not this line. It is not a line of yours.”

“**Any hand on that line.**”

“**And *asked*.**”

“Asked.”

“You have written *we are not going to be asked again.* Change that word.”

He waited.

“**Put *sent for* in it,**” said Dory Larkin. “A cart comes and a man comes and they stand in the road and they stand in the kitchen and they wait, and nobody in this house has asked anybody anything. **You are asked nothing. You are sent for.** I have had both and I am telling you which one costs.”

He made the change. It is in the day-book entire and it is the whole of what he learned on the sixty-fourth and it took four seconds:

> *We are five and we do not agree, and we are not going to be sent for again, and we mean it, and any hand on that line is one of the five of us and not the house.*
>
> *— asked, and read back at about the seventh hour of the afternoon, and changed by the person who said it, to **sent for**, and the change made, at her word and not mine.*

---

And then, because she was a woman who had thought about it for three days and because he had asked a proper question and got a proper answer, she gave him the part she had been holding back since the front of the pitch, and she gave it in the flat voice of a woman reciting something she has said to herself many times.

“**You have come here to find out what happens to us if we say no a second time,**” she said. “So find out. Ask me. I have been waiting three days.”

“What happens if you say no a second time.”

“**Nothing.** That is the whole of it and it has taken me three days and I want you to hear it properly because you have come four streets to hear it. Four years we have had that line and four years I have said no and twice the round has come and twice nobody has come, and the line is closed anyway, and there is a mark on it that I did not make, and I have never met the man who did, and I have never been able to find out what it is.”

“So refusing is free.”

“**Refusing is free and refusing is invisible and I have done it nine times and it is the only free thing I have ever had out of a Crown building in my life.** I worked that out in about a year and I want to tell you how, because you will want it and I would rather hand it over than have you take it off me in a month.

“The clerk at the door asks one of us for a line. If we answer, it takes him about nine minutes and it is done. **If we do not answer, it takes him the same nine minutes and it is done.** That is the whole of it. He writes something in a column that I have never seen and he goes on to the next one, and the thing he has written is not us and it does not have our name on it, and no person has ever come to this pitch or this house or this street about that line, and I have asked about it, because I have been asking about it for four years, and I have asked at the door and I have asked at the hall and a man in a hall said *the line is answered* to me and would not say by whom, and I have never found out.

“So you are asking me what happens if we say it twice, and **the answer is that the first time a house finds it out and after that the house is left alone, and it is not because they are right and it is not because we are brave. It is because a line that cannot be answered is a line somebody in that hall has to put something against, and they have got about eleven thousand of us and they have not got the days.**”

“And your mother.”

“And my mother is the reason I have not had a nervous collapse about it. **My mother cannot read and my mother can count, and about two years ago the district put a rate on this pitch and did not tell us what it was for, and my mother went in on her own and got it taken off and came home and told us what it was, and it took her most of a day, and she has told that story about four hundred times since and it is the reason I have not.** She is the only person in this house who has ever been into that building and she is the reason I am not frightened, and she has never once asked me what a mark in a column is and I have never once told her there is one.”

She stopped.

“So do not come back here and tell me we are brave,” she said. “**We are not brave. We have a good line and a bad pitch and a mother who can say a number and that is the entire method in this house and it is not anything you would want in a book.**”

---

He was in the lane off the lane at about the ninth hour and he had been refused three times in one afternoon by a woman selling cheese, and he had a sentence in his own hand that was not his, and it is in the day-book and it is the whole of the sixty-fourth, and the fourth column has one line in it and it is the only one of these nine days where the fourth column is about him:

> *The sixty-fourth. I have been refused by a household, in my own county, three times in one afternoon, and I want the difference between the three of them written down because I have not got it anywhere else and I am going to forget it in about a week.*
>
> *The first was the carts. The second was the rest of it. The third was the whole of what I am, and it was the only one of the three that cost her nothing to say and she said it in a row in front of about four stalls, and I would have asked her to come indoors if there had been anywhere indoors, and there is not, because of the pitch, because of the four pounds a quarter.*
>
> *And there is a fourth thing and it is the one that is in the fourth column and it is not a refusal at all and it is the reason I came up this row.*
>
> *On the sixty-first, in that row, in front of about nine hundred people, **a woman with a slate asked a woman at a pitch whether she had ever been put on a paper without being asked to.** I was four streets off in a yard with four bolts in it and I did not know it had happened until the third day, and I have not stopped thinking about it, and what I am going to write down and keep is this: **that question was a question about a claim, and it is the correct question, and it made a column, and the column is what cost them the front of the row.** And the question I asked at about the seventh hour this afternoon was not a better question. It was a different one. It was a question about what she wanted, and the answer is a sentence in her own words, and the sentence is worth nothing at the level of a hall and it is the only thing in this house that is hers.*
>
> ***And that is the whole of what I have learned in five weeks, and the woman I learned it from is the one who taught me that I was not in the row, and she did not know she was teaching me, and I am not going to be able to tell her, and that is the correct arrangement and I am not going to improve it.***
>
> *A household can be asked a question about a claim and the answer is a column, and the answer is worth the four minutes it takes and it changes nothing at the level of a hall. A household can be asked what it wants and the answer is a sentence in its own words, and the sentence is worth nothing at all except to the people who are going to have to say it out loud in a room, and I do not know which of those two things I have been doing for five weeks and neither did he.*
>
> *And she told me the trade. Refusing is free, and refusing is invisible, and a line that cannot be answered is a line somebody has to put something against, and they have got eleven thousand of us and they have not got the days. **A household does not have to be brave to be left alone by an office. A household has to be inconvenient, and it takes about four years to find out and it is not a practice anybody could teach anybody, and it is the only defence anybody in this novel has found against a line, and it is a defence that costs the front of a row and about four pounds a quarter over a season and a mother who can say a number, and it is the whole of what is left.***
>
> *She is going to stand in a room with a clerk in it on a day of her own choosing and say that sentence out loud, in their own words, in a column they choose, and they know what it will cost and they are going to do it and they told me they are going to do it and I am not allowed to be there. **I asked at about the seventh hour and she said no and it was the same word as the word at the front of the pitch, and I did not ask again, and the not asking is the first time in my life I have not asked a thing twice where I wanted to, and it is going to be the last chance anybody gives me of that, and I have spent it on a cheese pitch, and it was the right place to spend it.**
