# Chapter 0082 — The Household That Has Been Sent To Twice

She worked out the difficulty in the first hour and it was a new one and it took her the whole morning to get it into a shape she could carry, and the shape of it was this.

**She could not ask.**

She had been asking at doors for eleven years and it was the whole of her trade and it worked in four counties, and on the sixty-eighth morning she worked out that the method would now produce the wrong answer, correctly, and she worked that out about four streets away from the door where it happened, and then she walked to the door anyway and did it.

Because that was the cruelty of the place and it was not a cruelty anybody had done on purpose. **A household that has been sent to twice reads a third visit as a debt coming due.** And a household that believes a thing is owed does not refuse. A household that believes a thing is owed answers, because answering is what you do with a debt, and it answers out loud and in the door and in the ordinary voice, and it answers *yes* — and it is a good household, and it is a household that has never once been asked.

---

She was at a stall at about the fourth hour buying nothing, which she does about four days in ten, and she heard a woman say a sentence to a stranger.

It was the same sentence.

Not the same words. The same sentence. She has written it down both times and the two are different sentences with the same bones, and what is in the bones is: **you have not sent for it, and it is on the line, and I am not going to be the one who says it twice.**

The first time she heard it, a woman had said it to her sister at a rope stall at the top of the market street, on the sixty-seventh, and Tomas Vale had stood eleven feet off and heard it and had not moved for about a minute and a half and had written nothing down at all.

The second time she heard it, a woman said it to a stranger at a stall of nothing in particular on the sixty-eighth, and the stranger was a man with a barrow of onions who said, "**It is not the same line, is it.**" And the woman said, "**It is not. It is the Verrows' and it is four years and there is a third one coming and I have said my piece and I shall not say it again.**"

And the man said, "**Then that is the one to watch,**" and went back to his onions.

Tamsin Rook stood there and understood two things at once and neither of them was about a roll.

The first was that she was one of about nine thousand people in this county who had heard it, and that **it is a line**, and that a line is a thing with a shape, and that nobody in the market knows it is a line and everybody in the market is standing in one.

The second was that **she had just been told where to go**, by a woman who did not know her, for nothing, in the ordinary way, and had not asked her a single question first.

---

The far end of the lane. A door with a curtain in it and a step worn in the middle, and about nine feet of gutter and a wall with nothing on it.

**Five people in that house. It is a mother and two of her children and one more and the small one, and the small one is not a description anybody in the lane uses and it is the only word Tamsin Rook had for them and she used it twice and did not improve it.**

She knocked at about the fourth hour and a woman came with her sleeves up, and she did not ask who was there, and Tamsin Rook decided afterwards that this is now the second door in this town that has done that and it is not a coincidence and she has stopped writing coincidences down.

“Tamsin Rook. I am a courier and I am not from this county and I am not from the district. **I am going to ask you three questions and they are the same three I have asked at ten doors in four counties and I am not going to change a word of them, and I am telling you both of those facts before the first one, and you may shut the door on all three and I will not come back.**”

“You are going to stand there and say that.”

“I am going to stand there and say it. **And I am going to say the one other thing, which is that any answer goes on a thing I write on with my own hand and the hour against it, and anybody may read it, and I am telling you that before you answer and not afterwards, because that is the only part of this with a price on it.** And I will tell you the price, because you have not asked and I do not want to be thanked: it is fourpence, and it was fourpence eight days ago in a market a day up the water, and it has doubled and I cannot tell you why and I am not going to pretend I can to a woman in her own door.”

The woman did not move.

“Mave,” said Tamsin Rook. “I am going to use it. I am at a door and it is a word I have.”

“Go on, then. I have got the fire to want feeding.”

---

**First.** *Has any person ever put their name to a paper for this house — a Crown form, or a return, or a certificate — without anybody asking them to?*

“Yes,” said Mave Verrow. “**Twice. And I put it there both times, and I will tell you the whole of it in about a minute and then you are going to be wrong about it at me again, and I have not had anybody stand in my lane be wrong about it before and I am not going to pretend I am enjoying the experience.**”

“All right.”

“There are five of us in this house. The mark is in a column against the line, and a day on it, and a stamp somewhere at the foot. **I did not write the mark. I have never written the mark. I have never in my life laid eyes on that mark and I could not tell you this morning what colour it is.** What I did was put a hand against a line when a man asked me for one at the door, and he asked me as though asking a person for a hand, and I gave him one, and I have given him one twice.

“**And the first time I gave him one I thought I was agreeing to something.** That is the part I have not said out loud. I thought there was a thing at the end of it. He did not say what the thing was and I did not ask him and there is a thing about that. **I have gone into that building about four times in four years and nobody has ever told me what the mark is, and I did not think about it, because a mark is a mark and a man comes and asks and you give him a hand, and what else is there.**”

“You have not been asked anything.”

“**Not one thing, not by anybody, not ever.** And that is not a complaint, and I want you to hear that, because you have a board and I can see the board from here and you are about to put a thing on it, and **there is nobody in this kingdom that has ever put a question to me at a door. You have just done it and it is the first time and I am telling you that you did not have to.**”

---

**Second.** *Is the person who put their name there living in this house now.*

“I am,” said Mave Verrow. “**That is the second time a person has asked me that sentence in four years and the first one was a man with a board and I told her the same three words.** And I will tell you what I have not told her, because you have told me the price of the other thing first and I am not a fool.

“**I do not know what I agreed to. I have had four years to work it out and I have got nowhere, and the reason I have got nowhere is that I have nobody to ask.** The man at the door is gone by the time I have got my sleeve down. The hall is a building I have been in four times. And there is nobody — not one person, not one — that I can say *what did I agree to* to, and I have said that sentence out loud in this kitchen twice and both times the person I said it to was one of my own.

“I have asked twice about the day on it. **I have never once asked what it is.**”

---

She had the slate out by then. The one she had carried since the sixty-first, a penny and a half off a stallholder at a landing, four columns ruled on it and the hour at the top and no heading over any of them, and she wrote the two answers in the third column, ruled apart, and did not merge them.

And then she asked the third one, which is the only part of the method she has never been able to leave out, and she said it in the same words she has said at ten doors in four counties.

**Third.** *Do you want that written down, and may I write your no as a no and not as nothing?*

Mave Verrow did not answer it.

“**No,**” she said.

And Tamsin Rook put the chalk down, and did not ask it again, and wrote it, and ruled it off from the other two, and did not merge it, and put the hour against it.

It is in a reader's hand in a slate that cost a penny and a half:

> *Third column, ruled apart, hour against it. This house, the sixty-eighth, this door, at about the fourth hour. **No.** And the no is not about the district and not about the mark and not about the round. I am going to say what it is about and I am saying it because she has told me the price of the other half twice and I owe her the whole of it.*

---

“**It is about the small one,**” said Mave Verrow. “And I am not frightened, and I am not going to be talked out of it by a woman with a slate, and I have not slept, and I have been waiting since the sixty-first for somebody to come to this door so that I could say it out loud to a person instead of to my own hands. **So here it is. That is a no and it is the first one anybody has written down and it is the only one that matters and it is about the small one.**”

“Tell me the rest of it.”

“**The rest of it is four years and two hands against a line, and the third one is coming, and I have never been asked what the mark is, and I have worked out what it is. I have worked out what it is and I did not get it from anybody and I got it in the kitchen at about two in the morning like everything else.**”

She did not sit down.

“**A mark in a column is an answer. That is what it is. It is not a request and it is not a bill and it is not a notice. It is a hand against a line, and a hand against a line is a hand against a question, and the only thing a hand against a question can be is the answer to it.** And if the two of them are answers then the third one is owed, and a thing that is owed gets paid.

“And I have never once asked what the question was. **That is the whole of it. Nobody has ever told me the question and I have paid the first two answers without reading them and I would do it again if I asked and was told the question and the question was small.** And I asked my mother what the question was when I was about her age and she said *they will tell you when it is wanted* and I have believed that for about thirty years and I am not stopping now because of a woman with a board.”

---

And then Tamsin Rook said the thing she had carried for four years and a day, and she said it once, in her own mouth, to a woman in a door, and it is not a speech and she has not improved it since.

“**Being on a list that reads as a duty is not the same as being on a list that reads as a debt. And the difference is about four years and one neighbour in a queue, and by the time it is a debt it cannot be got back by asking.** That is the whole of what I know about it and I have been wrong about it eleven times in a month and I am telling you it before you decide anything, because you will do the right thing with it or the wrong thing with it and you will not be able to tell which afterwards, and everybody in this country is going to be in that position inside about four years and the ones who get it wrong first will be the ones that love somebody.

“**I cannot make it a duty again. I have been in this lane for a month and I have asked a woman at the end of it for a page and got a room, and I know every single thing that is in this that I know.** A duty is a chore and a chore is a thing you do for yourself in your own house on your own day, and if that is what the mark is then your third one is yours and I have no business here at all. And a debt is a thing that somebody else can call in, and if that is what it is then you have been paying the right money to the wrong man for four years and there is no office in this kingdom that will ever notice, because nobody at any door is going to tell you that you were mischarged.”

Mave Verrow looked at her for a moment.

“You have just told me it is hopeless.”

“**I have told you that asking does not get it back. I have not told you it is hopeless and I am not going to, because I do not know whether it is and because you are the only person in this kingdom who can find out, and you are not going to find out by asking me.**”

---

She did not ask the fourth thing.

She wanted to ask it. She has written that down in the slate afterwards and she has not improved it and it is eleven words: *I wanted to ask where the line is and whose name is on it and I did not ask.*

**The reason was not the leash and was not the fourth of last month and was not caution.** The reason was a woman at a table in front of her with a fire to want feeding who had already said no, and a no is a person, and a page is a page, and Tamsin Rook has spent eleven years refusing to do to one household what a clerk's hall does to about eleven thousand of them in a year, and there is no version of the question that does not require the woman in the door to go and stand in a room in a building she has been in four times in four years and say a thing out loud to a man with a book, and **there is a price on that and I have told her the price of the other thing and I was not going to do that to her on the same morning.**

So she wrote the no down as a no, and ruled it off from the two answers above it, and did not merge the columns, and read all three back one at a time, and Mave Verrow heard her own words and nobody else's and changed nothing at all, which is the first time in four years that has happened to Tamsin Rook at a door.

Then she told them the price, because she had promised a number.

And the price of the no was this, and she said it standing in the gutter with the slate under her arm and she said it as a fact and not as a warning, because a warning is a thing a person gives a person they are about to leave:

“**A no in this town is a household that has not answered.** You have been told that or you have not. I am telling you because nobody has told you and I have been in a book of refusals and a counter in four counties and I know what a no turns into. It does not turn into nothing. It turns into an open line. An open line is a second visit, and a second visit is a cart and a man and a day, and there are about nine of them in this row this month and one of the nine is a pitch at the end of a row and they have lost the front of it already.

“**And that is the whole of the price and I cannot reduce it and I am not going to pretend to be able to.** And I am a stranger and nobody has asked me and I am going to go now, and you have three days, and no cart is coming in three days, and the one that is coming after that I cannot stop and neither can you, and **you have told me no and I have written it down as a no and I am going to go and not come back to this door, and that is the last of what I have got and it is not worth anything and I would rather you had it.**”

She went down the lane about nine feet and stopped, because there was a boy with a barrow of nothing much in it at the mouth of it, and he asked her the way people in this town ask a stranger, which is by telling her three things about himself.

“Round's shut. Came the sixtieth, went the sixty-third, shut at the end of the sixty-sixth, and there is nothing in it now and there is not going to be anything in it again till the spring.” He shifted the barrow. “**And I will tell you the thing that half this town worked out this week and that nobody has got a name for yet: the third one is not going to be a round. There is no round left to send. Whatever the third thing is, it is not coming out of a cart that goes up and down the streets with a book on a stool, and that means it is coming off some other piece of paper, and I do not know which, and I have asked two people and one of them was rude about it.**”

“I am not asking you for it.”

“I know you are not, and I have said it anyway, and I am a carter with a barrow and I am telling a woman with a slate something for nothing on a lane, **because there is not one other person in this town that has said it out loud to anybody and I have been sitting on it since the sixty-sixth.**”

And Tamsin Rook stood in a lane in a town of about two thousand people, at about the seventh hour, with a slate under her arm and a no in it in a woman's own words, and worked out two things.

The first was that she had done everything her trade knows how to do, in the right order, in the same words, and had produced a thing that was about to cost a woman her child.

**The second was that there was no book in this town that would tell her when, and she was not allowed to ask for one, and the only thing that had ever gone to a place to find out whether a person was in it was a person on foot with a bag.**
