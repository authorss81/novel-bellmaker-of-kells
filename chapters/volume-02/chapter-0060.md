# Chapter 0060 — The Road To Miremarket

He did not decide for nine hours, and in the nine hours he wrote four columns, and in the four columns there is not one word that is his own opinion, and that is the first time in a month that a page of his has been made that way and it is the only one he will be able to read in a year without wanting to be a different man.

The stage is above the water at about eleven miles, and the tide turns at about half past four and there is a boat.

He came down to it at about the ninth hour with a slate under his arm, because the day-book had four days of road in it and a slate is what you use when you intend to read a thing to somebody who is standing up, and the reason he had a slate was that he had decided, somewhere on a parapet at a low drain gate at about the seventh hour of the previous evening, that he was not going to make this decision.

The reason he was not going to make it was Tamsin Rook, and she had said it to him at the tenth gate on the forty-third day and he had written it down: *a household is the only thing in this kingdom that can say a thing a document may not say for it.* And a road is not a household. A road is a set of people who happen to be one after another.

He wrote the question out first, because a man who asks a question in the wrong words is a man who has already decided.

*Has anybody here carried a paper for a household to a town downstream? And if a person standing here were to put a question, in their own words, to somebody who carries papers to a town called Miremarket — that question being: is a name entered on a roll sent anywhere when it is registered, and if so where — would that person carry it, on a day, for nothing, without saying what it was?*

He read it aloud to two people, in the same words both times, and he did not add anything to it for either of them, and he wrote down what each of them said, and the writing down took eleven minutes and it is the only part of this day he is proud of.

---

**The first was the ferry-man, Abel Rundle**, who has pulled a boat across this reach since he was about nineteen, and who said yes inside about four seconds and then said a great deal more.

“There’s the coach,” Abel Rundle said. “Up the bank, twice a week it runs in from the high road and it touches at Miremarket the same day if the water’s kind, and it takes barrow and all, I’ve seen a man take a churn of milk on it. Two of you and a barrow and a thing in a cradle, that’s the fare for a barrow and a *suit*, and you pay before you get on and I don’t carry anybody who says they’ll pay at the far end.”

“That is a very good answer,” Tomas Vale said, “and I want to write it down in those words and not in mine. May I ask you the second half on its own? If a person here were to put a question, in their own words, to somebody who carries papers to Miremarket — is a name entered on a roll sent anywhere when it is registered — would you carry it, on a day, for nothing, without saying what it was?”

“I’d carry it,” Abel Rundle said. “I’ll carry anything anybody hands me and I never read it, and there’s four of us on this reach and two of them will take a paper anywhere on this river for a shilling and think themselves lucky of the shilling. Miremarket’s four days down if the water’s right and it’s a market town and there’s more clerks in it than there’s anybody in this parish. You’ll get your answer inside a week or you’ll get nothing, and either way you’ll be stood in a market looking like a man who came a long way to ask.”

“Thank you.”

“Don’t thank me, I want the shilling,” said Abel Rundle, “and I want to tell you the fare’s a fare and there’s no talking it down, because I’ve a boy to feed and the last man that wanted a talk-down got it out of me for a good lie about the weather.”

---

**The second was Hanna Grieve**, who keeps the tally at the head of the stage, which is a ruled book with a column for the day, a column for the thing, a column for whose, and a column for the hour, and which is the same shape as a yard book at an inn, and which Hanna Grieve has kept for about thirty years, and which is on a bench under a lean-to about nine feet from the water.

Tomas Vale read the same words to her, in the same order, with the same pauses, and he did not add anything to it, and Hanna Grieve answered the first half and then stopped dead on the second half and looked at the barrow in the road and then at the cradle on it.

“A Crown bell in a Crown coach,” she said.

“That is my reading of it too and I would like to hear yours before I have mine.”

“That coach is on a Crown contract to the market at Miremarket and it is on a Crown warrant and it is on a list, and the driver keeps a list of what he takes on, and he has to, because he is not allowed to take on what he cannot write down.” She put her hand flat on the tally book. “You go up that bank at eight in the morning with a barrow and a thing in a frame in it, and nobody sees you, and you’re in a building at Miremarket before the middle of the second day, and a year later a man in a room in Fourmile says *show me the second day* — and there is no second day, because a coach does not keep a day. A stage keeps a tide. A coach keeps nothing at all. And you are standing there with a bell that cannot be rung and a receipt that says anybody may ask for it, and there is a page on a table that says a Crown object went into Miremarket on a Crown coach on a Crown day, and nobody put it there except the coach, and the coach did not do it on purpose.”

She said the whole of it in about twenty seconds and then said, at the end, in a different voice: “No. And I have said why and I am not going to say it better if you ask me again, and you may ask me once more today and then you may not, and if you ask me a second time I will say the same no in the same words, and that is my right and I have had it for thirty years.”

“There is no second question,” Tomas Vale said, and shut the slate.

---

He wrote both of them down at the head of the stage on the boards of a bench, in four columns, in the flat hand, and it took him until the light came off the water, and the four columns are these.

*Column one, Abel Rundle, ferry-man, this stage. Asked the three questions in the same words. Answered the first, yes, and the second, yes, and would carry a paper, in his own words, on a day, and named the fare and would not talk it down. Recorded whole, at about the tenth hour, and not improved.*

*Column two, Hanna Grieve, of this stage, keeps the tally, thirty years. Asked the same three questions in the same words. Answered the first, that there are four on this reach and two who will carry a paper for a shilling. Declined the second, in her own words, and the ground of it is a coach, a Crown contract, a driver who keeps a list, and a barrow with a thing in a cradle in it, and the sentence: a Crown bell in a Crown coach. Entered as a refusal at the eleventh hour, and the whole of it entered, and it is not a silence and it is not an answer and it is not going to be improved in the morning.*

*Column three, the road. Not decided.*

*And two things I am putting in the same column because they belong in it and I have been carrying them since Fourmile and neither of them is about a boat.*

*The first is that I stood in a district office in a real town on the forty-second day for the best afternoon I have had since I left a town with a wax on its door, and I did not ask them one word about the fifteen lines, and I could have walked in again, and I did not, and I am not going to, because a district in Fourmile has promised in writing to answer in an officer's own name any question a person puts about a line on that schedule, and a person putting that question is a household putting that question about its own line, and no household in that ward has come to the board, and it is not mine and I have no standing in it and I have spent eleven days watching what happens to a page when a man with a bell in a cradle decides he can fill it. So it is still owed and still unasked and I have written it here so that the day I stop noticing it will be a day somebody notices this line.*

*The second is that on the thirty-third day of last month I asked a woman in writing, in front of a witness, whether she would carry a finding on foot to doors of her own choosing, and she said no and gave no reason and told me on a towpath that I had not pressed her once, and I have not pressed her once in eleven days, and I am not going to open it now because I have a boat and a date and a signature and a man at a sluice and everything I could want out of a person today, and the one thing I could want today is for somebody to come with me, and she is four feet away and she can hear a strike and she cannot carry, and I am not going to be the man who asks her for a reason to get on a boat.*

*Column four, Tamsin Rook, own hand, own column, and I did not ask her.*

And under column three, and this is the whole of the decision and it is four lines long:

*The coach is out. Not refused by anybody — unavailable, because one person at a landing stage who has kept a tally for thirty years says a Crown object does not go in a Crown coach, and I have believed her, and I want it written that I believed a woman at a tally book over a man at a boat, and I want the reason written, and the reason is not that she is right about contracts. The reason is that she is describing a page appearing on a table in a building in Fourmile, and I have been in a building in Fourmile for an afternoon and I have read what a district officer will and will not put in a document, and I have worked out that what this business is made of is a small number of ordinary sentences that nobody chose because anybody was wicked. She did not choose hers either. She chose it because a driver keeps a list.*

*So the river. Four days down, on a tide, and the tide is not mine and I do not get a date, and I have a signature that is the same hand on a household of six in a lane two days back, on a smith's bill past a county line, and on four returns in a file in a building in Fourmile where a district officer has described it in his own hand in words I recognised before I had finished reading them: it leans forward and the descender of the g hesitates and comes back.*

*And the four counties do not share a bellwright. They do not share a market day. They do not share an office. There are four county stones on this road since Kells and there is a smith past the first one and a form-writer on the water below the second and nine hundred doors along a mile and a half of towpath, and there is not one thing the four counties have between them except a person who once signed something on behalf of a house and has been signing ever since, and he is not in a room, and he is not in a document, and he is at this moment asleep in a house above a sluice eleven miles behind us with a satchel on a hook and about forty forms in it for the spring.*

*I am going downstream to see the paper.*

And then, because he had promised himself in a shop past a county stone that he would write a page at without feeling sick, and because a promise like that is only worth making if it is kept in the worst hour of the day and not the best:

*And the part I do not like, and I am putting it in the same column and not in a new one: I have not asked her. Her four terms say she travels as far as I go and may stop at any hour without giving a reason and that I do not decide when she stops and am not asked to. She said out loud at a stage yesterday morning outside a real office in a real town that I was not to ask her, and I did not ask her, and I am writing down that I did not ask her, and the reason I did not ask her is not honour, it is that I was afraid of the answer, and if she had said no at a landing stage in front of two people I would have had to be a man about it in public for the first time in this volume, and there is no crowd here and no board and no Crown officer and the only people watching are a ferry-man and a woman who cannot hear me, and that is exactly why it would have cost nothing, and that is exactly why I did not do it.*

---

Her column is in her own hand on the right-hand board, and it is five words, and she wrote it at about the twelfth hour and gave it to him face down and did not explain it and he turned it over and read it.

*As far as it goes.*

That is the whole of the slate. That is the first line of a slate Mercy Slee wrote in a town four days behind them with the word *leash* on it underlined, and a woman who cannot hear a bell and is not obliged to obey anybody has written five words and gone and sat on a parapet, and she did not write a promise and she did not write a refusal, and she has not improved them, and he is not going to improve them, and he put the board down in the same orientation she had given it to him in.

---

They loaded at about the fourth hour of the afternoon. The frame went into the boat on the boards with the cradle under it and the four bolts made fast, and the cradle stayed under it, and the barrow went on the barge with the wheels chocked, and nothing was rung, because nothing in this batch has been rung and nothing in this batch may be.

The date was not his. Abel Rundle looked at the water for about four seconds and said the boat goes on the tide and the tide is what it is, and it is the forty-fifth at the top of the light and there is no argument available with a tide, and Tomas Vale wrote the date down in the fourth column of his own page and did not pretend he had chosen it.

At about the seventh hour of the evening the stage was empty and the boat was on the water and they were sitting on the boards at the head of it with the tally book shut in the lean-to nine feet away, and the tide had an hour left in it, and Hanna Grieve was on the stage with her back to them doing something to a rope, and Abel Rundle came up the path with a coil over his shoulder to go and do the same thing to a second rope, and stopped, the way a man stops on a path when he has been on a river all his life and has run out of anything to say.

He said, in passing, on his way past, to nobody in particular, in the voice of a man naming a thing he has named a hundred times:

“There are rolls at Miremarket that answer for people who never went.”

He did not stop after it. He was four paces up the path when he said the rest of it, and he said it to the path, and he did not turn round:

“That’s the Answer Rolls, that’s all they’re called down there.”

He did not say who asked him, or when, or what a roll was, or what the *never went* meant, or whether he believed it, and nobody on that stage could have told afterwards whether the two words were a name for a thing or a thing a man had heard of somewhere and repeated because it sounded like a name. He went up the path with the coil on his shoulder and did not turn round, and he was over the lip of the bank and gone up the road before either of them had got to their feet, and there was nobody standing on that stage to ask a second question of except a man with a barrow and a woman who cannot hear him.

The tide was what it was.
