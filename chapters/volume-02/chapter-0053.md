# Chapter 0053 — Six Years of One Hand

He got to the lane at about the eighth hour and left the barrow at the mouth of it with the cradle down in the hedge and the four bolts made fast, and he wrote the hour, and the words under the hour were: *Set down at the lane mouth, out of the houses, not carried in. Bolts made fast at the place it was set down.*

The lane goes up about a quarter of a mile and there are nine houses in it and a hard-standing of broken stone at the top where somebody turns a cart, and the whole of it is called nothing on any paper he has. The letter had said *the lane* and had said *in this lane* four times in four paragraphs without once saying the name of the lane, which he had noticed on the thirty-fifth and had written down, and which told him more about the writer than anything else in it.

There is no Crown object in the lane and no bell and no schedule and no district. There is a boy on a gate post at the top, about nine, and a woman at a door with a bucket, and four houses shut.

The first door did not open. The second door did not open. At the third, a woman looked at him for about four seconds and said *we have had a Crown man at this door and we have not had one for two years and you have not got a Crown coat on, so I do not know what you are*, and Tomas Vale said, *I am a man with a Crown bell in a field at the bottom of this lane, and I am not from the Crown, and I am not going to stand on your step, and I would like to read you four paragraphs that somebody wrote and forgot to sign, and that is the whole of what I want and I will read them standing in the lane and I will go away afterwards.*

The woman said, *that is the worst offer anybody has made at this door in two years,* and went in, and came out with a slate.

---

Maud Prine read the four paragraphs standing in her own doorway with the bucket still in her hand, and she read them the way a person reads a thing they wrote four days ago and have not looked at since, which is quickly, and without stopping, and with her own breath going wrong about a third of the way down.

“That is my hand,” she said, at the third paragraph. “And I did not sign it, and I did not write the *forwarded*, and there is a clerk in Fourmile with a wrapper and a pen who has answered me twice and has not once written a name on anything of mine.”

“May I ask who you are.”

“Maud Prine, of this house, and I do the writing, and there are six of us and there are five of us here this morning because my husband is at the upper works and comes home on the fourth day.” She looked at the barrow at the bottom of the lane, four hundred yards off, and could not see it and knew. “And I have heard about that.”

“I have not said anything about it yet.”

“You have not said anything about it because you have come about a form,” Maud Prine said, “and that is the fourth time anybody has come about a form in six years and I have got the letters ready, so would you like to come in or would you like me to come out.”

---

There is a room with a table in it and a fire that is not lit, and five people in the room, and the door stayed open the whole time, and Tomas Vale asked for the form.

That was not what he had planned. What he had planned was what he had done in Kells for a month: ask each of them the same three questions, separately, one person at a time, in a different room if there was more than one room, and write down what each of them said in his own words with the hour against it, and put the five answers in five columns and not merge them, because that is the whole of what that town had learned in twenty-one days and it is the only method anybody has ever seen work.

He asked the first one and got an answer that took the whole of the standing-up and was about the water. He asked the second and got an answer that took about four minutes and was about a brother-in-law who had not come back for a funeral. He asked the third, who was about nineteen, and who said *I do not know anything about forms* and then, after about a minute, said *there is a thing on the table I am not supposed to have touched*, and Tomas Vale said *you are not going to be asked about it and you are not going to be asked to say anything at all*, and the boy said *right*, and sat down, and that was the third answer.

Then Maud Prine stopped him.

“Mr Vale,” she said. “You are doing the thing you did in Kells. You are asking us one at a time so that we cannot hear each other. And it will not work, and I will tell you why it will not work, and then you can decide what to do about it.”

“Tell me.”

“We have been asked separately for six years,” Maud Prine said. “Not by you. By a form. The form comes to this door once a year and it is one sheet and it is put on this table and it is taken away by a man who does not live here. And the whole of what the form wants is the answer of this house. And there is no room in this house where the answer of this house lives, because the answer of this house is five people and a man who is not here, and we have never once sat down and worked out what we think, and we are not going to be separated into a back room to be asked by a stranger with a book who has a Crown bell in a field.”

“That is fair,” Tomas Vale said, and shut his book, which was the first thing he had done in a month that he had not planned. “Then I am not going to ask you anything separately, and I am not going to ask you anything at all, and I would like the form.”

---

The form is a **supplementary return**, and Tomas Vale had seen the species of it twice in a month and had never seen this one filled in, and it is a good form.

It is two sides. The first side asks the household what it wishes to state, and gives it four lines to say it in, under a heading that reads *reason, in the household's own words*, and that heading is the single best thing the Crown put in front of a ward in the whole of the twenty-one days he had spent watching it, and it is on the form because somebody in a Crown office stood in an arcade long enough to hear a girl of sixteen out. The second side asks for a name of a person present at the answering, and for the hour, and it says in its own small print at the foot: *A statement returned before the closing hour of the period stated above will be certified.*

That is the whole of the Crown's promise and it is a good one. It means that a household which says something, in its own words, before a date, gets a stamp. And the stamp is worth having, because a stamped answer is a return, and a return is a thing an office cannot send anybody to a door about.

And on the table in that room there were three of them, in a heap, in a house of six that had never in six years thrown a Crown paper away.

---

“There is the heap,” Maud Prine said. “That is six years. You may look at all of it and you may not have any of it.”

“May I look.”

“You may look and you may not turn them round, and I will not leave the room while you do it, and if you ask me what the small print says I will read it to you and I will not explain it.”

He looked at them for about twenty minutes. The top one was the current year's and had not gone anywhere. Under it, going back, were five stamped returns, each one carrying, in a box headed *name of a person present at the answering*, **the same handwriting**, and the writing was a young man's, and it sloped forward, and the same two letters did the same thing in *present* and in *certified* and Tomas Vale, who has read a hand in wax and in a bench-book and in a margin for nineteen years, looked at that hand for about four minutes and knew three things about it.

It was the same hand every time. It was not six hands that had agreed. It was one hand, five times.

And it was not a copy. A copied signature has a stiffness in it where the pen stopped and a blot where the nib was set down twice, and there is neither here, and the downstrokes of the *d* and the *g* have the same hesitation in all five, and it is not a traced thing.

Nobody had ever lifted a pen. Somebody had signed once.

---

“This is a copy,” Tomas Vale said. “Somebody at the district has traced this five times.”

“No they have not,” Maud Prine said. “Look at the dates.”

He looked at the dates.

The six-year-old one is the bottom of the heap. The five above it are, in order, this year, last year, and the three before. And on each of the five, in the box for the name of a person present at the answering, the same hand has signed **a different date**.

Not the same date carried forward. Five different dates, five different years, in one hand, with the same hesitation in the *g*.

Maud Prine watched his face and said nothing at all, and let him have it, and waited, and it was the worst courtesy anybody had ever done him.

---

“Sit down, Mr Vale,” she said. “I will tell you what happened, and then you will tell me whether you already knew.”

“The man is not in this house.”

“The man has not been in this house for six years,” Maud Prine said, “and he was in it for nine, and he is my husband’s brother, and he is alive, and he is two counties over, and I am not going to tell you his name and I am not going to tell you where, and I have already written that down in a letter and I did not sign it, so that is the second time I have not signed something of my own.”

“Then he is signing these.”

“He signed the first one,” Maud Prine said, “in this room, on this table, in the winter, before he went, and he did it because I asked him to. I asked him to and I have not forgotten asking and he has not either, and I will not pretend to you that he is the villain in this, because he wrote one answer and it was our answer and it was true at the time and there were six of us in this house and he was the only one of the six who could write, and that is the whole of it.”

“And the other five.”

“The other five came back.” Maud Prine said it without any weight at all, which was worse than weight. “The district takes the return in, and stamps it, and sends it back to the house with the stamp on it, because the house has to keep it, that is in the small print, a household shall keep the certified statement for the period stated. And in the year after, a man comes to this door with the next form and the certified statement from the year before is in his other hand, and he puts the two sheets side by side on this table and he says the answer you gave last year is the answer this office holds for you, and you may confirm it, and there is a line to confirm it on.”

“Confirm it.”

“That is the word on the form. *Confirmed by the household.*” Maud Prine put her hand flat on the table, not on any paper. “And it is not false. It is exactly true. He wrote it. It was our answer. It was true when he wrote it. And the form is a supplementary return and the district certifies it and a certified answer is a return, and it has never occurred to anybody in this lane to put those four things in a row.”

Nobody in the room said anything for about a minute.

“Then who brings them,” Tomas Vale said.

“A man comes down this lane four times a year,” Maud Prine said, “with a satchel and a stack of them, and he fills them in, and he has done since the year before the first one. He is not Crown. He is not a clerk. He is a man who is very good at writing and who is asked for by people who cannot write, and he does not charge, and he is not cruel, and he has been in this lane so often that my husband knows him by his step and has never once asked me what he is called.”

“Do you know what he is called?”

“I know what he is called,” Maud Prine said, “and I am not going to tell you, and I want you to hear the reason I am not, because it is not because he is hiding anything. It is because if you go up that lane with his name, then the man who fills in a form for people who cannot write becomes a man people know about, and the next person in this lane who cannot write will find that the man has stopped coming, and I do not know why he would stop, and I am not going to be the one who finds out.”

---

The man at the end of the table, who had said nothing at all for the whole of it, put both hands on the table.

“Maud.”

“Matthew.”

“Mr Vale has been in this room for an hour,” the man said, “and he has a Crown bell in a field at the bottom of the lane, and he has read a thing out of our drawer, and he has had your six years in his hand and he has not said one word about the children.”

“There are no children in this house,” Maud Prine said.

“There are two in the next house and there were two in the winter of the flood and one of them went on a cart, and you know which one, and I do not, and this man has not said a word about it and he is not going to and he has not asked.” The man did not look at Tomas Vale. “You have come to a lane of six houses and a man with a book has come and asked one question, and you have all got very pleased about the asking, and the asking is for him. I have watched a man in a town be very pleased about the asking for a month. What is he going to do with it that is for us.”

“I am going to ask you for one piece of paper and I am not going to ask this house to stand in anything,” Tomas Vale said.

“Ask.”

“The certified statement from six years ago,” he said. “The one your brother-in-law signed. Not this year’s, which is on the table and which the office expects back. The old one, which is a record and not an instrument.”

Maud Prine took her hand off the table and looked at the heap, and looked at it long enough that nobody at the table said anything.

“I may give you that,” she said. “It is ours and it is a record and we are not using it, and I may give it to you because I am the one in this house who does the writing and the paper is in my keeping. I may not give you the one on the table, because that one is going back to the office on the fourth day and if it does not go back then this house is on a schedule twice, and I am not going to be the reason a house is on anything twice.”

“That is the correct answer and I would have given the same one,” Tomas Vale said.

“You would not, but it is the correct answer and I am glad you are writing down that you agree with it.”

She took the bottom sheet out of the heap without straightening it, and she did not tear it and she did not fold it, and she held it by the corner, and then she put it on the table and put her hand flat on it.

“Copied fair and given out of my own hand, at the fourth hour of the afternoon of the thirty-sixth day of this month, to a man I have met once, at my own door, of my own act, and I have not asked this house to say anything to anybody, and there is no name on it but his and I am not giving you his.”

“Entered,” said Tomas Vale, in his own book, in his own flat hand, and he wrote out the words as she had said them and did not shorten them, and he read the copy back to her and she corrected him twice, and both corrections were good and both are in his book.

Then he wrote the date under it, and the hand under it, and six words describing the hand, and the six words were: *forward slope, hesitating descender, same pause before g.*

---

The man called Matthew would not look at him for the last hour and did not thank him, and said so.

“You are not thanked,” Maud Prine said, when they were at the door.

“I am not expecting it.”

“I am telling you that he is right and I am not going to argue with him in front of you, and I am going to tell you why he is right, and then you are going to go away.” She had the door frame. “Six years. And a form that is a good form. And a man who fills in forms for people who cannot write, and comes four times a year, and is never asked what he is called, and is at my door in the winter and at the four doors either side of us and in a lane two miles up, and it is not the Crown and it is not not the Crown, and there is nothing in this lane that can be put in front of a form. Not the flood, not the cart, not the boy that went. **You can put a district in front of a form. I have tried it twice and got the word** forwarded **on a wrapper.**”

“I know,” Tomas Vale said. “I have read it.”

“Then you know that there is no instrument in this kingdom for a lane,” Maud Prine said, “and you have come up a lane with a book and asked us to be witnesses to ourselves, and we will not, and I would like you to leave knowing that we will not and not to be disappointed in front of us, because I have watched a person be disappointed at a door in this month and it turns the door into something.”

“I am not disappointed in you,” Tomas Vale said. “I am disappointed in myself and I did it on the road and I have written it down there.”

---

He got the barrow at the fourth hour of the afternoon and wrote the hour and did not put the day-book away, and at about half past four he stood in the lane above the lane and wrote a thing that is on the page because he wrote it in a flat hand standing up, in daylight, with nobody watching:

*Asked them apart. Knew it would not work before I had finished the second one, and did it anyway, because it is the only instrument I have learned this month and because a thing that worked in a town of four hundred and eleven is a thing I want to be true of me. It was not true of me. They would not be a witness to themselves and they were right not to, and the reason they were right is better than the reason I was wrong.*

*And then they were asked together, and did not enjoy it, and answered anyway, and gave me the only thing in this lane that anybody could give me, which is a sheet of paper out of a drawer, given by the person whose hand it is, without a name, without a signature, and with the condition attached that the paper still in the door must go back on the fourth day. I am carrying a Crown form signed by a man for a household of six, in a hand he wrote once, dated six years ago, given to me by a woman who has been forwarded twice and has put her own terms on giving it away.*

*And the answer to the only question any of them cares about — who is answering for us — is that they do not know, and have never known, and would like it to stop. I cannot stop it for them. I have no instrument for a lane. I have told them that and I have offered them my book and my hours and my method, and I have none of those things that a district office is afraid of, which is to say I have nothing at all.*

*Cost, and I am putting it here because I have learned to put it where the person who paid it can see it: I have made a man in a lane of six houses dislike me, at his own table, on his own afternoon, for no reason that is his fault or mine, and he will not be thanked, and he is right.*

He came down to the top of the lane at about the seventh hour of the evening and stopped, and looked back up it, and the six-day heap was in a room with the door shut, and somewhere in that heap, under five stamped returns and one unstamped one, there were five dates in one young man's hand, none of which he had been alive to write.

*And there is one thing on the top sheet that does not fit and I am putting it here because I am not going to leave it out of the book in case I forget it: the name in the box this year is not that hand. It is a different hand. It is a careful hand, and it is not one of the six, and I asked no questions about it, because Maud Prine told me to leave, and because I have spent this month asking questions separately and I am not going to start again up a lane because it is what I know.*
