# Chapter 0075 — Four Columns Against A Column

Miremarket had a market on it on the sixty-first, a whole one, and about nine hundred people in the square, and Tamsin Rook bought a second-hand slate off a stallholder at the top of it for two pence and carried it down to the front of the cross at about the seventh hour of the morning and drew four columns on it with a stub of chalk, and the columns took her about ninety seconds, and nobody helped her and about four people asked her what she was doing before she had finished the last rule.

That was the finding of the sixty-first and she wrote it at the top of the slate before she wrote anything else in it.

**In Bramden there had been a bell.**

She had stood in a square on the fifty-third of this month with four columns and something to put in all of them, and the something had been a man with a bell in a crate saying four sentences at the top of the hour, and the whole of the difficulty of that afternoon had been that nine hundred people were waiting for him to say what it meant. That was a claim. She had a slate and a witness and a man who was publicly asking a town a question, and nine hundred people had nine hundred answers, and four of them had stood still while she wrote them down.

There was no bell in Miremarket. There was no man with a crate and no four sentences and no top of the hour. There was a market, and everybody in it had answered, and nobody in it had asked.

She put the slate down on the front of a pitch near the cross and left it there with the price side showing, and it was not a thing anybody in Miremarket had seen before, and in about forty minutes four people had read it and not one of them had asked her to explain it, which she had wanted, and by the ninth hour about nine hundred people had walked past it and about forty had stopped and none of the forty had anything in it to give her.

---

She stood in the middle of a market of about nine hundred people at about the fourth hour of the afternoon with a piece of chalk in her hand and nothing to do with it, and she had known there would be nothing to do with it since the seventh hour of the morning, and she had done it anyway, and that is the whole of what she learned on the sixty-first.

Column one was for the words of a person who had rung something, and the hour. There was nothing in it, because nobody had rung anything, and she left it empty, and she said out loud to a man who wanted to know why, and she said it four times over four hours to four different people, and it was the same sentence: **there is no bell rung in this square and if I fill that column I am the man who decides what a bell is for.**

Column two was for named bystanders, ruled apart, with a name and an hour at the head and the foot of each. She got eleven into it. It is on the slate and eleven of them are on the page in a reader's hand and every one of them said their own words and she did not improve one and two of them changed their own after she read it back. It is the best piece of work she has ever done and it is about a market on a market day.

Column three was for a thing somebody refused.

Column four was for a thing nobody could answer.

**Columns three and four were both empty for about four hours and the reason was not that nobody in the market had refused anything or that nothing was unanswerable. The reason was that the four columns are a set of questions about a claim, and a claim is somebody asserting a thing about a bell, and Miremarket is not asserting a thing about a bell. Miremarket is buying cheese.**

---

The man who asked her what she was doing asked it at about the ninth hour of the morning, in a voice with no malice in it at all, and he was the best of them and it made it worse.

He had a stall of sheepskins and a way of standing that made space, and he came and read the whole slate, all four columns, and stood back and looked at her, and said:

“Mistress. **What is this for.**”

And Tamsin Rook knew what he was going to say before he said it, and that was the whole of what happened that day, and she has thought about it a great deal since.

He said: “There is nobody in this square doing that. There is a man over there crying his baize, and there is a woman doing the thing with the boot, and there is a fiddle, and there is a man telling a story he has told four hundred times, and **there is one woman with a slate and she is the only one and she has been the only one for an hour and a half.**”

“Yes,” she said.

“Right,” said the man. “Then that is the answer to your slate and you have had it for free and you did not have to ask me. **Whatever you are doing, you are the only person in the square doing it, and the only person in the square doing a thing is not a column. It is a woman with a board.** I do not know your name and I would not use it if I had it, and I am not being unkind, and I am going to shut my stall and go to the back and I will open it again at the second hour, and you may stand there as long as you like.”

He shut his stall. He came back at the second hour and he did not say another word to her all day, and he was not a villain and he was not a fool and he was not one of the four, and she did not write him down.

**That was the second thing that went wrong and it went wrong in about nine seconds and nothing anybody did caused it.** The first was that there was nothing to put in the columns. The second was that she had been going to find out what a town thought a slate was for by being the only one, and a man in sheepskins had told her in one sentence what she would have spent a day finding out, and he was right.

She wrote it on the slate at the time, in the margin, in her own hand, in a line and a half:

> *Being the only one is not a thing about the method. It is a thing about the square. And the first question a market asks about a woman with a board is not what the board says.*

---

The refusal came at about the middle of the day, and it came the way the useful things have come all month, which is that somebody was doing their job.

A man from the district was at the end of the row with a book on a stool, going along the pitches, and he was not a clerk from the hall — he was a man in a coat with a district number on him and about two hundred lines in the book in front of him — and what he was doing was asking one question at one pitch at a time and writing the answer down.

Tamsin Rook stood about nine feet off and watched it for about an hour and a half, and it is the most competent thing she has ever seen a stranger do, and she wants it recorded that she stood there and thought it was well done, at the time, before she knew what it was going to cost a woman four stalls along.

The pitch it stopped at was a cheese and butter pitch, third along in a row of about nine, at the front, and the people on it were a family.

There was a woman doing the selling. There was a brother beside her with a handcart and a strap over his shoulder. There was their mother sitting on a stool at the back of the pitch with a blanket round her and her hands in her lap.

**Dory Larkin** was the woman at the front, and the district man asked her the question, and Dory Larkin said, in a market of about nine hundred people, with a fiddle going and a man crying his baize and the whole of a market morning in the air:

**“No. And I will say it as many times as you come.”**

The man at the stool wrote it down. He wrote it down in about four seconds and he did not argue and he did not explain anything to her, and then he said one thing that she heard twice and would carry for the rest of her life:

**“That is on the record. It says no and it has the hour on it and anybody may come and read it. I am obliged to tell you that, and nobody tells you that, and it is the only good thing this book does all day.”**

And Dory Larkin said, “I know it is. I have said no nine times.”

---

It cost them the front of the row.

Tamsin Rook saw it happen over about an hour and a half and she did not stop it and she could not have stopped it and she has never told anybody she could have.

The market keeper is not a district and is not a hall and is a man with about nine rows and a book of his own and about nine pitches let by the week. He came down the row at about the second hour of the afternoon and put his hand on the front of the cheese pitch, and he was not unkind, and he was not the district, and he said:

“That is one this month on that pitch out of about nine on this row, and I am not having two carts down it for a house in a dispute. **You are at the end of the row from the next market day.** Front pitch to end pitch on a row like that is about four pounds a quarter over a season and I have got nine pitches and you are the one I can move, and I am not saying it is your fault and I would have said the same to anybody.”

“How much over a season,” said the woman.

“About four pounds a quarter, if the weather holds. **It is not four pounds in one day. It is about four pounds taken off a market day in about eight market days, and I cannot do that twice, and it is the season that it comes off and not the day. And it is not a clerk and it is not a hall and it is me and my book, and no appeal goes anywhere from me and I would not take one.**”

That was all of it. He did not raise his voice once. Nobody in the row said anything to the Larkins. A woman two pitches along put her arm round their mother for about a second and then went back to her own butter, which is what a woman does in a row.

And the mother, from the stool, with her hands in her lap, said the number. Not the keeper. The mother.

**“Four pounds a quarter is the gas and the candles and something off the back of the rent and it is not a fortnight, it is the season, and there is a thing I am not going to say in front of nine stalls.”**

And Dory Larkin said, “Say it in front of nine stalls, Mother, I have said no in front of nine stalls.”

“No,” said the mother. “**I am not saying it in front of nine stalls. I am saying it in front of my own daughter and that is all I owe it to.**”

Tamsin Rook did not hear it. She was about nine feet off in a market of about nine hundred people and she was working the pitch and there was a fiddle and a man crying his baize and about two hundred people between her and that stool, and she did not hear it, and she wrote that down afterwards as the reason she stayed in the same trade for eleven years and as the reason she is going to be in it for a while yet: **there is a class of thing a person in a crowd can miss entirely, and it is not the loud thing, and no slate in the world fixes it.**

What she wrote on the slate, in column three, in her own hand, with the hour against it, is in a reader's hand and it is the first refusal of eleven that is on a public surface:

> *Third column, first entry. The sixty-first, this square, this row, at about the middle of the day. A woman called **Dory Larkin**, of the cheese pitch, third along, front of the row, said **no**, to a district man with a book on a stool, out loud, in a square with about nine hundred people in it, and he entered it and told her that it was on the record with the hour on it and that anybody might come and read it, and she said she had said no nine times, and I heard both of those and I wrote both of those down.*
>
> *And then it cost them, and I am not going to make this into a thing about a method, so: a man with a book of his own came down the row about two hours afterwards and moved their pitch from the front of the row to the end of it from the next market day, and the difference is about four pounds a quarter over a season, and the name of the man who did it is a market keeper and not a clerk and there is no appeal from him and he would not take one, and he was not cruel and he was not paid to and he did not once raise his voice.*
>
> *And it is not a child. Nobody was taken out of that pitch and nobody was turned out of a house and nobody was put anywhere. **Four pounds a quarter, over a season, and a hand on a line that says no, and that is the whole of the cost, and I was standing nine feet off and I let it happen and I would have stopped it if I had thought stopping it was mine to do.***

She read that back to nobody. There was nobody in a market of nine hundred to read it back to. That is the first time in eleven years she has had a record with a name at the head of it and no person to read it to, and she noticed it, and she did not do anything about it.

---

She found the district man in a lane off the square at about the sixth hour of the evening, standing in the road, having walked a market of about nine hundred for nine hours, and asked him one question, and got a straight answer in about nine seconds, and it went through her like a hand in a bell.

The man on the stool with the book — the district man, the competent one, the one she had stood and watched and thought was well done — was still in the town, because his round had not closed, and she asked him one question and got a straight answer in about nine seconds and it went through her like a hand in a bell.

“**Whose hand was on that line.**”

He said, “Mine.”

“Then that is a record that a household could read.”

“It is a record that **you** could read, and the household could come and read it, and it says no and it has the hour on it and no name on it.” He shut the book. “**I wrote no. A mark in the column. I do not put names in a column; there is no box for a name in that book and there has not been one in eleven years.** And I will tell you what the line did, because I have never had a person ask me and I am on my last day of a round and I can tell you: **the line closed. It closed because the year closes and the schedule goes on and a line cannot be held open, and the mark is there against it, and the schedule is finished.**”

“And they said no.”

“They said no, and it is on the record, and the record is good, and **the record is a record of the line being answered, and the line has been answered, and the line is done, and the schedule has gone on to the next house, and that is what a schedule does.**”

“Whose answer is it?”

He looked at her properly for the first time.

“That is a very good question,” he said, “and I am going to answer it, and then I am going to go and shut my book, because you are not from this district and I have been on my feet since the eighth hour of the morning. **It is not anybody's answer. There is nobody's answer on that line. There is a no in words, written down by a person, and a mark, written by me, and the line is closed. That is all three of those things and the third one is the one that is the office's.**”

And he put the book under his arm and went off up the lane, and he had not been unkind and he had not been defensive and he had answered four questions he had never been asked in eleven years of rounds, and he was right, and Tamsin Rook stood in a road in a town of two thousand people and found out that she had been a reader of documents for a month and had not once asked the only question there is:

**Whose is it.**

---

She gave the slate away at about the sixth hour of the evening, at a pitch near the landing, to a woman with a stall of needles and thread and about nine of her own on the board, and the price side went up, and before she left the landing she bought the back of another one off the same stallholder for a penny and a half, and that is the one she has carried since, and the other one, the one off a draper's woman nine days earlier with a kitchen on it, is in the bottom of her bag and has been since the fifty-ninth, and she put the eleven bystanders and the one refusal where anybody could read them, and she said why, and the reason was not caution.

“**A slate in a market is a thing a town can read.** That is all it has ever been. In Bramden it was a thing four people could read and now it is a thing a whole town can read, and the day after the day I put it down is the day somebody in it can write on it, and I want that, because the only question left is not whether the columns work. They work. **The only question left is whether anybody in this town will fill one in.**”

The woman with the needles said, “What is a column for?”

“For one thing somebody said and one thing somebody refused and one thing nobody could answer and one thing somebody rang,” said Tamsin Rook, “and the fourth one is the one that matters and I have not been able to fill it once in eleven years.”

Then she priced it, out loud, on a landing, in her own words, because she had promised herself on the fifty-third of this month that the next time she was asked to stand next to something she would say a number first, and a woman with a barrow had been owed a number since the thirty-fourth.

“**Fourpence,**” she said, to nobody in particular, and there were about nine people on that landing and all of them heard it.

“Fourpence for what,” said the woman with the needles.

“For the four columns. And it was fourpence in Bramden eight days ago, and **it has doubled, and I have been sitting on a step for an hour working out how, and I am not going to tell either of you a reason I have not got.**”

He was not there. He was four streets off with a frame bolted in a corner of a yard, because the frame has not been out of a cradle since the thirty-fifth day of this month and the four bolts are made fast at every place it has been set down and the second clause says it stays in the cradle for the carriage, and there is nowhere for it to be that is not somebody else's ground and he has been going about nine days in a town of two thousand people not ringing anything.

He came down at about the eighth hour of the evening and she was on the crane steps with her bag between her feet and a slate about eleven feet behind her on a pitch with the price side up.

She told him the four things, in that order, without softening one of them, which is what she does.

“The columns were empty. Miremarket is not asking a question about a claim and I put a set of questions about a claim into a market and it is the wrong set of questions and I have known that for about four hours and I did it anyway because I have been carrying a slate for a month and a slate wants a market. A man with sheepskins told me I was the only one and shut his stall, and he was right, and being the only one is the failure and I knew it before he opened his mouth.

“A household said no on the record, out loud, in nine hundred people's hearing, and I wrote it down, and it cost them four pounds a quarter over a season off the front of a row, and I stood nine feet off and let it happen.

“**And it changed nothing.** The line is answered. The schedule went on. There is no name in a column and there has not been a box for a name in that book in eleven years. And the man on the stool with the book is the best trade worker I have watched in a month and he is the second one this month who has been right in front of me while I was wrong, and I have had enough of that to last a year.”

He did not say anything for a while.

“The fourth column,” he said.

“The fourth column is a thing nobody could answer. It is the one that matters and I have not filled it in once in eleven years and I filled it in **once** on the fifty-third of this month, in a square in another town, with about nine hundred people in it, because a man stood in the middle of it and would not tell them what a thing meant.” She got her bag up onto her knee. “**There is no man in this square who is not telling this square what a thing means. That is the only difference and it is the whole of it and it took me eight days to work out, and I have not told you, and I am telling you now because you are going to write it in a book and I would rather it were in the book in my words than in yours in nine days.**”

“Fourpence,” she said, before he could get to it. “I have already said it once tonight and it has doubled and I cannot say why and I am not going to pretend to a market of nine hundred that I can.”
