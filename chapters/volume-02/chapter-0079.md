# Chapter 0079 — A Man In A Chair With The Answer In His Own Hand

He asked the front door who was in the town and Ivo Trant would not tell him, and the reason he gave was correct, and Tomas Vale wrote it down and then went and found out in about eleven minutes by asking a person, which is the only door he has, and it opens in about eleven seconds, and he has been walking at it for five weeks and has got this far and no further.

The woman who let the rooms over a dyer's on the north side had a book on a shelf by the stairs with about four years of names in it and a column for the nights and a column for what was owed and a column for the room, and she had written the whole of it in her own hand, and she told him in about eleven seconds that a district officer had been in the town since the eighth hour of the morning on the first day of the round and had taken two rooms for the length of the round and was leaving when the round closed, and that her name was written in the book against a room and a night at four shillings including the fire and a column for what was owed, and she said it without being asked because a woman with a lodging book has told people who is in her house every day for about nine years and there is nothing in it to be careful about.

And then she said something else, and she said it because he had asked her a question and she was a woman who answers questions, and it went into his book that night and it is one line in the fourth column and it is the only document he has found in Miremarket that is not in a hall:

> *A lodging book is a record of who is in a town, with a night against them and what they owe and a room, entered by the hand of whoever brought them, and nobody has ever asked her what it is, and she has written about nine hundred names in it in four years, and it is exactly the same shape as the roll in the hall and neither of them has ever heard of the other.*

---

He went at about the seventh hour of the morning and said his piece at the door before he was asked anything, because he has learned to.

“My name is Tomas Vale, of Kells, without district. **I have no standing in this county or in any other and I am not going to pretend to have one, and I have brought two sheets of paper and I am not going to give them to you without asking, and I am not asking you to believe a word in either of them.** I am asking whether a person may come in for about an hour and put two written answers in front of a person who can do something, and I would like you to say no now if the answer is no.”

The woman at the door said, “She will see you. She sees anybody who asks, that is not a favour, that is the job. **Up the outside stair, and the second one is loose.**”

District Officer Hester Vowles was standing at a table with a round's accounts open on it and had been up since about the sixth hour and had about a day and a half of a round in a book on the table beside it. She was not a big woman and she was not dressed like anything in particular, and she looked at the two sheets on the table and then at him and said:

“Sit down. **I am going to read these before I ask you anything and I am going to say so, and I am not going to be pleasant about it, because you have come with two pieces of paper and one of them is not yours and I would like to know whose hand is on it before we start.**”

“Yes,” said Tomas Vale.

She read the auditor's sheet first, standing up, with her hands behind her back, and it took her about a minute and a half, and she read the clerk's second, in about the same, and she read them both again together with them laid side by side, and she did not say anything for about three minutes.

“Right,” she said. “**Both of these are correct and I am not going to quarrel with either of them, and I am going to agree with both of them, and I am going to be right about both, and then we are going to have a very short afternoon.**

“Whose hand is the second one.”

“Elias Nabb, clerk, in a hall in this town, on a sheet of that office's own paper, on the sixtieth, at about the eighth hour of the evening, and he wrote it for me and he has asked not to be asked again this week and I have not asked him again.”

“**Good. Thank you. That is worth more than either of those two sheets and you put it in the first minute.**”

---

She sat down across the table from him and put her hands flat, and it is in the day-book that it is the fourth time in five weeks that a person in a chair has put their hands flat on a table, and it is the first time the hands have gone flat before he asked the question.

“Here is what you have come with, and I am going to say it back to you because a man who has brought me two documents and no question is a man I have met about four times in eleven years. **You have come to find out whether a person who can act is the thing that was missing.** And you have not asked me a question yet.”

“No,” said Tomas Vale. “I have not.”

“Then here is the answer, and it is two, and they are not the same answer, and the difference between them is the entire thing you have been looking for and it is not a door.

“**An office can act on a practice. An office cannot act on a person.**”

She said it the way she said the other two things, without emphasis, because she has said it in about nine thousand rooms in eleven years and it is the sentence her trade is built out of.

“Ask me to say that a practice is wrong, in writing, in my own name, and I will write it this afternoon and it will go to the district and it will be read by a man with a district above him and a file in front of him, and **he can stop it in this district tomorrow, because a practice belongs to the district and I am the district's officer here, and I have stopped things in this district in eleven years and the worst of them is a thing about a sluice and it worked.** There is no difficulty about a practice at all. Write it out this afternoon and I will sign it and it is worth something.

“Ask me to do anything at all about a person — a household, a child, a man who put a mark in a column and cannot say what it is, a clerk who was asked what the mark means and put it on a sheet for a stranger — **and I have nothing. Nothing at all. Not caution. Nothing in a rule, nothing in a book, nothing in eleven years, and I have looked for it about four times and I have not found it and I have stopped looking.** A district is a ground. A ground is a place where a thing happened. **You cannot act on a ground. A man at Fourmile has been telling you that for a fortnight and I have read his book of refusals and it is the most useful book in this kingdom and I have wanted it on my own shelf for about six years.**”

She let him have that and she let him have all of it and she was right about all of it and she was not going to be thanked for any of it.

---

“**The number in the auditor’s return,**” she said. “You have it and you have been carrying it for about eleven days and you have not asked me about it, and I am going to assume that you have not asked because you have worked out that a number in a man’s head is a number in a man’s head, and that is correct, and his own sheet says so, and if you cannot say *nowhere* in about four years you will have to say *a man told me*, and that is a worse sentence and he is right about that as well. **So I am not going to correct his arithmetic, because it is not mine to correct and it is not in any record and there is nothing in front of me to check it against.**”

“It is not right,” said Tomas Vale.

“It is not wrong. **It is smaller.** Go on.”

“About forty pounds a year. About eleven thousand lines. Four hours a day, eight hundred hours, a clerk at eleven pence.” He put the four numbers on the table in the order he had them. “And about three hundred and sixty pounds a year across nine districts.”

“**That is the number you have been carrying and it is about half of the one you have been imagining, and I am sorry, and it is not a comfort to you, and I would rather you had the right number than the comfortable one.**

“There are nineteen districts under this county. **Nine of them have adopted the practice.** That is the nine. About three hundred and sixty pounds a year is the nine, and it is right, and your man did his arithmetic correctly, and I have looked at it and I cannot improve it.

“And the other ten have not adopted it, and they spend it, and they spend more than we do, because an open line is nine minutes and a held line is nine minutes and a household that argues is about four hours a day, and **you cannot get nine thousand households in a district to agree to argue in a kitchen, so a district that has not adopted this practice has not saved anything. It has moved the cost. It has moved it from a counter to about nine hundred doors and it has paid two men a day to do it, and the man on a cart costs about a shilling and a half and his board, and the arithmetic is worse and every man doing it knows it and it is why the other ten do not do it.**”

“So the truth is more expensive than the mark.”

“**The truth is four pounds a day, and the whole of it is wages, and wages are the cheapest thing in this kingdom to find.**” She stopped and looked at him properly. “And here is the part I am obliged to say and I am not going to soften it, and then I am going to stop and you are going to be disappointed and I am telling you in advance so that you do not spend the afternoon finding out.

“**If all nineteen adopted this practice and it were stopped in all nineteen, the money would be about seven hundred and sixty pounds a year.** That is a good deal of money and it is about the price of a small bridge, and it is not money anybody finds in a place anybody looks. **A district does not get seven hundred and sixty pounds. A district gets an allocation and it spends it on whatever the district has decided to spend it on, and the district has been decided by people who are not in this room, and there is no part of this that comes to me, and none of it that comes to a clerk, and none of it that comes to you.**”

She folded her hands.

“You have wanted a number that would do something. **There is not one. That is not a failure of your finding, that is the finding.**”

---

And then she did the thing that is the whole of the sixty-fifth and that he has not stopped turning over, and she did it without any weight at all, the way a person asks a question at a door.

“**Mr Vale. What do you want me to do with these.**”

And Tomas Vale sat in a room over a dyer's in a town of two thousand people with two sheets of paper on a table, and he had about eleven days' worth of a method and a receipt with five clauses in his coat and a bell in a yard, and he opened his mouth, and there was nothing in it.

It took him a long time. She let it take him. She did not help him and she did not prompt him and she did not look at the sheets, and after about a minute and a half he put his hand flat on the table, which he had not intended to do, and said:

“I do not know.”

“That is the first time in eleven years that a person has sat in that chair and said that to me,” said Hester Vowles, “and I want to tell you what the last nine said, because you are going to go out of this room and do something with the rest of your life and you should know what the other nine were.

“**Eight of them wanted a document. And I have given eight of them a document, because a district can produce a document, and every one of the eight went away satisfied and about four of them wrote to me again inside a year and by then they had forgotten what the document was for.** The ninth wanted me to be a door. You will not believe this and it is the truest thing I have said today: **a person who wants a door can be got through a door and I have been got through about nine times, and every time it cost me something out of my own book and I did it anyway and I am not proud of one of them.**

“You did not want a document and you did not want a door, and you have sat in that chair for an hour and a half and you have put eleven things in front of me and not one of them was a want. And I have been doing this eleven years and **I have never had that, and it is the most useless afternoon I have had in about four, and I want you to understand that I know that it is useless and I am not going to help you.**”

“May I say the honest answer.”

“You may say it and it will not change anything and I would like to hear it.”

“**I wanted a document that a person could not refuse.** I have been after that since the fortieth of this month and I did not know I was after it until about a minute ago and it is not available, and it has not been available since the thirty-second, and I have known since the fortieth that it has not been available, and I have done it anyway for twenty-five days, and that is the whole of what is wrong with me and it is not a thing about your town.”

“That is the most honest sentence anybody has said in this room,” said Hester Vowles, “**and I am not going to do anything with it, and you are not going to get anything out of saying it here, and I want you to notice that I have said that to you before you asked me to.**”

---

He asked her one more thing, at about the second hour of the afternoon, and he had been carrying it for five weeks and he asked it as a question and not as a demand, and she answered it in nine seconds and it took four minutes off him.

“The district's finding of the twenty-eighth. It was withdrawn on the thirty-third. **Is it worth anything to anybody.**”

“**It is a Crown document and it is worth nothing to you, and there is a person in this county who could ask for it and be given it in about a week, and that person is a household, or a household's district, and not a bellmaker.**”

“I am not asking for it.”

“I know you are not, and I watched you not ask it, and I would like to say why you did not, because you did not for a reason and the reason is correct and it is going to cost you the only thing in this county that you could still have used.

“**You did not ask because you have no standing and you know it, and because the only person who could use it is a household, and because you have spent five weeks building a thing you could put in front of a household, and you do not have it, and so the finding is no use to you and you are not going to beg a stranger for a piece of paper that a stranger has to ask for twice.** And that is the correct judgement and it is correct about the finding and **it is also the exact reason that nothing in this county is going to change while you are alive, and I say that as a fact and not as a warning.**”

He wrote two things down in that room. He wrote them in his own hand and dated them and put the hour against them, and the first is a question and the second is her answer, and he has got both, and there is no second copy of hers because he did not ask for one, which he should have, and he has written down that he should have.

*Put to **Hester Vowles**, District Officer, this town, this date, at about the second hour of the afternoon: Can a district stop a practice, and can a district do anything about a person.*

*Answer, in her own hand, and I have it exactly as she said it and I have read it back to her and she has not changed a word: **Yes to the first, and the practice is the district's and I can stop it here and I have stopped things here. No to the second, and not out of caution. A district is a ground. You cannot act on a ground. And the whole of what the other nineteen do about it is move the cost, and I would not make a claim on that in writing for a man I met this morning.***

And then, at about the sixth hour of the evening, after four days of trying to decide whether to send it, he posted her a letter, which is the second thing he has ever put in a woman's hand in a month, and the first was a sheet in her own hand that she read in front of him and never took back.

It is four lines and it says: *thank you, and I am not going to write again this week, and if you ever want a person to read a whole row of a thing and say what it is in his own words, I am on a road and I am not in a hurry, and there is no form for that and there is not going to be one.*

He put it in the post and it cost eleven pence and it was the cheapest thing he has ever sent anybody and it is the only letter he has ever written that was not a demand.

And then he went back to the yard behind the draper's shop, and the four bolts were made fast in the corner where the frame had stood since the sixth hour of the morning of the fifty-eighth, and it was still in its cradle, and nothing had been rung in this county, and the receipt was whole, and he had one day left in the town.
