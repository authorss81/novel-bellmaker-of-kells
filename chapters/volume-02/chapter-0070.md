# Chapter 0070 — The Man Who Reads It Twice

The name had been on three pieces of paper in six days and he had not met the man.

It was on the return at Helling, at the foot of the approval, in a good upright hand. It was on the sheet a rider had carried up the landing road at the ninth hour of the evening of the fifty-fourth, in pencil, on the back, in the same hand. And it was at the foot of a letter of two lines and a paragraph, in ink, and the ink was on a private seal that was not a Crown device and cost about eleven pence to somebody.

He walked to the west side of the square at about the eighth hour of the morning of the fifty-fifth with the day-book in his coat and a letter refused by a woman at a water bell in the inside pocket next to a river-post receipt for eleven pence, and he had not slept more than about four hours and he had not wanted to and he did not think it was why he was careful.

The house was over a haberdasher's and it was a room, and there was a hat-box in the corner and a bag on the floor with the strap buckled, and the bag had a document band round it, and there was a lamp and a table with two chairs at it and no third chair, which he noticed, and a window with the sacking out of the bottom of it, which he had last seen eleven feet off about two days ago in a room over a saddler's shop, and it made him feel exactly as bad as he expected it to.

**Everybody who comes to this town is in a room over a shop.** That is the whole of what he had learned in four days about a market town and it was the first thing that was not a document, and he wrote it down at about the fourth hour of the afternoon and by then it had stopped being a joke.

Pell Rill was not a big man and he was not dressed like a district and he did not look like a company, and he came at the door before Tomas Vale had got to the second stair and said:

“Mr Vale. Come up. I have been up since the sixth and I have made a mistake about the chair and I have not had time to send for another, and you may have the chair by the wall and I will sit on the end of the bed, and if that is disagreeable I will sit on the floor, and neither of those is a complaint, I am telling you the situation.”

“No. That is very well.”

“You said that too quickly.”

“I did,” said Tomas Vale, “and you have caught it, and I will come to it in about a minute and I will not pretend I have not.”

He was civil for the first four minutes.

He was civil in a way that Tomas Vale had not been in a room with for about a month, and the first thing was that he did not ask, and the second thing was that he offered the day-book, and the third thing was that he was right about two things inside four minutes and Tomas knew both of them were right before Rill had finished saying them, and after the fourth minute Tomas Vale found that he was afraid of him, and it stayed that way, and it was the first time in six weeks that a room had frightened him without anybody in it being wicked.

He came to it in about nine minutes and he did not make a speech.

“I wrote the return,” said Pell Rill. “I recommended the practice and the district adopted it and it is in about nine districts now and I stand behind every word of it and I would like to know what you think is wrong with it.”

“That is the question I came with.”

“It is the wrong question and I have answered it anyway. Ask me the one you have.”

Tomas Vale sat in the chair by the wall with his hands on his knees for about a minute and a half, and he went where he had gone nine times in four days, and he did not find anything.

“**Nothing is wrong with it,”** he said. “That is what I have come to tell you, and it has taken me nine days to say it and I have said it in about eleven seconds. It is a clear document. It is short. It has a number on it. It recommends a practice, it gives a reason, and the reason is good, and the reason is true, and a household does not agree, and a schedule does not hold open, and a second round is a cart and a man and a day, and a man will not come twice to one door. I have been trying to find the false thing in it since the forty-eighth and there is no false thing in it, and that is the difficulty and I am not going to pretend to you that I have come further than I have.”

“Then I have made your week,” said Pell Rill. “I am obliged to you.”

And he said it kindly, and he was pleased, and Tomas Vale felt the back of his own neck go cold, and he wrote that down at the fourth hour of the afternoon with the hour against it, and the note is one line and it is in the book entire:

> *He said thank you and he meant it. That is the first four minutes of the whole of this and I want it recorded that I felt the cold before I understood why.*

---

“You will want to know what it saves,” said Pell Rill. “And I am going to tell you, and I am going to tell you the number first and the number is the whole of the argument, and then I am going to tell you the thing I have never said in a room, and you may stop me at either point and I will not go on, and I want that understood before I start, because men have been in this chair before and about four of them have wanted a confession and there is not going to be one.

“Where a household is returned as unable to make up its mind, this district returns about eleven thousand such lines a year. I know that number because I have to sign for the district's return and the return has a column headed *lines held* and it is not a small column.

“**A clerk at a counter, with a room, a fire and the ink, costs this district eleven pence an hour and I have that figure because I have to sign for it.** A held line is a letter out and a wait and a letter back and an entry. It is about nine minutes of a clerk's day. Eleven thousand lines at nine minutes is one thousand six hundred and fifty hours a year.

“**So the office stops the clock at the point where it stops it, and the office loses about four hours a day.** Two hundred working days, eight hundred hours. **Eight hundred hours of a clerk at eleven pence is about forty pounds a year, and that is the honest price of my recommendation, and I have never once put that figure in a document, and I am now putting it in a room, and I would like you to write it down and check me on it.**”

He put a sheet of paper across the table. On it was a column, in a good upright hand, of the kind of thing a man works out on a journey and does not send to anybody.

“There. That is the four hours. It is not a large number. **I want to be exact about how small it is.** It is one clerk out of four. It is about two per cent of the district's answer. In nine districts it is about three hundred and sixty pounds a year, and in the whole of this and every district under it, it is a sum you would lose in a week gambling on a boat. **And that is the whole argument and there is no other argument and I have never been able to improve it.**”

He put his hands flat on the table.

“Now. The half that is mine and not the district's, and I have never said it in a room, and you did not ask for it and I am giving it to you because you came in here cold and did not pretend you had not.

“**What does the truth buy?** That is the question, and everybody who has ever come at this has gone straight past it, because they are all looking for the false thing. The truth buys a household four hours of argument a day. Not four hours of attention. **Argument.** The office writes back and says, *we cannot record one of you, you must come to a room and agree*, and then a cart goes out and two men go into a kitchen and four people who have never had a conversation about it in their lives have one now, and it is four hours, and at the end of it they have either agreed — in which case the line was never *unable to make up its mind*, they were not unable, they had not been asked — or they have not agreed, and then the line is still open, and then the office has to send a second cart, and there is no third.

“**And that is the correct answer to the question the office was asked. That is why it survives. Nobody in this kingdom has thought to ask the question a different way and I have looked for about nine years and I have not found one, and I have been right, and being right about a professional matter is a thing I am very good at and it has never once been any use to me.**”

---

He got up at some point and took the bag off the floor and put it on the table and unbuckled the strap, and what was in it was not one thing. It was a bundle of papers tied with a tape, and the tape had a broken seal on it, and the top paper was a sheet with about forty lines on it.

“That is a schedule this town keeps about a market,” he said. “**You may read it where it sits. You may not have it and there is no copy and I have never had a copy.** It goes back to a building on the last day of a round and it is not a record, and I do not call it one, and I want to say that before you look at it, because if I let you look at it first and said that afterwards you would be right not to believe a word I have said all morning.”

Tomas Vale did not touch it. He read standing up with his hands behind his back, the way a man reads a smith's stock book, and the room was very quiet, and the sheet had the following things on it.

It had a column of numbers down the left, and a column of dates beside them, and then a column, and the column was about the width of a thumb, and inside it there was a mark, and the marks were not all the same mark, and beside each mark there was a small thing that was either a cross or a letter. And at the foot of the sheet there was a stamp, and the stamp was upside down relative to the rest of it, and there was a line of writing at the top in a hand that was not Rill's.

And there was nothing else. There was no column headed *what the household said*. There was no column headed *who agreed*. There was no name of a household anywhere on the sheet at all, only the forty numbers, which were numbers and not names.

He read it three times and he could not say what it was.

Not because it was obscure. Because it was legible. He had spent a month learning to ask *what is the document* and he had got very good at it and he had never once had it fail, and it failed here, and he stood in a room over a haberdasher's in a market town and looked at a sheet of paper with a stamp on it and could not put a sentence to it, and the reason he could not was that there was nothing in it that belonged to anybody. Forty numbers, forty dates, forty marks, a stamp, and about forty people, and it did not say one word about a single one of them.

**It was a record of a thing having been done and the thing was not written down and there was nothing anywhere on that sheet to say what the thing was, and he had been in rooms with four hundred people and he had never in his life been in front of a document that would not tell him what it was.**

Pell Rill watched him do it. Tomas Vale was certain of that afterwards, and certain that the man had not looked away once, and it is in the book.

“You will want me to tell you what the column is,” said Rill.

“Yes.”

“I am not going to, and I am going to tell you in advance that I am not going to, so that you can spend your hour on something else and not on waiting for me. **I have been asked that question four times in nine years and I have not answered it once and I am not going to answer it to you, and I would rather say no to you now than in an hour when you have spent the hour and I have your attention for nothing.**”

“Then why show it to me at all.”

“Because you asked me one question in a letter and you did not tell me your business, and I have carried this for two days and read it twice, and **I do not think it is good of me to have read it twice, and I am telling you that in advance so that you will not waste an hour finding out whether I have.**”

“There it is,” said Tomas Vale.

“There what is.”

“You read it twice before you answered me. You said so in your letter, before I came. In writing, in your own name, about nine hours before we were in a room. And you have said it twice more since the eighth hour and you have not once asked whether I would think less of you for it.”

“Why would I,” said Pell Rill. “The first time I read it I did not know whether I had read it or remembered reading it, and a man who cannot tell those two things apart from each other is a man who should not write a letter about a document at all. So I read it again, and I have not written to anybody about anything since. **It is not a confession. It is a man checking a thing he is going to be asked about, and I would do it again at about the seventh hour this morning, and if you were the auditor in that county you would rather I had done it than that I had not.**”

And Tomas Vale thought, and did not write it down for two days:

*That is the most frightening thing anybody has done to me in a month, and the man did it out of the ordinary professional care that a careful man takes, and he is not hiding from me, and I have spent six weeks working on the fact that everybody in this business is hiding from something, and there is a man standing in front of me who read a document twice and told me so in a letter, and I do not know what to do with it, and there is nothing to be careful about, and that is the danger.*

---

It was at about the second hour of the afternoon when Tomas Vale said the thing he had come to say, and he said it to a room and the room had one man in it, and he had said in a book on the fiftieth that he had spent a month asking a man for a door.

“I am not going to ask you to be a door,” he said. “**I am asking this room to be a room.** And I have said that to one other room in a month and it was a real room with about nine people in it and a district officer in a chair, and he told me afterwards that I had been the complaint and not the compliment, and he was right, and I have been thinking about it for five days. So I am saying it to a room of one, and I am aware that a room of one is the hardest kind there is, and I am aware that I am doing it anyway, and I would like you to notice that I noticed.”

“You may ask me whatever you like,” said Pell Rill. “And I want to be exact about why, because you have been exact with me all morning and I have not earned it.

“**I will answer you because you asked me a question in a room. I am not doing it because I think you are right.** I have been read at by about four people in nine years and I have answered all four of them, and I am not the same man in this room as I am in my own, and if you were to go back to your county and say that an auditor of this district agrees with you, that would be the single most useful thing anybody could do to me, and it would also be entirely false, and I would want you to know in advance that I have thought about the risk to myself and I have decided to take it. **A man who is not asked keeps his own account. I have read about four hundred schedules and I have never once been asked a question by a household, and I would like to be, and you are not one, and I have not pretended you are.**”

“Then I will ask you one and it is not about the return. It is a question a woman asked me on a road on the sixth hour of the fortieth day of this month and I have been carrying it since and I have put it to about nine rooms and it has been put away nine times, and it is the only question anybody has ever asked me that I have not been able to ask anybody else.

“**What is the document?**”

Pell Rill did not answer for about a minute. He took the sheet, squared it on the table, and got a pen out of the bag, and wrote for about three minutes, and then turned it round and pushed it across, and it is in Tomas Vale's day-book entire and it is the second sheet a man in a stranger's room has written in his own name for him that he did not have to write, and the first was at Fourmile on the fiftieth, and Tomas Vale has thought about that a great deal since.

> *It is a record.*
>
> *It is a record of the answer given, entered in the answering column against the line, in the order in which the answers were given. It is the only thing it is. It is not a household's answer, and it is not a person's answer, and it is not a copy of either one of those, and I want to be exact, because I have read your question and I know what the difficulty in it is. **It is the entry, and the entry is made by the office, and the office is not in dispute between anybody in this room.***
>
> *So: a household is asked a question. The office does not obtain an answer from the household. **The office obtains an answer from a person**, and the person is a person and not a household, and the office records that it has obtained an answer, which is true, and the office records the answer, which is the person's, and the office treats the line as answered, which follows. There is no false statement in that. There is a great deal of true statement in it and one step across and the step is a professional one and I have never once thought it a dishonest step and I would like it recorded that I have gone back over about nine years of my own arithmetic looking for the dishonest step, and there is not one.*
>
> *And the number I gave you this morning is not in that sheet and has never been in it and is not in any other record I keep, and I have never put it in one. **It is in my head. My head is not a record. That is the entire difference between me and a book, and it is the only difference there has ever mattered, and I would be obliged if you would write that sentence down and keep it, because in about four years you will be in a room telling somebody the number and somebody will ask you where it is written, and if you cannot say *nowhere* you will have to say *a man said it to me*, and that is a different and a much worse sentence.***
>
> *Signed, Pell Rill, auditor, this town, this date, the second hour of the afternoon.*

---

He let Tomas Vale have the sheet and then he did a thing that was not in the letter and that Tomas Vale has not stopped thinking about.

“May I look at your book.”

“You may.”

“Not to take it. To read one page, standing up, with my hands behind my back, and I will put it down where I found it and I will not copy it and you will not have to let me, and I am asking because I am about to say something to you about your book and I would rather say it having read it than having not.”

Tomas Vale gave it to him.

Pell Rill read one page standing up with his hands behind his back, and he read it the way a man reads a document, which is a thing Tomas Vale had never seen anybody do and has thought about since. It took him about a minute and a half, and then he put it down exactly where it had been, and squared it, which nobody had asked him to do and which he noticed himself doing and mentioned afterwards.

“Four columns,” he said. “The question and the hour against it, the answer in its own column, the refusal in its own column. **Nobody has merged them. That is the first thing and I want to say it before anything else, because a book like this is very rare and the reason it is rare is that the man who keeps it is usually in a hurry, and you have not merged them in a month and I have not seen anybody in nine years who has not merged them.**”

“It is not mine,” said Tomas Vale. “It is four miles back and it belongs to a girl who is sixteen and I have never seen the inside of it.”

“Then it is the best book in this county and I have not read it and I would like to be told that I have not been told anything about it in a way I could carry back.”

He was quiet for a second.

“Now the other thing, and I am going to say it and I would rather say it than have worked it out on a step and brought it up to you in a month.

“**That is the most expensive book in this county and you have written it for nothing and you have shown it to a stranger in a room, and you did not ask me whether I was allowed to read it, and do you know what the price of a book like that is?**”

“No. I have not thought about it.”

“It is this. It is that anybody may ask. That is the whole of the protection and there is no other, and every person who has ever written anything down about a household has understood that inside about nine days and I have watched four of them understand it and two of them stopped. **You have not stopped, and you have not asked me one question about whether I am a thing that ought to see it, and that is not carefulness, that is a man who has decided that being careful is what a careful person feels like.**”

“I have written your name in it. Twice, this morning. Both times with what you said.”

“I know you have. I read it.” Pell Rill put his hand flat on the table for a second and took it off again. “And that is correct practice and I would not want a man to do otherwise. **And in about nine years, if anybody in this or any district ever wants to know what I said on the fifty-fifth of this month in this room, they need only find you. And there is one of you and there are not very many of me.**”

The room was very quiet.

“I am not saying that as a threat and I am not asking you to tear it out. **I am saying it as the only warning I have got, and I have had about nine years to find a better one, and I have not, and the reason I have not is that the practice is correct and the warning is about you and not about the practice.**”

“Yes,” said Tomas Vale.

---

It was at about the fourth hour of the afternoon when he asked the last thing, and it was the only question he had come with and he had saved it, and he had saved it because he had been in the room for about six hours and he had watched a man answer four things in four hours and he knew that the sixth thing would be given to him if he asked for it at the sixth hour instead of the fourth.

“Where are the records kept.”

Pell Rill looked at him for a second.

“That is the second question in two days that has come out of a room over a shop in this town and gone the wrong way round,” he said, “and I am not going to pretend I have not noticed it, and I am going to answer it and I am going to tell you why I am answering a question I have no reason to answer.

“**For a schedule of this kind, the entries go to a clerk's hall. Not a district office. A clerk's hall.** It is a building with shelves in it and one man in charge of the shelves and about nine others, and the man in charge of the shelves does not talk to a district officer and does not talk to anybody else either, and he is not obstructive, he is a man with eleven thousand pieces of paper and a set of rules about who may hold them, and the rules are correct.

“And I am going to tell you the rest of it and then I am going to stop, and I am telling you in advance that I am about to take something away from you, and I would rather do it now while you can be angry with me about it than at the end.

“**You have no standing in that building.** Not a complaint. Not a refusal. There is nothing in this kingdom by which a man who is not a district officer and holds no office may ask a clerk's hall for anything at all, and if you go down there you will be told so by a man who will be pleasant about it, and he will be right, and I would rather you heard it from me than spent two days on a river to be told it on a step. **I am not permitted to say that to you. I am going to say it, because it is true, and because a man who arrives at a door without knowing what the door is for is a man who gets a worse answer than one who does, and you have been getting worse answers than that for a month.**”

“Where is it.”

“Down the water. Two days by the river post, and there is no faster thing on this reach, and I am not going to tell you the name of the town in a letter and I am not going to tell you the name of the town in this room, **because if I tell you the name of the town then the next thing that will happen is that you will be in that town, and the thing after that will be that somebody there will know you went, and I would rather you found it the way you have found every other thing in this month, which is by asking somebody a question they were not expecting.**”

“Then what is the use of you,” said Tomas Vale, and heard himself say it, and it came out harder than he meant it, and he did not take it back and he did not soften it.

Pell Rill did not appear to be wounded. He appeared to be about nine seconds behind, in the way of a man running a thing through.

“**That is the fairest thing anybody has said to me in nine years and I would like to keep it.** I have given you four things: a document, a reason, a number, and a refusal. Three of those are mine to give and one of them is not, and the one that is not is the only one of the four that is any use to anybody, and I gave it to you because you are right that the use is nothing without it and because I do not want to be a man who gives the comfortable half. **The other three are for me. They are how I know what I am doing. If I did not have them I could not tell whether a practice I was about to recommend was sound or merely something I am used to, and I have recommended one that was not sound, in a year in another district, and it stood for four years, and it was found by accident by a person who had no reason to be looking for it and had not been paid to be, and I have never told that story in a room and I am not going to tell it to you, and I have said that I am not going to, and I would like you to notice that I have told you a thing I am not going to say in order to make the not-saying visible.**”

“You keep doing that.”

“I have noticed,” said Pell Rill. “I do it about four times a year and it has never once helped anybody and I do it because it is the only trade I have.”

---

## The consequence

“Before I stop,” said Pell Rill. “That chair has been empty all afternoon and the reason is that the woman who travels with you is not in this room. I asked the woman over the shop and she said the woman had gone out at the second hour with a slate and would not say where to.

“I am going to tell you something and then I am going to stop, and I am telling you in advance because I have found out twice in nine years that being kind in a room is a way of being wrong in a room, and both times it was by saying nothing.

“**The question you put to me is not yours. You said so yourself, and you said it came off a road on the sixth hour of a day, and she is not in this room, and it is not mine to ask her through you and it is not yours to answer for her.** I am not going to put it. And I have sent it up to a room over a draper's on the chance that she is in it, and if she is not then it was a waste of a boy's legs, and I will not send again.”

“That was a thing to do while I was sitting here.”

“It was a thing to do while you were sitting here,” said Pell Rill, “and I have not pretended otherwise and I would like it in whatever you are writing that I did it behind you and not to you, and that I would have done the same thing if you had been in my chair and I had been in yours, and that neither of those makes it a good thing to have done to a man who has spent a month learning to ask people things at their own counters. **You have asked me five questions today that were not yours and every one of them has been the right question, and I have enjoyed all five, and I would like you to notice that a man who enjoys five questions of that kind in one afternoon has stopped asking whether the person in front of him is the right one to ask.**”

The runner came up the outside stair at about the fifth hour of the afternoon with a folded paper in a hand that was neither the post nor the river, and Tomas Vale read it standing on the top step, and it was four lines in a good upright hand, and it was addressed to nobody at all.

> *I understand from the woman over the shop that you are the man who travels with Tamsin Rook, and that the question you put to me is hers and not yours, and I have believed that for about an hour, and I am not going to put it to a person through a man.*
>
> *So I have sent this up to a room over a draper's on the chance that she is in it, and if she is not, then it was a waste of a boy's legs and I will not send again.*
>
> *I am going to ask her one question and it is: **what is the document.** And I am telling her before she answers it that if she answers, I will write her answer in my own hand, in my own book, in this town, with this date and this hour against it, and that her name will be at the head of it, and that it will go down the water with the entries and be in a clerk's hall inside two days, and that nobody will be able to take it out of it, including her, and including you.*
>
> *She may say no. If she says no I will not ask again and I will not enter it and I will think no more about it. I am telling her that in advance because I have been in rooms where nobody did that, and the whole of what has gone wrong in my trade for nine years has been somebody being asked a question and finding out afterwards what it was for.*

She was in the room. He heard her read it twice through the door, and then he heard her say, in a voice he has heard give an answer at four hundred feet of river wall and at a landing stage and in an inn yard, “Say that I have read it and that I say yes, and I want him to write the four lines in his own book with my name at the head of it and the hour against it, and I want him to write them exactly as I say them and I want to see them before he shuts the book.”

He carried that down the outside stair and back up again in about nine minutes.

When he came back into the room he put the chair back where the chair had been and did not sit down in it.

“She is going to do it.”

“Yes.”

“You told her what it would cost her.”

“I told her precisely, and I have told her nothing else, and I have not helped her with it, and I have not put a second question to her of any kind.” Pell Rill put the pen down. “Mr Vale. In nine years I have had four people in this chair. One asked me a question that was his and got a good answer. One asked me a question that was his and got a bad one. One did not ask me anything at all. **This is the first time an answer has been given to me by somebody I did not meet.** And I will tell you what I have got out of a note and a runner and about four words from a woman over a shop, and then I will stop talking.

“She asked me whether I would enter it, and I said yes, and she asked me a second thing, which was whether I would enter *that* she had said yes. And I said no. **And she thought about that for a while and then she let me enter that she had said yes, and it took her about nine seconds, and it is the only time in nine years that a person has taken the cost of a thing and protected the fact that they had taken it, and I have written down both halves and I have written down which half she spent the nine seconds on.**”

“You wrote down the whole of it.”

“I wrote down the answer. **A person's answer to a question, with the hour against it, is a record, and a record is the safest thing there is, and there is nothing in it anybody can be used for, because a person is not a hand and a person answers for herself.**” He looked up. “You have been asking me for five weeks what a document is. I have answered you twice today and you have written both answers down, and I would like you to notice one thing about your own book before you go, and then I will not say another word about it.”

“What.”

“**You are carrying two leaves of paper of the same kind and you have never once put them in the same place.** One of them is a leaf somebody folded in four and put between two pages of a stock book and could not account for. The other is a leaf somebody folded once and put into a book with a number on the spine. **And you are carrying both, and you have told me in a room that you would not join them, and I believe you, and I want to be clear that believing you does not make them the same.**”

He walked back to the room over the draper's at about the seventh hour of the evening of the fifty-fifth with two sheets of paper in his coat that were not his and a third fact in his head that was, and the frame was in the yard behind the shop with the four bolts made fast where it was set down, and the bell was on the sill in its crate with the sacking off, and he did not ring it, and he has not rung it since.

She was at the window with the slate under her arm, and she said, without turning round, “He wrote it down in front of me and I read it and it is a good sentence and it is not mine, and I have been eleven years writing sentences at doors that people took away, and I have never once had one of mine written down as being mine, and I am going to be sick about it and I am not going to be, because I priced it in a smith's shop on the fifty-first and I have known the price for three days and I paid it with my eyes open on purpose, and you are not going to make it into something you did.”

“You should have asked me.”

“**Then you would have said no.**” She turned round. “And you would have been right, and it would have been the fourth time, and I have been the fourth thing that happens to you twice over now and I am not doing it twice. You did not ask me, and I would not have let you, and he told me the price before I answered and I am the only person in this building who could have got it wrong and I did not. You can put that in your book with the hour on it if it is any use to anybody.”

“It is no use to anybody. It is in the book anyway.”


He wrote for two hours. It is in the day-book entire, and it is the last entry of the fifty-fifth, and it is the one that decides Batch 0003, and the four columns are still four columns and the hours are in the flat hand and nothing was merged, including the part where he contradicts himself, which is in the fourth column on its own, and he read that part back twice and left it.

> *The fifty-fifth. I have met him. I want it recorded first that nothing happened.*
>
> *He is not a villain. I did not want that to be the finding and it is the finding, and I have been travelling five weeks to meet a man who could be made into a villain by being made into one, and I have now met him, and he is a competent courteous careful auditor who agrees with me about two things and is right about both, and he answered every question I have asked him in his own name and in writing and gave me nothing that is not his to give and one thing that is not his to give and told me in advance that he was giving it.*
>
> *And here is what I have actually lost, and I did not see it go.*
>
> *For six weeks I have had a thing to be angry at. It has never been a person — it has been a column, a stamp and a habit, and I told myself that was a virtue. **And for six weeks I have also had a use for being right: every man in a room who refused me was a man standing between me and a question, and I could go round a refusal, and a refusal is a thing you can work on.** I have worked on about eleven of them. Two of them gave me everything.
>
> *A man who agrees with you has nothing to be angry at and nothing to work on and no reason to be careful, and he will be in his chair tomorrow and I will not be able to get at him again, and I could not get at him this morning if I had wanted to, and he offered, and he offered twice.*
>
> ***He is the most dangerous thing I have met since I left Kells, and he is dangerous because he read a document twice and told me so in a letter before I asked him anything.** I have built a month of work on the fact that everyone in this is hiding from something, and he is not hiding from anything, and a man who is not hiding from you has no reason ever to move, and there is no lever for a person like that. I do not have a lever. I have a number about eight hundred hours, and it is about two per cent, and it is a good number, and I cannot answer it, because I have no answer to forty pounds a year. I have an argument about a signature. There are four hundred people in a clerk's hall two days down the water who are owed an argument, and not one of them is going to be helped by my argument.*
>
> *I asked him what the document is and he answered it in his own hand and it is a record, and the record is the only thing it is, and he is right, and the whole of what I have wanted for five weeks is in his answer and I do not have a use for any of it.*
>
> *And he told me two days before we met that he had read a schedule twice and that he did not think it was good of him, and he told me that a clerk's hall is two days down the water and he would not give me the name of the town, and he told me in advance that I have no standing in it, and he is right on all three and he told me the whole of it and there is nothing left to find out about him and nothing left to be careful of and that is the whole of the danger and I do not have a way of writing it more clearly than that.*
>
> *And the fact she gave me is in the book at the fifth hour of the evening of the fifty-fourth and I have not slept on it and I have not used it and it is a date against a line about a water bell and the day it goes in the book is not the day anything happened at her door, and there was no man at her door on any day at all, and nobody has ever asked her, and it is a fact about an office and not about a bell.*
>
> *I have a bell with no claim in it, and a certificate in a book that is the only one in the world, and a chain of two shops, and a sheet of paper in a man's own hand saying that the whole of it is a record, and a name that is four counties off and has now got a face and a good hand and an answer, and a woman at a copper in this town who has made a decision I am not allowed to visit, and a woman at a water bell thirty miles off who has refused me and owed me nothing and been owed nothing and been owed a day, and she was right about the day and she was right about the bell and I was right about the lateness, and all three of those are on the page and none of them is a win.*
>
> *A record has to be kept somewhere. A record is a shelf and a man and about eleven thousand lines, and it is two days down the water, and there is a town with a clerk's hall in it, and I have been told the name of it by nobody and I am not going to have it given to me, because he is right, and because the only way I have found anything in five weeks is by asking somebody a question they were not expecting, and I am not going to be handed a town by an auditor who is being kind to me in a room on his last day in it.*
>
> *So: the river goes down. It takes two days. I have a barrow and a frame and a receipt with five clauses in it and a bell in a crate that a master in this town certified in his own hand with the hour, and I have a name to look at in a room I have not found yet, and I have a fact about a day that nobody asked a woman for.*
>
> *And I am going down the water with nothing to say to anybody, which is a thing I have never once set out to do in my life, and I have not rung the bell since the fifty-second, and I am not going to ring it on a river.*

He shut the book and put it under the mattress, which he had never done, and he was careful about that too, and could not have said why.
