# Chapter 0087 — A Name On A Roll Four Counties Away

The thing he wanted on the morning of the seventy-third was a place, and there was no such thing in this town.

He had known that for about a fortnight and had not done anything about it, and the reason he had not done anything about it was that everything else he had wanted had turned into a sentence somebody else had already said, and about six days ago he had worked out that a man who keeps asking for the wrong thing in four counties is not looking for the thing, he is looking for the asking.

So he got up and counted the bolts, which is four, and made the frame fast, and went up the street.

---

Tamsin Rook was at the end of the yard with her back to a wall and her slate under her arm, doing the thing she does at a landing, which is nothing, in public, for a living.

“I want a town name,” he said.

“You have four counties of towns.”

“I have a name in my head from a market three days ago and I do not have the town with it, and if I say the name to a stranger in this town I am going to be asked whose name it is.”

“Then do not say the name.”

“I am not going to say the name. **Ashlade. Four counties up.** A woman at a fish pitch said it out loud on the seventieth to a woman she was in a line with, and she said the name belongs to somebody at that house and that the line there has not been answered this year or the year before, and that there is nobody at that house now who can answer it, and that it has gone about four years without a hand.”

Tamsin Rook wrote it down. She wrote it in the third column, and ruled it off, and did not merge it with anything.

“That goes in the third column and not the fourth,” she said. “**The fourth is for a thing nobody could answer. I have had one in it and I am not going to have two and it is not going to be a habit.**”

“How many in the fourth.”

“One. And I have not improved it and I am not going to, and if you ask me what it says I will read it to you and if you ask me twice I will not read it to you again.”

“I was not going to ask you twice.”

“You were thinking about it in the road and I could see it and I am telling you now so that you have not asked.” She put the chalk in her pocket. “Go and ask somebody about your town. **And when you come back, do not tell me the answer, because I did not write the name down to find a place. I wrote it down because a woman said it in a market and I have been asking at doors for eleven years and I have never once in my life had a name handed to me by a woman in a queue who wanted nothing for it, and I did not want to lose it.**”

That is the whole of the exchange and it took about four minutes and neither of them improved it afterwards.

---

The clerk's hall is the biggest building in this town that is not a church, and Tomas Vale had learned that on the fifty-eighth at about the seventh hour of the morning, and had learned the rest of it at about nine the same evening, and the rest of it was that a clerk's hall is the one building in a town of two thousand people a man may not walk into, and that the reason has nothing to do with the building and everything to do with the fifth clause of a receipt he had signed in a watch house in another county, and that the clause is not even about this building, it is about him, and there is nobody in this town who can waive it.

He got to the front of it at about the seventh hour of the morning and stood in the street, and the door was open, and it was the fourth hour of nothing, and he stood there until his legs told him about it.

**That was the resistance and it was not the door.** The door was open and there was a notice on the wall about nine feet inside it and he had used the notice once and it had cost him the better part of a morning and it had got him three sentences in three hands and the whole of a clerk's own trade. He could go in. **What he could not do was ask.**

Because he had asked. Nine times in a street on the fifty-eighth, and once properly at a counter on the sixty-second, and a man had put a sentence on a sheet nobody asked him for on the sixtieth, and a woman had answered him on a landing two days ago and given him a paper and had been poorer for it since the sixtieth, and he had four people in this town who were all right and none of whom was a door, and he had run out of buildings before he ran out of questions, and that was new, and it was on the seventy-third that it stopped being a discovery and became the ordinary condition of his life.

Ivo Trant came out to the step with a broom in his hand and looked at him for the length of two breaths.

“**You have not asked me anything in four days and I have noticed, and I have decided not to make anything of it, and I want it noticed that deciding not to make something of it is the whole of what I have done about you and it took me about nine seconds.**”

“I have not come about anything you can answer.”

“Then you have come to the front of my hall to tell me you have nothing, and I have been at this door twenty-two years and that is new.” The broom went down against the jamb. “**You can go in. You can ask. The notice means it and it has meant it since before I came. What you cannot do is stand here and be looked at, because there is a woman at the cross who has been looking at you since you came round the corner and she has told two people, and by the fourth hour of the afternoon this will be the third person, and in about four days it will be a thing a stranger knows about before he has opened his mouth.**”

“I had not thought of that.”

“**Nobody thinks of it. That is why the notice is the only thing in this building that anybody has ever used and I have never once had to defend it.**”

---

Bryn Pethick was at the counter and had ink on the side of his hand again, and put a chair at the wrong side of it, which Tomas Vale had now seen him do twice and had begun to understand was the whole of his opinion.

“Sit down. **You have come with a town.**”

“I have come with a town called Ashlade, which is four counties up, and with a name that a woman in a market says belongs to a house there, and a line that has not been answered this year or the year before. **I am not asking for the roll. I am not asking for a book, a page, a copy, or a sight of a shelf. I am asking one question, and I told the man at the door I told it to and I am telling you so there is no mistake about it in about four seconds.**”

“All right.”

“**Is there anything in this building about a household in Ashlade.**”

And the clerk answered it in about four seconds and Tomas Vale had to put his hands on his knees.

“**No.**

“**And I am going to tell you why no, and I am going to tell you the whole of why, and you are not going to like about four inches of it. A roll in this hall is a book of answers. It is this district's answers, and this district is this town and the ground about it, and a line is a line in this district or it is not a line in this hall. Ashlade is four counties up and there is about eleven hundred of us between here and it, and not one of them is in this district, and there is no form that crosses eleven hundred people and there never has been.**”

“Then where does a line about Ashlade live.”

“**In Ashlade.** In a hall like this one with different men in it, on a shelf like this one, and if it has not been answered this year then it is on that shelf and it is not on this one, and I have never seen it and I cannot tell you a thing about it and I am not going to guess at a thing about it in front of you, because a guess that is said out loud in this building is a guess somebody repeats in a fortnight.**”

He squared a sheet, and stopped, and then did not square anything.

“**Now. You are going to ask me the other one, and I am going to answer it, and I have already worked out what I am going to say and I would like to say it before you ask so that you know I have thought about it, and that is a thing I do not do and I do not know why I am doing it today.**”

“I have not asked it.”

“**Where does a name go.**” He said it like a price. “**And the answer is that a name has to go somewhere, because there is a number against the line and a number has to mean something to somebody, and if the number meant nothing to anybody then it would not be a number, it would be a mark, and a mark is cheaper and I have never in four years seen a mark where there was a number.**”

“And the number means what.”

“**That is the whole of what I know and it is nine words.** He put his finger on a column and did not touch the paper. “**The names go up.**”

“Up.”

“**The answers are ours. The names are not. The answers come in on paper from a round and they go on a shelf here and a clerk enters them and a clerk closes them and the hall holds about nine hundred lines a year and about forty pounds of wages and nobody has ever asked me one question about the arithmetic, and I have offered it to about four people.**” He looked up. “**Up. To the district. And I have never been upstairs.**”

“The district has been shut since the sixty-seventh.”

“**The district is always shut. There is no district in this hall, and there has not been a district in this hall in four years, and the officer is a person who comes on a round and goes again, and what she does with a number between the first of a round and the last of it is between her and a building over a dyer's, and there is no form in this kingdom that says what she does with it, and I have looked for one at three different hours on two different days and there is not one and I did not think that was anybody's secret, I thought it was simply that nobody has had to write it down.**”

Tomas Vale sat on a chair at the wrong side of a counter in a town of about two thousand people and had the thing he had come for, and it was a shape with a hole in it.

“Then the names are in two rooms over a dyer's on the north side.”

“**Or they are not, and I have never been, and I have never asked, and if you go up those stairs you will find three books open on a table and a fire out of about a week ago, because I have looked at the room from the bottom of the stair once and I do not do that.**”

---

And then he said the sentence, because it had been in him for four days and because there was nothing else in him.

“**What is the document?**”

Bryn Pethick put the pen down, and did not take eleven seconds this time, and did not pause.

“It is a record of the answer given, entered in the answering column against the line, in the order in which the answers were given. It is the only thing it is. It is not a household's answer and it is not a person's answer and it is not a copy of either one, and the office obtains an answer from a person and the entry is made by the office, and the office is not in dispute between anybody in this room.”

Then he said a thing he had not planned to say and had to stop and start again on.

“**I said that to you on the sixty-second in about nine seconds and I did not write it down, and I have thought about that in the last four days, and I have decided I was wrong to save the ink.**”

“You said you would not write things down.”

“I said I would not write down things that were mine. **This is not mine. It is the office's, and it is on a sheet of this office's paper, and there is a column on that paper for it, and the column is empty, and it has been empty for four years, and a clerk who has an office's position in his hand and no line in the office's column is a clerk who is keeping a thing in his head, and I have read about four hundred sheets off that counter and I have never once been able to say which of them were in a man's head.**”

He wrote it standing up, on the hall's own paper, with the hour against it, and it took him less time than the talking had, and he signed it and gave it across, and it is the fourth sheet in a stranger's own name that Tomas Vale has been given by a person who did not have to give it to him.

“**No second copy,**” Bryn Pethick said. “You have not asked and I am not going to offer and I would like it noticed that the first time you came in here you asked for a copy of a line and I said no, and that was the correct answer then and it is the correct answer now, and I have never once wished it was a different one.”

---

And then Tomas Vale did the only thing he had left, which was the thing he had done at Fourmile on the fiftieth and in a room over a haberdasher's shop on the fifty-fifth and at a counter on the sixty-second, and it took about four seconds and it changed the shape of the morning.

“**Has anybody ever asked you that.**”

Bryn Pethick looked at him.

“**What.**”

“Where the names go. Not you. **Has anybody, in four years, in this building, asked any of the nine of you where the names go.**”

And the clerk said, “**No.**”

And Tomas Vale asked it a second way, because he had learned in five weeks that the shape of a question is half of what is in a room.

“**And nobody has asked you what the number means.**”

“**No.**”

And the second no was not the same no, and Tomas Vale knew it inside about a second and a half and it went through him like a hand in a sill.

**The first no meant *nobody has ever asked me*, and it is a thing a building says about itself and it is true and it is on a wall in nine words and everybody has read it. The second no meant *nobody has ever asked me, and you have now, and I know what I am going to do, and I am going to do nothing*, and it is the same word and it is not the same sentence and the difference between them is the whole of what has happened to this town in six weeks.**

“Yes,” said Tomas Vale.

“**I am going to do nothing.** I am going to go back to the answering column at about the eleventh hour and I am going to enter about nine hundred lines a year into a column in a paper, and I am not going to go upstairs, and I am not going to ask the officer, and I am not going to ask the man in charge of the shelves, and I am not going to go to Ashlade, and I am not going to write the word *up* in any book in this building.**

“**And I am right, and you know I am right, and I would like it written down that I was asked and that I said no, and I am not writing it down, because a clerk's name in a book is the only thing in the room that can be used against him, and I have one child and a house and I have been in this hall eleven years.**”

He put the pen down.

“That is the whole of it and it took about forty seconds and I have not said it to anybody in four years and I have not said it to you because you asked me, and I want that both ways, and now go out of my light because I have nine lines in front of me.”

---

He went out into the street at about the sixth hour, and there was a man at a stall on the corner selling nothing in particular who said the word *upstairs* twice at a woman and did not know who he was talking to, and he stood in the street and worked out that he had been in that hall for about an hour and three quarters and had learned the shape of a hole.

Then he walked about two hundred yards and stopped, because there was a step, and because his hands were cold, and because he had been carrying a sentence since the fortieth that had just turned out to be a thing with a handle and a cost on it and he wanted to sit down and find out which.

**He had carried it thirty-three days.** A woman on a road at about the sixth hour of the fortieth day had asked him what the document was and he had written the question down in a book because he was a careful man and it was the best question anybody had ever asked him. And he had put it in four counties, and he had put it in four buildings, and a clerk of four years had answered it in nine seconds, and a clerk of eleven years had put it on a sheet with the hour on it and said *I did not write that and I think it is right*, and an auditor had answered it in a room over a haberdasher's shop and told him to write down that his head was not a record, and a district officer had told him a ground could not say a thing.

**And a clerk had answered it on the sixty-second in nine seconds, standing up, and had gone back to a column, and had not thought about it again, and had been right.**

The sentence was a true sentence about a document. That is all it had ever been.

**And Tomas Vale had taken it out of nine seconds and carried it for thirty-three days and put it in four buildings and a book, and a man with a barrow in this town had said *I have been in a clerk's hall and been told what a document is*, and about four hundred people in this county were going to hear about that by the end of the week, and every one of them was going to hear it as a key.**

He sat on the step and got the day-book out and looked at the four columns, and the third one was the one a person's own words went into, and the fourth was the one a thing nobody could answer went into, and he had filled the second of those on the sixty-second in a man's hand about the hand on a line, and it was the only genuinely dangerous thing in the book.

Then he looked at what he had actually done with the sentence, and it was this.

**He had made it a record.** A record is a sentence other people may read. He had been told that in a room on the sixty-sixth, standing against a wall, by a clerk who had put a hand against a line in nine seconds, and it had gone into him as a discovery, and it was the most useful thing anybody had said to him in a month.

And a column is a stamp and a stamp is a hand and a hand is a person, and a person in a column is a person that the column is about, and **Tomas Vale was the person the column was about, and he had not put himself there. He had put a sentence there and the sentence had put him in.**

He wrote at about the eighth hour of the evening, on the step of the draper's, in the flat hand, with the hours, and he filled the first and third columns and put four lines in the second and did not merge two of them:

> *The seventy-third. I asked a man a question in a building this morning and I have carried the question for thirty-three days and it took him nine seconds, and I am going to write down the finding in the plainest words I have and then I am not going to improve it.*
>
> ***The sentence was never a door. I have been carrying it as one since the fortieth, in four counties, into four buildings, and a clerk said it to me the second time in nine seconds standing up and then went back to a column and has not thought about it since. It is a true sentence about a document. That is the whole of what it is. I made it into a key because I had nothing else to carry and I am the man in this town who is known for having been told what a document is, and that is not a thing anybody wanted and I have not heard of a household that wanted it.***
>
> *And the harder half, and I have checked this twice and I am not going to put it anywhere else because I want it in one place: **the habit is mine and not theirs. I have been blaming four counties for not being a door and they were never doors, and the reason I could not find out was that a sentence in nine seconds is not a door, and a man with a barrow and a month of nothing to carry will make a door out of anything that is true and holds still.***
>
> *And the habit is a column and a stamp. That is what it is and I have not got a better word for it and I am not going to look for one. **I went to a clerk's hall this morning and got a sheet of paper with a position on it in a clerk's hand with the hour against it, and it is the same shape as nine hundred lines a year in a column with a stamp at the foot, and mine has my name at the head of it, and the reason it does is that a sheet with a person's own name on it is a thing a person is read by, and I have spent thirty-three days learning what happens to a person a thing is read by.***
>
> *And the thing I went for. **Ashlade, four counties up. There is nothing in this building about Ashlade and there never was, and a name goes up, and up is a district office in two rooms over a dyer's that has been shut since the sixty-seventh with three books open on a table, and a clerk of eleven years has never been upstairs and is not going to be, and is right, and told me so at about the sixth hour in his own hearing and asked for it to be written down that he had said no.***
>
> *And I have not got a place. **A boy of eight, taken six days before the day any of this started, is a name. And a name is not a place. A name is not in this building and not in any building and not in Ashlade; it is in about nine thousand kitchens and about nine thousand heads in four counties and there is no book of it anywhere in this kingdom and there never has been and there is no form for one.***
>
> *And the whole of what I have learned in thirty-three days of carrying a sentence is the sentence that tells me what to do about it: **the only thing that has ever gone to a place to find out whether a person is in it is a person on foot with a bag.** I have got a bag. I have got a barrow I cannot pay with and a frame in it that cannot be rung and a receipt with five clauses in it and thirteen documents in a coat, and there are four counties, and no book, and a woman walking beside me who said nine words on a landing yesterday and has not asked me for anything and is not going to be thanked for them.*
>
> *And I am not angry and I have not been able to be angry at anything in this town for about ten days and I have gone looking three times, and there is nothing here to be angry at. **There is a clerk who is right, and a man in charge of shelves who is right, and a district officer who was right on a landing and gave me a paper and is poorer for it, and a woman at a fish pitch who gave me a town for nothing, and about eleven thousand of us doing nine minutes a year each, and not one of them is an agent and not one of them has ever been punished and there is nobody in the whole of it.***
>
> *I am going to shut this book. Nothing was rung in this town today, the frame is four streets off with four bolts in it, the crate is in a smith's yard with the sacking off, and all five clauses stand and I have not written the fifth one today because today I did not need to.*
