# Chapter 0066 — Asking For A Bell In The Open

They came over the stone at about the seventh hour of the morning of the fifty-first, and the stone has a letter cut into the west face and a different letter cut into the east, and the west face is the letter for the county they had spent nine days walking through, and Tamsin Rook stood on the east side of it with her bag on her shoulder and did not go on for a while.

The barrow was behind her with the cradle on the boards and the four bolts made fast where the frame was set down, and the frame had not been out of the cradle for eleven days and was not going to be out of it for anything.

She was doing arithmetic. She had been doing it since the forty-fourth.

The thirty-fifth, and a woman at a water bell saying *nine days*, and the nine days ran out on the forty-fourth, and the forty-fourth was a day she had watched him spend, and on the forty-fifth he said out loud in a ferry-man's hearing that the boat went on the tide at the top of the light. She had had that number in her head for seven days and had not said it, and she was not going to say it, and she wanted to be very clear with herself about why.

It was not hers. That was the whole of it, and it took her seven days to be sure of it. The nine days were a term she had set and he had accepted, and a term that runs out is a term that has run out, and it is not hers to extend it after the fact because she has noticed, and it is not hers to waive, and there is nobody in this kingdom she could hand it to. If she mentioned it she would be a woman pointing at a man's arithmetic in front of a smith's yard, and she had watched people do that to other people for eleven years from the other side of a barrow.

So she had said nothing, and now she was standing on a stone with two counties behind her, and the arithmetic had followed her over it.

The road on the east side is a better road. That is not a thing anybody chose. It is that a river road and a market town make their own weight, and the county line falls where it falls, and the four letters on the stone are the only thing anybody voted on.

---

Amos Trewin's yard is a hundred and forty feet of frontage and a wall on the west side and a smithy that is not a smithy in the trade sense, because there is no fire in it that could do the work, and Trewin said so before she had got the bag off her shoulder.

“Two things,” he said, “before you say anything, and they are the same two I said to the man on the barrow, and you may tell him I said them again.

“I am not a master of the Bellwrights' Measure and I have never been in that house and I cannot certify anything at all. Not a bell, not a staple, not a hinge. And I will not cast anything, and there is no fire in this shop that could do it, and I have not cast a bell in nineteen years and I am not going to start.”

“Then I have come to the wrong shop again.”

“You have come to a shop that sells metal and knows where metal comes from,” said Trewin, “which is a different thing and a smaller thing and about nine times in ten the thing a person actually wants. Sit down. I have customers at eight and you can talk to me in front of them, because I do not say one thing to a Crown man and another to a woman with a bag, and it saves me about nine minutes a week.”

There were six of them in the shop by then, waiting on benches, and Tamsin Rook took the stool at the anvil end, which is the end a person from the road is allowed.

She had it ready. She had had it ready since the boat.

“All right,” she said. “Is there any law against a man buying a bell.”

The shop went quieter in a way she felt rather than heard. Trewin put down a hammer he had not been using and turned round, and the six of them looked at him, because in that shop a thing said badly by a stranger is a thing the owner answers and not the stranger.

“No,” said Trewin.

He said it the way a man says the first half of a sentence, and then he came the rest of the way across the shop, which took him a while, because he is not a small man and there were benches in the way.

“There is no law against a man buying a bell. There is a bar on a bell. **The bar is on the bell and it is in the county you have just walked out of and you are standing in a different one, and the bar was entered on the twenty-sixth of this month against one bell and not against your man, and I would be obliged if you would let a room finish before you open the next question in it.**”

“Was it entered against one bell.”

“It was entered against one bell. I have read the notice. Everybody in that county has read the notice and about four of them have understood it, and I have read it and understood it, and you have not, and that is not a disgrace, it is a notice.” He put the hammer down on the anvil and did not pick it up again. “And I will tell you the half of it that nobody in your county is telling you, because you have a man on a barrow who has been reading a receipt at people for a fortnight and I have watched him do it, and he is not the only one who reads.

“**A bar is on a bell and a bell is a thing you carry.** If a man carries a barred bell over this stone he has brought it into a county where it is not barred on paper and it is barred in every shop in this county that has ever been asked, and I would not stand in that position with my name on a bill and I would not advise a man to stand in it. That is not law. Law stops at this stone. That is trade, and trade has never once needed a court to do it and it does it anyway, and it will be doing it to your man about four months from now whatever paper he is holding.”

“Then a bell can be barred in a county it has never been in.”

“A bell can be barred anywhere,” said Trewin, “by anybody who hears about it, and the paper will say the opposite, and the paper is what you will have and the shop is what you will have when you try to sell it again.”

He went back to the anvil, and the shop started up again, and Tamsin Rook sat on the stool at the anvil end of a smith's shop eleven miles into a county she had not known existed a week before, and she was not going to say the rest of it, and she had already said the first part of it badly in front of six people who were not her problem.

She had wanted the room to hear a man kept from a thing. What the room had heard was a woman who could not find the sentence.

“Right,” she said. “Then say the whole of it to me and I will keep it. What is the law about bells in this county.”

“There is no law about bells in this county. There is a Measure, and the Measure is a company of masters, and a company may bar its own work and may bar other work, and it does it in open rooms with about eleven people in them, and it writes the reason down, and the reason is always a reason you could argue with if you were in the room.” He looked at her. “**That is the thing your man is not going to get and is not going to try for, because he wants a door, and there is no door, and there are about nine masters between his barrow and a certificate and every one of them is a man with a shop and a book and no reason to lose either.**”

“Then who certifies in this county.”

“Masters come round. There is one in Bramden for the market, and the market is three days, and he has a room over the saddler's on the north side for the three of them, and you cannot walk into it, and I will not take you up, and I will tell you why, and it is the third thing I have said to you and it is the last.” He wiped his hands. “I have eleven customers in this week who cannot write and if a stranger walks in behind them with a question about a bell then every one of them is standing in a shop where a Crown matter is being discussed, and they will not come again, and they have to come. **I am not refusing you because you are Crown. I am refusing you because you are a question, and I have customers, and the two do not go in the same room.**”

---

Bramden is a market town of about eleven hundred and it is on the water, and the landing is a stone slope with a crane on it and about nine boats, and the market is on a square with a market cross on it and about four rows of pitch and awning and it runs three days, from the fifty-first to the fifty-third, and the last of the three is the day the whole town is in the square, and the river post is the fastest thing on the reach and goes down on the second of a month and comes up on the fourth, and the down boat is away from the Bramden slip at about the eleventh hour and does not wait, which is a thing she learned in about ninety seconds from a carter on the landing and which she wrote on the back of her hand in pencil because she is the kind of woman who will need it on the way back.

The room over the saddler's shop is two rooms. The lower one is a shop and sells harness and does repairs and the back of it is a bench with a fire in it and eleven men on it by about the ninth hour, and the stair is outside and the door at the top is shut.

Tamsin Rook put her bag down on the top step, where anybody coming up could see it, and knocked.

The man who opened the door had grey in his beard and no particular expression at all and he looked at the bag on the step and then at her face and did not ask about either.

“Rowan Ketch,” he said. “You are not on my book.”

“No. And I am not here about a making. I have three things and the first one is that I am going to say all of them out loud in this room and the second one is that I am going to write the third one down and sign it, and if you would rather I went away I will go away now and you may say so and I will not come back this week.”

“Somebody always says that,” said Rowan Ketch. “Come in. Mind the step, it is the step and not the door.”

---

The room was two tables, a bench along one wall, a window with the sacking out of the bottom, and about eleven men and women in it, and about half of them Tamsin Rook could put to a trade inside a minute and the other half she could not.

The fire was in a bad state and somebody had put a chair against the draught.

“You have said three things,” said Ketch. “I have heard one and a half.”

“I have said what I am not here about, which is a making, and that I will write the third thing down. That is the one and a half.” She put her hands on the table. “The second is that I am going to read two documents out loud in this room, and both of them have been read at me and neither of them is mine, and one of them is the property of the Crown and I am going to read it in a room of tradesmen because the man it is about is standing in Bramden with a barrow and does not know how to ask a room a question, and I have asked it for him, and I am not going to pretend to anybody that I asked it for him.

“The first document is a custody receipt in five clauses. It was signed at about the fifth hour of the morning of the thirty-third in the hand of a watch captain in a town called Kells. I am going to read all five and then somebody may say a word about any of them.”

And she read it.

She read it well. She had heard a man read it three times and she had read it once herself on a landing stage in a different month, and she gave the whole of it, in order, with the hours in it, and she did not editorialise between the clauses, and when she came to the fourth she made her voice exactly as flat as it had been in an inn yard with eleven people in it, and when she came to the fifth she did not slow down at all, because slowing down on the fifth clause is what people do.

> *Released as the same object. The retaining bolts are for the carriage only and are to be made fast at every place at which the frame is set down. The object is not to be rung and no clapper is to be furnished for it by any person.*
>
> *The frame remains in its reinforced padded cradle for the carriage.*
>
> *It is to be produced at the request of the district, or of the watch, or of the market court, and a request may be made by any person and not only by an officer.*
>
> *The maker is not the keeper, has no claim, cannot certify it, and may not say what it means.*
>
> *This receipt does not make the holder a keeper of any route, and no answer to any signal carried on or by the object is to be entered as anybody's answer for a household of this ward, and the holder has no standing to speak for any household in Kells or in any ward.*

The fire had gone out entirely by the end of it and nobody had got up to do anything about it.

“A receipt for a bell in a frame,” said Ketch, “that cannot be rung, cannot have a clapper, cannot leave its cradle, may be demanded by anybody at all, and whose holder is forbidden on pain of nothing whatever from saying what it means.” He looked at the window. “I have been in this trade a long time and I have never seen a piece of paper drawn against the trade. I would like to say that it is well drawn, and I would like to say it more than I expect anybody here wants me to.”

“You may say it,” said Tamsin Rook. “It is well drawn. It is the best piece of paper I have read in a month and I have read about four hundred of them, and the woman who made the fifth clause out of the third one is a person I have never met and I would like her to know it is the only clause anybody has ever been afraid of.”

She turned round then, to the room, because that was the second reason she had come up the outside stair and she was not going to lose it to a compliment.

“The second document is a finding. It is dated the twenty-sixth of this month and it is made by the Bellwrights' Measure, and the Measure is this room's own company and I am going to read it here because that is where it belongs and because I would like about eleven people to hear it said out loud by the person it concerns, and it is four lines long, and I have it because the man with the barrow has a copy and he gave it to me to read out and not to keep, which is the difference between a person and a clerk and he made it himself.

“It says: the origin of the bell cannot be certified. The maker may not ring it in a public place in this county. No certificate can issue. **The bar is on the bell and not on the licence.**”

She put the four lines down on the table face up, the way you put a card down.

“I have one more thing,” she said, “and then I will get out of the room and let a man do his work. Ask it in whatever words a master of this company thinks a question ought to be asked in, and answer it in your own name, and I will write your answer down exactly as you say it, and then I will go downstairs and leave the men in the saddler's shop alone, which is what I came up here to arrange and did not think of until I was on the step.”

Ketch looked at her for a while.

“That was a good place to put it,” he said, “and I have been in rooms for thirty years and I have never been handed one that was handed to me like that. You want a paper and I have no paper for this, and I will tell you that before you ask, because the not-telling is the thing that costs people.”

“Then answer it out loud and I will make the paper.”

“I can do that. Sit down.”

She sat down.

“**A man may buy a bell he has not made.**” Rowan Ketch said it to the room and not to her, and then he turned and said it again to the man at the end of the bench who had a pair of lasts in front of him and had not moved for eleven minutes, because that man was the only one in the room who had gone the colour of the wall. “Any person may. There is no standing, no parish, no district, no years, and no entry in any book of this company, and I could not stop him if he had the money in a bag, and if he has not the money in a bag that is between him and the man who sells to him.

“A bell may be certified by a master where the making is complete. **The making is five things and I will say them because half this room thinks it is four. The mould. The pattern. The mark. The date. And the hand.** All five, and all five on the same bell, and the hand is the man who cast it and not the man who bought it and not the man who sold it. A bell with all five may be certified by any master in this company, **including by a master who did not make it, and the great majority of the certificates I have signed in thirty years are on bells I did not make.** A bell without one of the five may not be certified and may never afterwards be made certifiable, and the want attaches to the bell and not to the man, and he may keep it and ring it in a field for the rest of his life and it will still be what it is.

“And the metal is not a mystery to be guessed at. If the man who sold it to you kept a book and the book says where the metal came out of, and that man's man kept a book, and that book says the same, and the chain is long enough and unbroken, then the metal is as traceable as a road. **If the chain is broken anywhere then the bell is as good as a story, and there is no master in this company who will sign it, and there is no amount of asking me in a room that will change it.**”

The last man on the bench said, in a voice that had gone, “Is that the answer, then.”

“That is the answer. And I will tell you the half of it that makes it no use to a man, because a man who has been refused four times in a month does not need to be flattered in a room. **Traceable is not the same as good.** A bell with five on it is a bell that can be proved, and a bell that can be proved is a bell that somebody can stand in front of later and say *this is the same bell, and here it is, and here is who had it, and here is what was said on it.* And there is not one of us in this room who has not wanted that about a thing we have done, and there is not one of us who would want it about the work of our own hands. **That is what a certificate is. It is a promise to a stranger that a thing is what you say it is, and it is only worth the making to you and never once to him.**”

---

He wrote it out on a sheet of the circuit paper afterwards, at the table by the window, and put the hour in, and signed it, and did not look at it again. It is four lines. It is signed **Rowan Ketch, master, for this company, this town, this date**, and there is no seal and no counter-signature and no copy left behind in the room, which was her doing and not his; she asked for it and he said there is no second one and she said then I will take the one.

And when she had it in her hand she put her own pen on the table and drew a sheet of the same paper towards her and wrote the question out, and she wrote it exactly as she had said it in the room and not as she would have put it if she had thought about it for a day, and then she wrote under it:

*Asked by Tamsin Rook, of no parish in this county, at the ninth hour of the morning of the fifty-first, in a room over a saddler's shop in this town, before about eleven persons, and answered by Rowan Ketch, master, in his own name, in his own hand, with the hour.*

And she signed it and gave it to him.

He read the bottom of it twice, which she watched him do.

“May I say what that costs you,” he said, “before you have it back?”

“You may say it.”

“You have just made yourself findable.” He gave her the sheet back and did not give her his. “Not to me. To whoever holds the book. A question with a name and an hour and a room on it is a thing a clerk cannot refuse to answer in about a year, and a thing a clerk cannot refuse is a thing a clerk must do something about, and there is nothing in the five lines you have just written that says you are asking for a thing. It only says that on the fifty-first of this month, in a room, a person asked and a master answered. And that is a complete record and it is enough. **Anything with a date and a room in it is enough, and I have been on circuit for thirty years and I have never once been asked to say a thing I did not mean, and nobody has ever thought to write my answer down and sign it and hand it to the person who asked, and now one has, and I would do the same again and I still want you to know what it is.**”

“I know what it is,” said Tamsin Rook.

“Yes. And you wrote your own name on it before you knew that, which is the part I have not got past and have stopped trying to get past.”

She stood up and put the sheet in her pocket, and then took it out again and looked at it, and he watched her do it.

“I did it on purpose,” she said.

“I have no doubt you did.”

“I want to be exact about it, because I have been on the road eleven years and I have never once put a name to a thing and had it used.” She put it back in her pocket. “He reads a receipt out loud in lanes and there is no name on the receipt, and everybody says *that is the man with the barrow*, and it has been him for a month, and it has been him in four counties and nobody could put a single thing on him. And this morning in a room of eleven tradesmen there was going to be a master saying yes to somebody and I have watched men in this trade for a month and every single one of them was going to look at the door when he said it, because they all know what happened to a bell in a yard once. **So I signed it before he said it, so that it would not be his.**”

The room had gone very quiet, and there were eleven men and women in it and not one of them looked at the door, and Tamsin Rook stood at the end of a table with a bell-founder's certificate in one pocket and a piece of her own handwriting in the other and understood, about four seconds later and in front of everybody, that she had just built herself a fifth clause.

“Then I have one thing to say and I am not going to soften it,” said Rowan Ketch, “because you have been straight with this room for an hour and you do not get a soft answer at the end of it.

“**You came up an outside stair to make sure a man was not blamed for something, and you have made certain that from today there is a piece of paper with a name on it that a man in an office may read, and you did it to protect him, and the name on it is yours.**”

“That is correct.”

“I am not saying it was wrong. I am saying it was a decision and you made it in about four seconds and you made it about somebody else, and everybody who has ever been in a room and got this wrong has done it in four seconds about somebody else.” He put his pen in his pocket. “It is not a cage. A cage is a thing somebody else wrote. That is a thing you wrote, tonight, in your own hand, and you may unwrite it by not signing the next one, and that is the only difference and it is the whole of the difference and I would not want a person in this trade to go eleven years without knowing it exists.”

---

They came down the outside stair at about the eleventh hour and the sun was out and the square was doing what a market square does, and Amos Trewin was in it, in his own shop's account of himself, standing at the north end with his back to a wall and about six people round him and one of them a woman with a slate.

He had been there since about the ninth hour, and he had not gone and looked for a bell, and he had not sent anybody, and when she got to him he said, without being asked:

“Master Ketch comes on market weeks and he is in that room on market weeks and I have known that for nineteen years and I did not tell you this morning because you asked me a question about a man being kept from a thing and I answered the question you asked.”

“You did answer it.”

“I did, and it was a true answer and it was not the answer to the question you had, and I have been doing that for nineteen years and the only reason I mention it is that you have just come down that stair looking like a woman who has been given something.”

“I have been given a piece of paper with my own name at the bottom of it,” said Tamsin Rook, “and I would like to walk to the landing and put it somewhere dry, and then I would like to stand on a slip of stone for about a quarter of an hour, and then I would like to come back and tell him he cannot afford a bell.”

“You cannot afford a bell.”

“He can afford a barrow and a receipt and a day-book and about fourpence, and he is going to be asked for a pound and something, and he has told me so himself in a room full of people a week ago, and I have been doing the other sum since we came off the boat.” She looked at the square. “And the second thing I have learned this morning, and it is not a small one, and I would like it written down somewhere before I say it out loud to a man who writes everything down, and I am not going to say it to him this afternoon because he will put it in a book and then it will be a thing that was said on the fifty-first, and I would like it to be a thing I noticed on the fifty-first and did not hand over until the second.

“It is that a man may buy a bell, and a master may certify it, and a smith will sell the metal with a record behind it, and every one of those three people is a person and not an office, and I have been in a room today where eleven people agreed with each other about a matter of craft inside about four minutes, and I have been on the road for a month asking questions at doors and offices and landings and I have not once got four minutes of that from any of them, and not one of them has a column and a stamp and a habit in it.”

She stopped, because a woman with a barrow had come up the slope off the landing and was standing at the bottom of it, waiting, not coming up, in the way of a person who has work and does not want to be thought to be listening.

“I am going to be careful about that,” she said. “There is a difference between a room that agrees and a room that is right, and I have not worked out which one I was in, and I would like a day with the square in it before I say it in a room again.”

The woman with the barrow said, “Are you the one that was up at the saddler's.”

“I was.”

“My man said a master answered a question and put a name to it. He wanted to know if it was worth the walk up.”

“It is worth the walk up,” said Tamsin Rook, “and it cost me more than the walk up, and if your man wants the name it is on a sheet at the saddler's and it is not mine to give and it is not his to give either, and he can read it standing on the bottom step, which is what I would do.”

And the woman went down the slope, and Tamsin Rook stood at the top of it and did not call after her, and after a while she said, to nobody, in a voice she did not intend to make carry:

“*I want to be exact about it, because I have been on the road eleven years and I have never once put a name to a thing and had it used.*”

And then she went and found him, because he had to be told that it is yes, and that it is a pound and something, and that the metal in it is worth three and four, and that he is going to be asked where the last one came from in a room in front of about eleven people and he is not going to be able to lie about it because there is nothing to lie with.

She did not say the last part. She saved it, because she wanted to see whether he would arrive at it by himself by the time she got to the bottom of the street, and he did, at the crossing, with the barrow's off wheel in his hand, and he said:

“Where the last one came from.”

“Yes.”

“In a room. In front of people.”

“In a room over a saddler's shop, in front of about eleven people, and the master is the man who certified a bell for somebody else in about nine seconds in front of the whole of them, and he will ask you that question because it is the question he asks everybody, and there is no answer you can give that sounds like anything but what it is.” She took the bag off her shoulder. “I have been up a stair for two hours and got a piece of paper with my own name on it and I would like to go and sit on a slip of stone by the water for a quarter of an hour, and I am telling you where I am going to be, and it is not on the slate, and I am not asking you to come, and I would like you to notice that I have told you the place and not asked you for anything at all.”

He looked at the square, and at the landing, and at the money that was not in his coat, and back at her.

“Tamsin,” he said. “How much is a bell.”

“A pound and something. And I am not saying it because I have it.”

“I know you are not saying it because you have it,” he said, “and I am going to go and find out what the something is, and you are going to go and sit on your stone, and at about the fourth hour of the afternoon I am going to go to a smith who has told me he will not cast anything and ask him the price of a thing he has not got the fire to make.”

He put the barrow's wheel chocked with a stone from the kerb and started up the north street with his hands in his pockets, and Tamsin Rook watched him go, and then she went down the slope to the water and sat on a slip of stone with a piece of paper in her pocket that was worth more to her than the answer in it, and did not take it out, and did not read it again, and stayed there about a quarter of an hour.

The bell is in the smith's stock, and the money is not in the man's coat, and the third of these three is not going to be fixed by either of the other two, and she knew that at the bottom of the stair and had known it since the boat.
