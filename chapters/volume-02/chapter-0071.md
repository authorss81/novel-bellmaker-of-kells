# Chapter 0071 — Down The Water

The post is the fastest thing on the reach and it goes down on the second of a month and comes up on the fourth, and the second was the fifty-second, and Tomas Vale did not want the fastest thing on the reach.

What he wanted was a boat that was not in a hurry, and there is one, and it is not much of anything: a carrier's boat that goes down when it has a load and takes two days and lies up at a landing overnight with the water in it, and it carries barrows because barrows do not walk. It had eleven things on it when it came alongside the slip at Bramden and two of them were his, or one of them was his and the other was the barrow, and the man on it looked at the frame in the cradle and did not ask about it and wrote *a frame* against it, which was more than a district had done for it in a month.

He had a crate as well, and the crate was the thing the morning went on.

---

It was at about the second hour of the afternoon on the fifty-sixth and the barrow was on the boards with the cradle on its bed and the four bolts made fast, and the crate was on the boards beside it with the sacking off because Tamsin Rook had said it would rot.

The man on the post had grey at the throat and a book with a wood cover, and he had written *a frame* and shut the book and was coming back along the boards with a line he did not appear to need, and he stopped at the crate.

“That one.”

“Yes.”

“What's in it.”

And there it was, the whole of the last two days of a river, in a question about a crate, on a slip, with a woman eleven feet off and a line in a man's hand.

The two answers were both true and only one of them was fit to be said.

The first one was: *A bell. I bought it of a smith four miles past a county stone and a master of the company certified it in his own hand with the hour on it and there is no claim in it.* That answer was correct in every particular and it was also a thing he had learned not to hand to a stranger on a river, because it puts a man who certifies bells into a boat with a man who has got a barred one in a cradle four feet away, and there is nothing in the world that makes that go well.

The second answer was the true one and he could not get it out.

**He had bought a bell to say something with, and he did not know what.** That was what was in the crate. Not the metal and not the mark and not the certificate. A thing he had paid a pound and fourteen shillings for out of another person's purse to have a mouth with, and he had been on the road twenty-four days with it and had not found out what it was for, and he had said so out loud in a square in front of about nine hundred people and had been right to.

**And a man who says that on a river is a man who has already told a stranger he has nothing.**

“I'll tell you what it is,” he said, “and I am telling you because you are going to write something down and I would rather it were in your book than in mine.”

“All right,” said the man.

“A bell. It is not mine. I did not make it and I cannot. **It has nothing in it.** It is not kept and I am not its keeper and I have no route and nobody has put a claim in it and there is no claim to put in it, and I have not rung it since the day I bought it, and I am not going to ring it on this river, and if you want to know what I am going to do with it I cannot tell you, because I have not found out.”

The man wrote it. Or he wrote *a bell* again, and Tomas Vale could not see the page, and the man shut the book and said:

“That's the honestest entry I've made on this reach in about nine years and it's going to be the shortest. **Load says a bell. A bell's a bell. I've got no business knowing what a man's going to do with it and neither have you.**”

“Yes,” said Tomas Vale.

“And I'll tell you the other thing, since you've been honest and it costs me nothing to be. **If your bell had a keeper's name in it I'd have had to ask.** A mark and a day and a man's name at the top of a page, that's a bell somebody is minding, and a bell somebody is minding is a bell I'm carrying for two, and I write down who I'm carrying for. **Nothing in it and nothing on it is the easiest cargo on this water and I have carried about nine of them in my life and every one of them went to a town where a person was waiting.**”

That was the whole of it and it took about four minutes and the man did not ask one question more, and he was right about the cargo and he had not the smallest idea what was in the cradle four feet away, and Tomas Vale wrote that down afterwards as the first transaction of his working life in which a man told him something true for nothing.

---

The second thing the man told him, at the same place, about ten minutes later, standing with the line in his hand and looking down the reach, was the reason the rest of the day went the way it went.

“You're going to Miremarket.”

“How do you know that.”

“**Because a barrow does not go to Miremarket.**” He shut the book again. “Not off me, and not off anybody. There is a clerk's hall in that town and there has been one there since before my master's father had the boat, and a clerk's hall is not a district office, and a river knows the difference, and it has known it longer than either of those two buildings has been standing.

“A carrier's book says what a thing is. A clerk's hall has a man in it who writes down what a thing is. **Two books about one barrow is a thing a carrier does not want.** Not because a clerk will come out and take it off you. Because a clerk is a man who has been asked before, and if a thing comes into Miremarket with my book on it and then turns up in a hall four days later, there is a man in that hall who now knows my book, and he will have an opinion about my trade, and I have four boats and one of them is paid for.”

“So it comes off at the landing.”

“It comes off at the landing, and the landing is **outside** the town, and it is a mile and a bit, and there is no clerk's hall between here and there and there is a boatyard at the end of it with about nine men in it who will take a barrow round a town for money and who do not keep a book of what they have taken round, and I will tell you why they do not keep one. **They are not a carrier's business. They are a man's legs and a handcart. Nobody has ever asked them what a thing is, because nobody has ever needed to know.**”

Tomas Vale stood on a slip with a barrow behind him and found that he had just been given the whole of the next two days by a man who was not on his side and had no reason to be on anybody's side.

“And the name of the town,” he said. “You said it and you have not asked me for anything for saying it.”

“You did not ask me for it,” said the man. “**That is how I have got it.** I have been putting a barrow into towns for thirty years and if you stand on a slip and ask a man the name of the town he is going to, he tells you and thinks nothing of it, and if you ask him what the town is like he will have a story. You have been doing it the other way round for a month and it has cost you a bell.”

---

Tamsin Rook had been eleven feet off the whole of it, which is where she stands, and she came along the boards when the line was made fast and did not ask and said:

“Say that part again. The bit about a clerk's hall not being a district office.”

“I have not got it in my head in your words and I am not going to pretend I have,” he said. “I have got the words *a river knows the difference* and I have got a mile and a bit of walking between the landing and the town, and I have got the name of the town off a man's book, and that is the whole of what I have and two thirds of it is a boatman's.”

“Fine. **What have you decided.**”

And he had decided it, and he had decided it in about nine minutes on a slip, and the reason it was decided was that he had nothing else to decide it with.

“I am not going in through the front of anything with a barrow in it,” he said. “I am going in as somebody's business, because that is true. **I am a carrier's man with a load.** That is not a lie. I have a barrow and a bell I did not make and a sheet of paper with a master's hand on it, and every one of those is a thing a man is carrying for somebody, including me, and the day I stop being a man carrying a load is the day I start being a man with a story, and I have been a man with a story for five weeks and it has cost me every room I have walked into.”

“And the barrow.”

“The barrow comes off at the landing. The frame does not leave the cradle, not at the slip, not at the landing, not at the boatyard, not in the yard. It is bolted at every place it is set down and it is bolted there tonight and it is bolted there tomorrow, and the receipt says it stays in the cradle for the carriage and it is going to stay in it until there is somewhere else for it to be, and that is not me being careful. That is the second clause, and the second clause is the reason I have not been able to pay for a bell with this barrow, and I have made my peace with the second clause.”

“And then there is the crate,” she said.

“And then there is the crate. **The crate goes in as a smith's consignment**, and the reason that is not a lie is that it is a smith's — it came out of a smith's cellar and it was put in a crate by a smith and a smith is a person a carrier's book already has an entry for, and a crate of metal in a crate off a smith is a line a man writes without looking twice at it. And if he looks twice I will tell him, and he will write *bell metal* and put a stroke after it, and that is a lie by omission, and I have not written it in my book and I am telling you it is a lie by omission and I have decided to do it.”

She did not say anything for long enough that the water moved twice under the boards.

“Then say the other one,” she said. “The one you have not said.”

He had been carrying it since the middle of the afternoon and he had been carrying it the way he had carried the thing about Ivet Slade, which is to say in the wrong order.

“I have five shillings in the whole of my pockets,” he said. “I counted them at about the fifth hour of the morning in a room over a draper's and it is the first time in about six weeks that what was in my pockets was the whole of what I had. And I am going to pay a man with a handcart two shillings and sixpence to take a barrow and a frame and a crate round a town to a yard he does not live at the end of, and I am going to pay the man whose yard it is a shilling for the corner of it and for not asking me what is bolted in it. **And I am telling you both of those now, before I do either of them, and not afterwards, and you are not going to tell me not to.**”

“No,” she said.

“You were going to say something.”

“I was going to say something and I have stopped and here it is, and you asked me for it by telling me in advance, which is the only way I have ever known a person to be decent about money. **I am not going to tell you not to, and I am not going to tell you that is the right thing either, and I want you to hear the shape of what I have just said, because it is the shape of everything I have said to you for a month.**”

“Go on.”

“Five weeks. Four counties. Four offices, and a room over a saddler's shop, and a front room with a copper in it, and nine hundred people in a square, and not one of them has ever cost you a penny and not one of them has given you a thing, and two of them gave you everything, and both of them did it because you had walked in with nothing in your hand but a sheet of paper and a reason for being there. **Nothing in the hand. That is the whole method. That is your entire trade and you have been doing it since the thirty-second and you have never once paid for it, and no wonder nobody has sold you anything.**”

“And now I am going to pay for it.”

“**Now you are going to pay for it,**” she said, “and I want the two halves kept apart in your book, because you will not keep them apart yourself, and then in about four days one of them will be the story and the other one will be gone. Here is the first half and it is true and it is yours: you have been refused by four offices for nothing. You have a receipt in your coat that says you have no standing in any ward in this kingdom in five places and one of the five you wrote yourself. Nothing you have done for five weeks has cost a living soul a farthing, and that is not because you are careful. **It is because you have been free, and a free man is a thing a town will talk to at any hour, and a man who pays is a thing a town has a price for.**”

“And the second half.”

“The second half is that a man who has been free for five weeks is going to be very good at paying, and he is not going to notice that he has changed, and in about a month he is going to be a man who knows what everything costs, and that is the most ordinary thing there is and there is nothing in this kingdom to stop it and there is not one clerk in any hall who has not been where you are going.”

She shouldered her bag and went and stood at the stern and looked at the wake for about four minutes and then came back and said one more thing, and it was not a warning and it was not an approval.

“You are not doing it for the frame and you are not doing it for the bell. **You are doing it because you have nothing to say to anybody and you have been refused for five weeks, and on the water a stranger is a man who can be paid to look away, and it is the first thing in five weeks that has been available to you and it is going to taste like something.** And I am not going to take it off you. I priced a bell in a smith's shop and I paid it with my eyes open and I have not been sick about it once, and I am not going to be sick about this either, and I would like that written down in a book in your own hand as a thing a person said on a river and not improved.”

---

The boat lay up at a landing that is not in any of this, on the first night, with the water under her and the barrow on the boards and the four bolts made fast where she was set down, and nothing was rung, and the receipt was whole and no clause of it was broken and none of them was mentioned.

He wrote for about an hour at about the eighth hour of the evening, on a plank, in the flat hand, in four columns, and it is in the day-book entire, and the fourth column is the one where he is allowed to contradict himself, and this entry is mostly in it.

> *The fifty-sixth. Down the water, with nothing to say to anybody, and I want it in the book that I said that out loud to a woman on a slip and she did not improve it and neither did I.*
>
> ***I bought a bell to say something with and I cannot say what.*** I said that to a man on a slip at about the second hour of the afternoon and it is the first time in six weeks I have said the whole of that sentence to a stranger and it took four seconds and it cost me nothing and it is the only free thing I have had since the thirty-second. I said it to a stranger because a stranger is a man with a book, and the man wrote *a bell* against the crate and shut the book and told me it was the shortest honest entry he had made in nine years, and then he told me that if there had been a keeper's name on it he would have had to ask, and a bell with a keeper's name in it is a bell two men are minding, and I have got a bell with nothing in it and it is the only piece of luck I have had since the twenty-sixth.
>
> *And a clerk's hall is not a district office and a river knows the difference, and the river has known it longer than either building has been standing, and a barrow does not go into a town with a clerk's hall in it, because a carrier's book says what a thing is and a clerk's hall has a man in it who writes down what a thing is, and two books about one barrow is a thing a carrier does not want.*
>
> *I have been asking offices for five weeks. I have never once asked a river. The river answered me at about the second hour of the afternoon and it did not want anything and it did not ask me a single question, and it gave me four things, and three of them are about how to get into a town and one of them is the name of the town, and I did not have to work for any of them.*
>
> ***MIREMARKET.*** *That is the name, off the wood cover of a book, on a slip, at the second hour of the afternoon of the fifty-sixth. Nobody gave it to me. Nobody willed it to me. It is in a carrier's book in a column headed for the place a load is going, and the man who wrote it wrote it about nine hundred times a year and was not thinking about me at all, and I have got a name for a place for the first time in a month out of a piece of paper, and I want it recorded that the method works and that it is the same method and that I have been using it in the wrong direction for five weeks.*
>
> *And the name is Miremarket, and it is about two thousand people, and there is a clerk's hall in it, and there is a market, and there is a landing a mile outside it with a boatyard at the end of the landing and about nine men at the boatyard who take a barrow round a town for money and keep no book of what they have taken round, because nobody has ever needed to know.*
>
> *And here is the thing I have got to write down before I get any further from the slip, and it is the whole of the fifty-sixth and I am not going to improve it.*
>
> ***I am going to pay a man to carry a barrow I could carry and to pay a man to say nothing about what is in it.*** *Two shillings and sixpence and a shilling, out of five shillings, and I am telling the woman I travel with both of those facts before I do either of them, and she is not going to stop me, and she was right that I would not have told her if I had not made myself, and she was right about the reason, and the reason is not the frame and it is not the bell.
>
> *Five weeks. Four counties. A bar on a bell in a sealed yard, a receipt with five clauses, and nothing that has cost one living soul anything, and the whole of why nothing has cost anybody anything is that I have been free. **A free man is a thing a town will talk to at any hour. A man who pays is a thing a town has a price for. That is the whole of what I have learned on the water in one afternoon and it is not a thing I can put in a receipt and it is the only thing I have learned since the thirty-second that I can put anywhere.***
>
> *I am not going to be proud of the five shillings. I am going to write them down in a column of their own with the hour against them and the names of the two men in it, because in about four days one of those two facts is going to be the story and the other one is going to be gone, and she told me that on a slip and she is right and it is the sixth time in a month she has been right about me and not one of the six has been about a bell.*
>
> *And there is one thing I have not put in this entry and I am going to leave it out of it, and I am writing here that I know I am leaving it out, because a page that admits it is leaving it out is not the same as a page that has it in it, and I have one of each in this book now and I have told nobody about either.*
>
> *So: the frame is in its cradle and the bolts are made fast at every place it has been set down, and it has been set down twice today, on a slip and on a plank, and the cradle rides on the barrow's bed and the second clause is the reason I cannot pay for anything, and it is going to hold tomorrow. Nothing is rung. No clause has been broken. **I have used the fifth clause twice today, and both times as a fact about myself, and it has told me the truth about my standing on a slip and it has cost me a place, and it is the only piece of paper I own that has never once tried to be a weapon.***
>
> *I have nine days. It is a town of about two thousand with a clerk's hall in it and about nine hundred things in it that are not the clerk's hall, and the clerk's hall is the one building I cannot get into, and I have known that since a room over a haberdasher's last night and I have believed the man about it since about the second hour this afternoon.*

---

The boat came down into the reach on the second day in the middle of the light and went at the speed of a thing that is not in a hurry, and Miremarket came up out of the flat on the left bank about four hours before dark, and it was a town on a river with a stone landing and a crane and about two hundred feet of quay and a square behind it with a cross on it and a market that was not a three-day market and was not up, and there were about nine roofs above the roofline that were not houses, and one of them had a chimney on it that was not a chimney.

They came alongside at about the ninth hour of the evening, and the frame was bolted on the boards and stayed bolted, and the crate came off and stood on the quay, and the barrow came off the next, with the cradle on its bed and the four bolts in it, and nothing was rung, and the man on the post said his piece of advice and it was four words long and it was *mind the second step*, and Tomas Vale wrote it down because he had begun to write down what men said to him, and he could not have said when that started, and it had not started on the slip.

The town was called Miremarket and it had about two thousand people in it and a clerk's hall in it, and neither of those facts had cost him anything, and the barrow had to go a mile and a bit, and he had a shilling and sixpence left in his pockets and a woman on a quay who was not going to tell him not to.
