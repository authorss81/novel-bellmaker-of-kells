# Chapter 0073 — A Name At The End Of A Lane

She could not ask, and the reason was not the leash and not the fourth of last month and not anything anybody on any road has ever said to her about either of them, and she had known that for about nine days and had not said it out loud because there was nobody to say it to.

The reason was a shape.

**A name on a roll is not a place.** She had said it four times since the boat and once to a river and she had never once had anybody answer it, because it is not an answer, it is a rule about a kind of object. A roll is a list of places that people are not. A page is not a page. And the one thing a courier may do that a clerk may not do is go to a door and say her own name out loud and let the door decide, and that is the whole of the profession, and it is eleven years old, and it does not work on a page.

She wanted a page. She was standing in a lane in a town of two thousand people at the fourth hour of the afternoon of the fifty-ninth, four days from a boy of eight, and everything she had brought to this lane was a method for standing at doors.

“Under the gable with the lean-to,” she said, to herself, and then, out loud, to nobody: “**That is the shape of it and it is going to take me to a room and not to a page and I have got no way of arguing with a room.**”

---

The house at the end of the lane was a gable with a lean-to and a low wall out front that had been higher once, and there were five people in it and one of them could not write.

She knocked at about the fourth hour and a woman came to the door with her sleeves up and did not ask who was there, which is the first thing that has ever happened to her at a door in eleven years and which she decided afterwards had been the answer.

“I am Tamsin Rook. I am a courier and I am not from this county and I am not from the district. **I am going to ask you three questions and they are the same three I have asked at ten doors in four counties and I am not going to change a word of them, and I am going to tell you both of those facts before I ask the first one, and you may shut the door on all three and I will not come back.**”

“You are going to stand there and say that.”

“I am going to stand there and say it. And I am going to say one more thing, which is that if you answer me at all, the answer goes on a thing I write on with my own hand and the hour against it, and anybody may read that thing, and I am telling you that **before** you answer and not afterwards, because that is the only part of this that has a price on it.”

“Go on, then,” said the woman. “I have got a pot on.”

**First.** *Has any person ever put their name to a paper for this house — a Crown form, or a return, or a certificate — without anybody asking them to?*

“Yes,” said Bess Marrow. “Eleven times, and I will tell you the whole of it in about a minute and then you are going to be wrong about it, and I have never had anybody stand in my lane and be wrong about it at me before and I am not going to pretend I am enjoying it.”

“All right.”

“There are five of us in this house and my mother cannot write and I do all of it. I write the answers. I have written one every year for about nine years. **And the hand on the paper in the hall is mine about nine times in ten, because I am the one who goes to the door and I am the one who hands it in, and what is on the paper is not what I wrote.**”

“What is on it.”

“**A mark.** In a column, against the line. That is all. A mark, and a day, and a stamp on it somewhere at the foot.” She put two fingers on the door frame and kept them there, and not on him. “I did not write the mark. I have never written the mark. I have never in my life seen that mark, and I could not tell you now what it is, and I have gone to the office about the day and not about the mark because I want the day and the mark is not mine and I am not going to have it in my hand for a week.”

---

**Second.** *Is the person who put their name there living in this house now?*

“That is the question nobody has ever asked me and it is the one I could answer,” said Bess Marrow, “and I am not going to answer it, and I want that written down with the hour on it as a no and not as nothing, and I will come to it if you ask me the third one first.”

“I have not asked you the third one.”

“**Then ask me the third one.**”

He was not there. He was in a yard four streets off with a frame bolted in a corner of it and he had told her at about the seventh hour that he was not coming and had not asked her to, and she had walked the lane alone and would have walked it alone if he had offered.

**Third.** *Do you want that written down, and may I write your no as a no and not as nothing?*

She got a slate off a woman who let a woman out of a draper's shop on about the sixth hour and paid a penny and a half for it, and she paid it herself out of a purse that had been flat for eleven years and was not flat now, and she wrote the hour at the top of it, and then she wrote in three columns and ruled them apart, and she did not merge them, and she did not put a heading over any of them.

“**Yes,” said Bess Marrow. “And yes to both halves, and my name on it, and here it is, it is Bess Marrow, of the gable with the lean-to, and you may write *no* and not *nothing*, and I will tell you the whole of why, and it is not the reason you are expecting and I have had nine years to get it right.**

“I am not giving you the book. Not today, not in a week, not for a district, and not if a man from a hall stands in my lane. **I have never taken it out of this house and I am not going to, and here is the reason and you may write it down and you may not improve it.**

“**The day this book leaves this house it stops being the book of my house and it becomes evidence about my house.** And it is the only thing in this kingdom that says what the five of us actually said. Not what we sent, not what was written on it at a door with a man standing in the lane — what we said, in the room, at the table, and the day. And the district is right that a return is a good record of what a household sent. **The district has never in nine years had a record of what my house said, and this is it, and it is in a kitchen drawer, and if it goes into a hall then a thing that is about us is a thing anybody can use, and I have got a mother in this house who cannot write and a brother on the cart who cannot read and a house that is paid for to the end of the year.**”

“That is the best reason anybody has given me for anything in a month,” said Tamsin Rook, “and I want to say something about it and I am not going to, and the reason I am not going to is that it is the reason I have not gone into nine hundred houses in eleven years, and I would rather not hear it in my own voice.”

---

And then the woman let her read it.

That was the part Tamsin Rook had not planned and has never been able to account for. She asked for nothing. She asked the three questions and she wrote the no down and then she said, *may I read it, here, with you looking at it, and I will not take it out of your house and I will not write a copy of it and I will not have a page of it,* and Bess Marrow said, “Then read it, and I shall be sat here while you do, because if anybody ever reads that book in this house it is going to be with me in the room and not on their own in a lane,” and she sat down at the table and put the kettle on the end of the stove and did not leave the room, and that is the whole of the permission and it was granted in one sentence.

It is a book with about nine columns ruled in it in one hand, and the hand is Bess Marrow's and it is a careful hand, and there are nine years of it in about four inches of spine.

**The first column is the round.** The day the cart came, every year, written down. Every year, without a gap.

**The second, third, fourth and fifth columns are her mother, her brother Rafe, her sister Nan, and the fifth person, who was away at the works that year and whose column is empty and is not the same empty every year.**

**The sixth column is what was said**, and it is in her mother's language and not in a district's language, and it says things like *nothing to say* and *we were not asked* and, in one year, in the fourth column, *my mother says no twice and both times means it*, which is the only year in nine in which anything was written in the hand column and it is a hand and not a name.

**The seventh column is a name against a day**, and it is the column that is the book, because the day is the day *that person* said it and not the day the cart came and not the day the paper was signed.

She read the seventh column four times and did not copy it, and she was not permitted to and did not, and Bess Marrow watched the whole of it from the table with the kettle beginning to sing, and nobody said anything for about four minutes.

Then she stopped at the eleventh place.

“Bess. **I have found a thing and I am going to say it and you are going to say no to it, and I am going to say it in a row of the column I am allowed to have, which is the one with a thing nobody could answer in it.**”

“Say it.”

“Your book has the round’s day in the first column, for nine years, with no gap. And the seventh column has the day a person said a thing. And in eleven places those two columns do not agree, and the roll in the hall has a day in it too, and I have not seen the roll and I am not going to ask to.” She put her finger on the first column and then on the seventh, in the room, and did not touch the page for long. “**In eleven places the day in your book and the day in the hall are different, and in all eleven it is the hall that is earlier, and in nine of the eleven the day the hall has got is a day the round was not in this street.**”

Bess Marrow stood up and came and looked at it, and she did not touch it either, and she was not a quick reader and she was not pretending.

“Say that again about the round.”

“In nine of the eleven, the hall’s day is a day the cart was not here. Not late. Not before. **Not here.**”

“So they have written down a day.”

“They have written down a day on which nothing was asked, because **somebody is closing a line and the day you close it is the day you close it, and it is not the day you asked and it is not the day anybody spoke.** I have not seen the roll and I cannot tell you that is what they are doing. I can tell you that it is what it looks like from a kitchen table in a town of two thousand people, and I can tell you that you have kept the round’s day in that book for nine years for a reason, and the reason is that a household is asked on a day and not on any other day, and there is only one day in a year your house is asked on, and you wrote it down because you are the one who has to be ready for it.”

Nobody said anything for a while and the kettle went on past the singing.

“**Then a man at that hall can close my line on a day when nobody in this house has been asked anything,**” said Bess Marrow.

“That is what it looks like,” said Tamsin Rook. “And I am not going to improve it into something I know, because I have been wrong at improving things in this book eleven times and it has been wrong every time, and I would rather be a woman with a thing that looks like something than a woman with a thing that is.”

---

She stayed until it went dark because Bess Marrow was in until dark and there was nothing else in the lane to do, and the lamp came on, and at about the ninth hour she read the three columns out loud to the woman, one at a time, and the woman heard her own words and nobody else's, and changed one word.

“*May I write your no as a no and not as nothing.* **Change that last bit.** I have been not-nothing for nine years and I know exactly what it is and it is not a no, it is a hole with my house in the bottom of it, and I am not having *not nothing* on a slate in a lane. **Write: and may I write your no as a no.** Nothing else. I do not need you to promise me the second thing. I am asking you to put the first one down and then stop talking.”

So it was written: *and may I write your no as a no.* And Tamsin Rook read it back and it was right, and she put the hour against it, and it is the first of eleven refusals of hers that has become an answer, and she has thought about the difference about nine times since.

And then she did the thing she has done at ten doors in four counties and which has never once done anything, which is that she looked for the boy.

Not with the question. There is no version of the question that finds a boy of eight taken six days before the day any of this started, and she is not going to insult a woman's kitchen by trying to build one, and she has not asked, and she is not going to, and she is going to be honest with a reader about that in a book in her own hand on purpose.

**She does not know whether Jory Fenn is on a roll in this town. She has been four days in this town and she has not asked one person, and she is not going to, and the reason is not courage and it is not the leash and it is not that she is frightened of the answer.**

It is that there are about eleven thousand lines a year in this district and a clerk in a street told a man yesterday that a roll is a shelf with a number on it, and a page is a page, and a name on a page is a mark in a column, and **she would be doing to about eleven thousand households in this county what she has spent eleven years refusing to do to one, and she is not going to do it in a lane with a slate and a lamp and a woman who has a pot on, and there is a name and there is a woman in front of her, and a woman in front of her is a person and a page is a page, and she has been on the road for a month because the road is the only thing that has ever gone to a place to find out whether a person is in it.**

And she had come here for a page.

**The road had not found anything. It had made her findable.** That was the finding, and she wrote it at about the ninth hour of the evening on the back of a slate she had paid a penny and a half for, and it is in her book in her own hand, and it is the first thing she has written in a month that she has not improved afterwards.

> *The fifty-ninth. I went to the end of a lane for a page and I got a room, and the room is the thing I have been walking towards for a month, and I want it recorded that I have been wrong about the shape of it for the entire month and that the wrongness was not stupid and did not feel like being stupid while I was doing it.*
>
> *Eleven years I have said that a name on a roll is not a place. I said it four times on the water and once to a river. It is true and it is also not the question, and I have been asking a road to answer a question about a page for a month and the road has been the wrong instrument the whole time and it is the only instrument I have, and I have not said that out loud to anybody including him and I am saying it here for the first time and it is the first thing in this book I have not written for a reader of it.*
>
> ***A roll is a list of places that people are not. The road is the only thing that has ever gone to a place to find out whether a person is in it. The road has not found one. The road has made me findable, and being findable has been the cost, and the cost has been paid once and it was on the fifty-fifth of this month in a room over a haberdasher's shop, and I priced it, and I am not going to price it again.***
>
> *And I have not asked whether he is on a roll in this town. I have not asked one person, and I am not going to, and it is not because I am frightened of the answer and it is not because there are eleven thousand of them. It is that there is a woman at a table in front of me with a pot on who has given me her name and her no, and she is a person, and what I wanted in this lane was a page, and **a page is a thing I would have to take off a woman who has kept one thing in her kitchen drawer for nine years and who told me the reason, and the reason was right, and I have been carrying the same reason for eleven years and I would not have recognised it in a book.***
>
> *What I have got, and it is more than I came for and less than I need, and both of those are true at once:*
>
> ***A name in a household’s own book. Eleven places where that book and a roll in a hall do not agree. And a fact about a day: on nine of the eleven, the day the hall has got is a day the round was not in that street. That is a fact about a day and not about a name and not about a man, and I have not seen the roll and I am not going to ask, and I have not improved it into anything, and it is in a column of its own on a slate that cost a penny and a half and the hour is against it.***
>
> *And a woman who will answer me again, which is eleven years and about nine hundred doors and the first one that has ever said so.*
