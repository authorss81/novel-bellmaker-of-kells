# Chapter 0093 — Fifty-One Pounds

The yard on the water at the west end of this town is open at the first hour and does not stop, and at about the fourth hour on the seventy-ninth day of the month Tomas Vale stood at the gate of it with a barrow he could not park anywhere and asked one question, and the question was the only one he had, and it took him about nine seconds to say and about four minutes to stop wanting to say a second one.

He had it on a slate and he did not use the slate, which is the first time in a month.

“**Is the chore yours.**”

Marden Vey put down a coil of rope he had been coiling and did not pick it up again.

“No,” he said. “**Say the rest of it.**”

“Four days ago a man in this yard told me that the whole of a firm's ruin is that four men have never once told each other that a firm is them, and that a chore is a thing a man does for himself in his own yard on his own day, and that it is about eighteen words long. I wrote it down in a book and I have not improved it. And I have come four streets and a market to ask you one question and the question is whether that thing is yours.”

“**It is a chore and it is mine and it is not the firm's, and it has been mine since about the spring, and I have not done it, and the reason I have not done it is a man with a barrow.**”

“I am not going to persuade you of anything.”

“**Then you are going to stand at that gate for about a minute and a half and I am going to tell you why a man with a barrow is the last reason in the world, and then you are going to be told what happened at about the fifth hour this morning, and I would like it noticed that I am not going to pretend it happened because of you.**”

---

That is the whole of the decision and it took about a minute and a half and it was made in a yard by a man with nine men on his books, and the man with the barrow was at the gate and was not in it.

Nobody in that yard was persuaded by anybody from outside it. Tomas Vale was at a gate with a slate he had not used and he watched the whole of it and he could not have stopped any part of it and he did not try.

**A chore is a thing you do for yourself, in your own yard, with your own hands, on your own day.** And the reason four men could not do it for four years is not that it is difficult and not that the practice is obscure and not that there is no column for it, and it is not that nobody told them, because a merchant in this town said it out loud in a counting house on the seventy-fifth, in his own name, and asked for it to be written down that he refused six households with it, and it is in a book in a coat four streets from this yard.

**It is that a firm is not us and never has been, and a thing done for a firm is not a chore, it is a favour, and nobody in a firm owes favours to itself on any day of the week.**

And the hand went down.

**Will Preedy** had been in that yard since he was a boy and had not said one word from the moment he came in, and at some point in the minute and a half he went through the counting-house door and got the firm's book off the shelf, and the book is the firm's and the line in it is in the name of the trade, and he did not write on that line.

He wrote a new line, under it, in his own name, with the day against it, and then he put a hand against it, and then he wrote his own name at the foot of the hand the way a hand is written, and he did it standing up, and it took him less time than the talking had, and he did not explain it and nobody asked him to.

Marden Vey watched the whole of it and did not say anything, and afterwards he said three things, and they are the whole of what that part of the yard said that day.

“**One: that is a hand of a person of this firm, in that person's own name, against that person's own line, and it is not the firm's line being answered, it is his, and the practice in that hall says that where anybody cannot make up its mind one hand is put against the line and the line is treated as answered, and it does not say whose hand, and that is a good rule and I have said so and I am not going to unsay it.**

“**Two: nobody persuaded him. I want that in whatever you are keeping. I have been in this yard thirty-one years and I have watched about nine things done in it that were not mine and he did not ask and nobody spoke to him and he was not even in the room for the first half of it.**

“**Three: and this is the one I have thought about for most of the night, and you may write it down, and I am telling you that it does not save us.**”

---

“It does not save us.”

“**It pays six households and it is the first money that has moved in this room in four months, and it is the whole of what a hand on a line is for, and it is going to cost me my yard and I have known that since about the fourth hour this morning.**

“**A man with money in a town of two thousand refused me four times in four months and he was right four times. He refused a house that could not show its own answering. And we can show it now, from about the fifth hour this morning, and the four of them are not going to hear about it, because a man who says no to you once is not going to say yes to you in the spring on the strength of a mark in a column, and he would be a fool to, and I would not lend to me either.**”

“So the line is answered and the firm is finished.”

“**The line is answered and the six get paid today, this morning, out of a bag that has been standing on that table since the winter, and in the spring there will be a fifth man with money in this town and I will go to him and he will say no, and he will be right, and that is the whole of what I have learned in four years and I have not got anybody to be angry at.**”

---

The bag went into a room upstairs at about the seventh hour and came down again, and the total of the six claims was about fifty-one pounds, and the six claims were in a yard-book kept for thirty-one years by a dead man against a day, and a hand against a line is the only record in this kingdom that a person stood in front of somebody and said something.

**They were paid in that room, one at a time, by Marden Vey, out of a leather bag, and he read each claim out before he paid it, and the reading-out is a thing he invented for the morning and told them so.**

The first was a woman called **Ruth Dall**, who had stood at that gate in the winter with a man who had died before the snow was off and had asked her to put it in, and she had not wanted to and had asked him twice what it was for, and he had told her both times, and she had a hand in the book and had wanted it entered and had been entered.

She was paid about nine pounds and she counted it at the table and put it inside her coat and said:

“**Two years.**”

“Then that is the whole of it.”

“**I am not saying that to you. I am saying it in this room because there is nowhere else to say it, and I have said it to my own sister twice and she has told me to be quiet, and I am not going to be quiet, and it is two years and it is about nine pounds and I am old enough to have counted every week of it, and there is no more of it and there was never going to be any more of it, and you can write that down and you can write the two years against it and I do not want it read out.**”

He wrote it down and did not read it out.

---

**And about nine hundred people in this town cannot be paid and are not owed anything and are not going to be owed anything, and Marden Vey said that out loud in the room after Ruth Dall had gone and said it himself and did not have it said for him.**

“**There are about nine hundred people in this town who could each remember a thing Ivor Salter said to them at a gate. He was dead a good many years before he died. A gate is where people say the true things and I have said some.**”

“That is not nothing.”

“**It is nothing, and it is not nothing, and both of those are the same and they are the whole of what a rule is. **Nobody has been robbed.** That is not a comfort I have invented for a bag of money in a room. That is four hundred years of a rule doing the only thing it is for, and if I broke it this morning for nine hundred people who remember a gate, then in about four years this county would be full of men who say a thing at a gate and come back with it, and every one of them would be owed it, and there would be no end to it and no way to say no to any of them.**”

“So the six are paid and the rest are not.”

“**The six have shown a hand and the rest have shown a memory, and a hand is a record and a memory is a story, and I am not going to be a man who pays stories, and I am not going to be a man who says the rest of them are owed it either, because they are not, and because that is the part that would rob him.**”

---

**Ruth Dall was standing at the gate when Tomas Vale came out of the yard, and she had not asked him for anything, and he had not asked her for anything, and he had a barrow he could not park anywhere and a slate he had not used, and he had watched nine seconds of a man do a chore for himself and could not have stopped any part of it.**

He wrote it at about the ninth hour of the evening, on the step of the draper's, in the flat hand, and it took him three attempts.

> *The seventy-ninth. A chore is a thing a man does for himself, in his own yard, with his own hands, on his own day. That is the whole of it and it is not a mystery and it is not a column and no clerk can enter it and it took four years and a dead man's yard-book and about eighteen words spoken in a counting house by the man it belongs to.*
>
> ***And a firm cannot do a chore and a household can, and that is the whole of the difference, and here it is in the plainest words I have: a household is a person and a firm is not, and the only thing in this kingdom that can be asked a question and refuse is a person, and so the only thing that can do a chore is a person, and a firm is four people who have not told each other that they are each other, and about four hundred years of a rule about dead men's money turns entirely on that and on nothing else.***
>
> *About fifty-one pounds went into six hands in a room upstairs this morning, one at a time, and the man who paid it read every claim out loud first and invented the reading for the morning and said so, and one of the six was a woman who had waited two years for about nine pounds and said the two years out loud in a room and then asked for it not to be read out, and I wrote it down and did not read it out.*
>
> *And about nine hundred people in this town remember a thing a dead man said to them at a gate and not one of them can be paid, and that is correct, and it is the reason he has not been robbed, and the merchant who said it is right and is not a villain and is going to lose a yard in the spring.*
>
> *And the thing I did. **I asked one question at a gate and it was a good question and I have written it down and I want it noticed that I did not persuade one person of anything.** A man who has been in a yard since he was a boy went through a door and put a hand on a line in his own name and did not say one word while he did it, and nobody spoke to him, and he was not persuaded by me and he was not persuaded by his partner and he was not persuaded by anybody from outside that gate, and I have been in this town twenty-two days and I have watched about nine things happen in it and I was the instrument of one of them and I am not going to pretend to be anything else.*
>
> *And the question was a chore. That is what I have learned and it took me twenty-two days and a gate. **A man with a barrow cannot do a chore for anybody and cannot persuade anybody of anything; the only question a man with a barrow can put to a yard is a question whose answer the man was going to arrive at on his own day anyway, and if it is not that then it is an argument and I have had six weeks of arguments and I am not going to have a seventh.***
>
> *And the leash. I had the slate and the chalk and the four columns and I did not put a question on it. That is a non-use and it is the second one in a month and I am writing it down because the first one was on the sixty-fourth and I did not want to be a man who uses a thing to get a person into a room, and the reason this time is that she has told me six times since the thirty-fourth what the asking costs the person who says no and every one of the six cost me and not one of them cost her, and I am not going to put an eighth thing in front of her and call it work.*
>
> *The firm is answered and the firm is finished. **All five clauses stand. The frame is four streets off with four bolts in it and no clapper. The bell was rung once in a market in this town on the seventy-seventh and I have not touched it since that morning and I do not know where it went. Nothing else was rung.***
>
> *And a clerk works in a building in this town whose name about nine hundred people in this town heard two days ago in a market square, and a page of his own hand went into a room over a draper's shop last night and it is in a coat on a table and it is a record about a man who has a wife and a boy, and the two things are about a hundred yards apart and they do not know about each other, and I am the only person in this county who can see both and I have no standing to say so to anybody, and that is the position on the seventy-ninth and I am not going to improve it.*
