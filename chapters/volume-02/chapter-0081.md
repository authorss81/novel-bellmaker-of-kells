# Chapter 0081 — A Name Read Before It Is Needed

He worked out what he wanted before he was out of the room, and then he stood in the yard behind the draper's shop and worked out that he could not have it, and both of those took about four minutes, and the second one is the whole of the sixty-seventh.

What he wanted was a sentence from somebody that said what a name on a roll is *for*.

He had four people who could have said it. Ivo Trant had told him that a clerk's hall is the one building in a town of two thousand a man may not walk into, and had been right. Bryn Pethick had said a person is the only thing in this kingdom that can be asked a question and can refuse to answer it, and had been right, in about nine seconds, standing up. Elias Nabb had put a sentence on a sheet nobody asked him for and had been right, and had said he would not be asked again this week, and the week was four days gone and there was nothing in the asking that had changed. And a woman at the end of a lane had given him nine columns in a kitchen drawer and a reason for never taking them out of the house, and the reason was correct.

**Not one of the four could be asked, and the reason was not standing and was not the fifth clause of a receipt and was not a clerk's door. The reason was that he had asked all of them, in four different buildings, in five weeks, and every one of them had answered, and every one of the answers was correct, and not one of the answers was a sentence about what a name is for.**

He had run out of buildings before he ran out of questions. That was new.

---

The difficulty, when he got to it, was not that the list was hidden.

It was that it was not hidden, and he had spent nine days standing in front of a building.

**There is no door to the list.** There is a shop and a street and a market and a woman at a stall and a queue of about six people at a chandler's counter on a market street, and no person in the queue has ever been stopped and questioned about anything, and nothing about the queue is hidden from anybody including Tomas Vale, and he had been in this town nine days and had not looked at one.

So on the morning of the sixty-seventh he did not ask anybody anything at all, and he stood in a street, and he let it happen to him.

---

He was at the fourth hour at a canvas and rope stall at the top of the market street with about six people in a line at it, buying twine for about four different jobs, and he stood eleven feet off with a barrow behind him that he could not park anywhere else.

The woman in front of the counter said, to the woman behind her, in the ordinary voice of a person keeping a place in a line:

“**You have not sent for it.**”

“I have not.”

“No, I know you have not, I am not being unkind, that is not what I am saying. **It is on the line and you have not sent for it.** It has been on the line four years and nobody in that house has ever sent for it and I am telling you now because I am not going to be the one standing in your lane saying it.”

“That is the whole of what you have come to say to me at a stall.”

“It is, and I have said it, and you may go and be telling them and I shall not come and say it again. **Ada, I am not saying it for me. I am saying it because I have a house of my own and I have sent for mine, and I have sent for mine four years and I shall go on sending for it, and you are my sister and I would rather say it here than in your kitchen with your mother in it.**”

“You needn’t.”

“I know I needn’t. **Verrow. That is the name and it is on the line and it is yours and it is your mother's before it is yours and it has been four years.**”

And the woman in front said, “Well, I cannot make it up on my own, that is the whole of what I have ever said about it,” and the woman behind said, “Then that is what you will say when they come,” and the line moved along, and nobody looked at the man eleven feet off with the barrow, and nobody said anything else, and that was the whole of it.

Tomas Vale stood eleven feet off with a barrow he could not park anywhere else and did not move for about a minute and a half.

Then he walked about thirty yards and stood against a wall and did not write anything down.

---

**The claim a household makes on its own name is that the household is the one being asked.**

That is the whole of the professional half and it came to him in a street and not in a room, and it took him the better part of an hour to get it right and he got it wrong twice on the way, and the way he got it wrong is in the book.

A name on a roll is a duty. He had that on the sixty-third, out of a man who was glad to be asked and had had four years to get it in the right order, and he had carried it eleven yards and not once thought about what it was a duty *to*.

A duty is a chore. A chore is a thing done because the line has to be answered and the line comes round. And a chore, said a woman in a queue about her own sister, **is a thing you do for yourself, in your own house, with your own hands, on your own day.** You are not being asked anything. Nothing is asked. The only question in the whole business is which of you it falls to.

And *that* is the claim. Not against the office, which has never once claimed a household for itself and does not want one. **Against the other four.** A name on a line stops being a mark in a column in about four years and becomes the thing the house is owed by, and the house stops asking itself who is the one being asked and starts asking itself who is going to answer, and the person who says the name in the queue is not informing her. **The person who says the name in the queue is telling her which of the two of them it is going to be, and she is thanking her, and she is right to.**

He wrote a bit of it in his head and not in the book, and then he stopped, because a man who writes a sentence down on a step has already decided the sentence is going to be needed, and the sentence is a document, and he had learned nine days ago what a document does.

---

The second one came at about the sixth hour and it was not a queue.

He was at the cross, where there is a well and a trough and about nine things going on at once on a market day, and two women at a pitch of nothing in particular were talking across a gap of about four feet, and neither of them had anything to do with him, and neither of them lowered her voice, and people have not lowered their voices in a market since before anybody in this kingdom began writing things down.

“— and that is the one with the barrow,” said the first. “And the frame in it. And the frame is in the yard behind the draper's and the draper is furious about it in a quiet way, and the bolts, and the bolts he is in the yard counting every day like a man counting sheep.”

“Is he in the district?”

“He is not in the district. **His name is Vale.** And he came down the lane at the end asking after the Verrows like anybody, and I told him far end and I told him it was not a secret, and he thanked me, which nobody does.”

“I have seen the frame. I have been in that yard for the canvas.”

“That is the same man. And I will tell you the other thing, because it is funny and because it is not. **He asked Wick what the roll in the hall is. Out in the street. Nine words.** And Wick told him, and told him the word for it and the number of them, and Wick is not a fool, and Wick is still telling it.”

“Was it the tax men?”

“**It was not the tax men.** He wanted to know what the roll in the hall was and I have been in this town eleven years and the last man who wanted to know what the roll in the hall was was a tax man and he wanted the other one.”

Tomas Vale stood at a trough in a market town of about two thousand people and heard a woman at a pitch of nothing in particular give his name to another woman in the ordinary way, in a market, in the middle of a morning, and neither of them was doing anything, and neither of them was talking about him.

**Nobody had ever been stopped. Nobody had ever been forbidden. Nobody had been punished for saying it and nobody ever will be.** She had said his name because a man with a barrow and a frame in a yard is a thing that happened in this town this month, and a thing that happened is a thing a town says out loud, and she had said it in a queue to a woman she had known a long time, which is what a town does.

He is not, as far as he can find out, frightened of anything. He has been in this town nine days. **He is known.**

---

He went up the lane and past two rooms over a dyer's on the north side and there was a light in the window of the upper one at about the sixth hour, and he did not go up, and he wrote one line about it and the line was *a light in the upper room and I have not been up and I am not going to be this week.*

Then he went to the smith's yard in that street where a crate is standing with the sacking off, and looked at a bell that has no claim in it, and did not ring it, and did not want to, and had written in his own hand three days ago that he is not going to ring it in this county at all.

Then he went back to the yard behind the draper's shop and the frame was in the corner where it had stood since about the sixth hour of the morning of the fifty-eighth, in its cradle, with four bolts made fast, and he counted them because counting them is what he does.

And then he sat down on the step of the draper's with the day-book on his knee, and he did not write until about the fourth hour of the evening, and he wrote for about two hours, which is a duration and not an hour, and he filled three columns and did not merge them and he put the hours in the flat hand.

> *The sixty-seventh. I did not ask anybody anything. I stood in a street for most of a day and I let it happen to me and I am writing that down first because it is the first thing I have done in five weeks that was not a method.*
>
> *And here is the finding and it is one line and I have got it exactly and I am not going to improve it.*
>
> ***A name on a roll becomes a duty in about four years, and the household reads it that way first, before any office, and it is read out of a queue, by a neighbour, in the ordinary way, and the neighbour is not an agent of anything and is owed nothing and is usually right about who they are.***
>
> *And the professional half, which is the half I have not got anywhere in six weeks, and which I have been carrying about eleven yards since a man called Skell gave me the first half of it in a front room and I did not know what I had:*
>
> ***A duty is a mouth. A mouth is for a claim. And the claim a household makes on its own name is not a claim against the office. It is the claim that the household is the one being asked.***
>
> *Nobody in that office ever claimed this house. The hall holds a shelf. The whole of what a name on that shelf comes to is that in about four years it stops being a mark and becomes a thing the house is owed by, and the house begins to ask itself which of the five of it is going to answer, and it settles that among itself, in a kitchen, out loud, in the ordinary way, and a woman in a queue says one sentence about it in front of a sister, and the sister goes home and tells her mother, and the mother tells the two others, and in about four years that household has agreed to something that no office asked it and no office will ever know about, and it will honour it, because honouring it is the only thing anybody has taught it to do with a name.*
>
> *And here is the part I have to put in the fourth column and cannot improve:*
>
> ***It cost one sentence. That is the whole price of it. A thing that took four years and about nine hundred lines a year to become a duty was made by a woman at a rope stall, in about twenty-five words, to a woman in a queue, in the ordinary voice, and no document was in it, and no document is in it now, and there was nobody at any door at any hour, and nobody has been asked anything, and the two women will both be dead in about forty years and the sentence will still be in that house.***
>
> *And I have been in this town nine days and I have not been able to think of a single question that would have found that, and I have asked a cart, a man with a net, a draper, a clerk at a counter, a man in a chair, a master in a room, a woman at a water bell, a form-writer at a sluice and a woman in a kitchen. **Every one of them was the right person to ask. That is the finding. There was no wrong person. The question was in a queue and I was not standing in one.**
>
> *And I am not angry and I have been not angry about this since about the second hour of the afternoon and I have been checking, because a man who has lost the last person he was angry at in five weeks has a hole where the anger was and things go into the hole. Nothing has gone into it. I have been walking about this town all day with a thing I cannot do anything about and I have not wanted to hit anything since about the fourth hour and I am recording that as a fact about me and not as a virtue, because I do not think it is one, and I think it is worse, and I am not going to write a paragraph about how noble it is to be calm in a market town.*

He shut the book and did not improve any of it and went to see whether the frame was still bolted, and it was.

**Nothing was rung anywhere in Miremarket on the sixty-seventh, and none of the five clauses was broken, and the fifth one was used once, in his own hand, as a fact about who he was not allowed to be: he has no standing to speak for any household in Kells or in any ward, and that meant this morning that he was not allowed to ask a woman at a rope stall a question about her sister.**
