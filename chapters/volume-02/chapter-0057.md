# Chapter 0057 — Somebody Took the Cradle

The yard had four vehicles in it when they came in at about the eighth hour of the evening of the fortieth day, and the frame went on the barrow against the far wall with the cradle on the boards and the four bolts made fast, and Tamsin Rook counted the bolts twice, and the ostler counted them too, without being asked, because she had said *four* and pointed at them, and he was a man who counts.

The three other vehicles were a wagon without a cover, a miller's cart, and a hired chaise standing in the corner with a horse still in the shafts and a boy asleep on the top of it. There were eleven people in the yard by the time it was dark: the ostler, a carter who was eating off the wagon, a wagoner doing something to a wheel, the chaise's boy, a woman under the wagon's tilt waiting for a coach that was not coming tonight, two drovers, a man with a dog, and four more who came and went and are not in any book.

She asked the ostler where he kept the book.

“Book?”

“The book for the yard. Stabling, carts, and anything a person leaves.”

He brought it out and it is a quarto with a column for the day, a column for the thing, a column for whose, and a column for the hour, and it is kept with a stub of pencil and not a pen, and the last four days of it are in a carter's hand and a carter's hand is not a good hand and Tomas Vale's is a very good one, and it took her the length of the yard to work out that nobody had ever been asked to use this book as a witness to anything.

“You want a line in it,” the ostler said.

“I want a line in it at the second hour in the morning if a man comes asking about a bell,” Tamsin Rook said, “and I want the line to be in his words and not mine, and I want about eleven people to be standing about when it is written. Can you do that.”

“I can leave the book out,” said the ostler, “and I can be here, and I will tell you now that I will not hold a Crown bell for anybody, and I would rather tell you now than have it come up at two in the morning.”

“You are not holding it,” Tamsin Rook said. “It is on a barrow against your wall with four bolts in it and it is going to be there when you open the door.”

Tomas Vale went in at about the ninth hour and was asleep by ten, and he had not slept properly for seven days, and she had watched him not sleep for seven days, and she had not asked him about it once, and she sat out the rest of the evening with her back against the wagon wheel and her bag between her boots and she did not ring anything.

---

He came at about the second hour.

He came on foot and alone and he did not hurry, and Tamsin Rook was sitting where she had been sitting and he knew she was there, and he stopped about ten feet off the barrow and said:

“I am requesting the object in that frame to be produced.”

“All right,” Tamsin Rook said. “I am going to read that back to you before I do anything, and I am going to ask you three things, and if you do not like one of them you may say so and I will enter that you said so.”

“Go on.”

She read the paper back.

It is one side. It is not Crown paper and it is not on Crown paper and it is a good, plain, correct sheet, and it is dated the fortieth day and it is timed — the hour is written in the corner in the same hand — and it says that a request is made, under the third clause of a custody receipt of the thirty-third day signed by a watch captain at Kells, for the fixed stripped survey carrier held by Tomas Vale to be produced at this yard, for the purpose of a person looking at it. It does not ask for it to be moved. It does not ask for it to be opened. It does not ask for anything to be done to it at all.

And it does not have a name on it.

“Thank you,” Tamsin Rook said. “That is a lawful request and I am not going to argue with it and I am not going to refuse it, and I want to say both of those things in front of witnesses because in about a year somebody is going to read this page and want to know which one it was.”

---

“First question,” she said. “What is your name, for the book.”

“I have not got one to give.”

“All right. I am going to ask you the second one and it is the same question with different words, and if the answer to it is the same I am going to write down that the answer to it was the same.”

“Do what you like,” the man said.

“Who is standing behind you?”

“There is nobody behind me.”

“Then I am going to write in this book at the second hour that a request was produced by a person who gave no name and who had no witness at the time of making it, and I am going to write it in that order, and the order is the whole of the fairness in it, and if you would rather I wrote it in another order you may say so now and I will write it in another order and enter that you asked.”

The man did not say anything for long enough that a barrow came through the yard behind him.

“Write that,” he said.

“Second question, and it is the one that matters.” Tamsin Rook stood up. “What are you going to do with it when it is produced.”

“Look at it.”

“That is what is written on your paper. I am asking you the other thing.” She did not raise her voice, because a woman who raises her voice at two in the morning in a yard is a woman who has lost. “Are you going to ask it to do anything.”

The man looked at her for a moment, and then he said, “I am going to ask it to tell me what it is.”

“No,” Tamsin Rook said. “And I am going to say why, out loud, in this yard, with about eleven people standing in it, and I am going to say it by reading a piece of paper, and I am going to read all of it because I have never in my life read a clause out loud and been thanked for it and I am not starting now.”

---

The inn's yard book is written on the knee at two in the morning by a woman holding a pencil in her left hand because her right hand is on the rail.

It is eleven lines and it is the only document in the world that records this, and it reads:

*Fortieth day, second hour of the morning. A request was produced at this yard under the third clause of a custody receipt of the thirty-third day from Kells, signed by a watch captain, the holder being Tomas Vale. The request was read out entire by the holder's companion, the sheet being one side, dated this day, timed the second hour, and asking that the object be produced at this yard for the purpose of looking at it. The requester was asked for his name for this book and gave none. The requester was asked who was standing behind him and said there was nobody behind him. The request was not refused. The object was produced in this yard at the second hour in its mounting frame and its reinforced padded cradle upon the barrow, the retaining bolts made fast, and was not taken out of the cradle and was not rung, and no person in this yard has rung it at any hour.*

*Then the holder's companion read out the fourth and fifth clauses of the receipt entire, and the requester was asked again, in the words already used, whether he intended to ask the object to say what it was, and the answer was that he intended to ask it to tell him what it is, and the fourth clause was then read to him again, which is: that the maker is not the keeper of it, and has no claim in it, and cannot certify it, and that the man who holds it is the man who was asked to hold it and is not the man who made it and is not a man who may say what it means. And the fifth clause, which is: that this receipt does not make the holder a keeper of any route, and that no answer given by any person to any signal carried on or by this object may be entered by anybody in any book as the answer of a household of this ward, and that the holder has no standing to speak for any household in Kells or in any ward.*

*Then the requester looked at the object in its cradle for about a quarter of an hour and did not touch it and did not go round the other side of it, and then went out of the yard and did not say anything on the way out.*

*The eleven people standing in this yard at the second hour are the ostler of this house, a carter out of the wagon against the north wall, a wagoner, a boy on a hired chaise, a woman under a tilt, two drovers, a man with a dog, and four persons who came and went and are not named here because they would not give their names, which was their right.*

*Entered by the holder's companion. Not signed. She will sign it if anybody asks her to and she is not asking.*

---

“You could have refused that,” the man said, from about ten feet off the barrow, at about the fourth hour.

“I could not,” Tamsin Rook said. “It is a lawful request and I was made the request by about a hundred and forty people over a month in a town four miles from here, and every one of them wrote down the same sentence, and the sentence was that a person who has a right to ask must be answered and the answer must be written down, or the right is a thing that exists in somebody's opinion. I have spent a month watching a girl of sixteen refuse to merge two answers because of exactly that, and I am not going to be the woman who finds a loophole in it at two in the morning because a man I cannot place has a sheet of paper.”

“So you gave him the bell.”

“I gave him the frame in a yard in daylight in front of eleven people and a page in a book,” Tamsin Rook said. “There is a difference and you will find it in about a year and I would rather you found it on a page than in a story.”

He looked at the cradle for a while.

“The second clause is the good one,” he said. “The one where the holder may not speak for a household. That is the one that stops me. I have been at this since the third hour and I have been trying to get at the fourth clause — the maker may not say what it means — because that is the one I could have used, and I have got it, and it has not done me a single thing, because a clause that says the man holding it may not say what it means is worth nothing at two in the morning unless somebody has got to the point of asking him to say it, and you got there first, and you did it by reading out a clause about a household instead of a clause about a bell.”

“Then it is well made,” Tamsin Rook said, and hated herself about a third of the way through the sentence.

“It is extremely well made. It is the best piece of paper I have read in four years and it was made by a man who has no authority to make anything and who wrote a fifth clause nobody asked him for at five in the morning in a watch house in a town where he had been arrested two days before.” He was still looking at the cradle. “You understand what you have done, do you.”

“No. Say it.”

“You have established, in a yard with eleven people in it, that this receipt works. That it can be produced at any hour, at any yard, on a sheet with no Crown seal on it, by anybody with a pen. You did not refuse him, and you entered his refusal of his own name in a book, and you produced the object in about nine minutes. **You have made it cheap.** You have made it a thing that costs a man one sheet of paper and a walk. And in about a year there will be a man somewhere in this county who does not want the object, who wants a *third* of this receipt, and by then this will be a thing that happens on an ordinary day and nobody will stand in a yard about it.”

Tamsin Rook sat down on the wagon wheel.

“Yes,” she said.

“I am not going to say that you were wrong. I want that entered somewhere if there is anybody to enter it with. I think you were right and I think it has cost you a night's sleep and it will cost more than that, and I would like somebody to write down both halves in the same hand, because the two halves are the only thing I have ever seen work in a book.”

He went out of the yard and up the lane and did not say anything on the way out, and Tomas Vale slept until about the fifth hour.

---

She did not wake him at the second hour and she has never given a reason for that, and nobody has ever asked her for one, and there is a woman at a water bell four miles below here who asked for a week before she did anything with what she had been told, and the two of those decisions are in the same book and neither of them is a decision anybody made a fuss about.

He came out at about the fifth hour with his coat on and she was sitting where she had been sitting, and he read the page in the yard book standing up, and it took him about nine minutes, and he read it twice, which nobody asked him to do, and the second time he put his finger on the line about the fourth and fifth clauses.

“You read it out to him,” he said.

“All five clauses. The fourth and the fifth twice.”

“The fifth is the one that stopped it.”

“The fifth is the one that told him what he could not do with it, and it took eleven minutes and it is the only reason that yard is not a robbery,” Tamsin Rook said. “The fourth is the one he came for and it is a good clause and it did nothing for him. **Your fifth clause is what makes a request checkable**, and I have thought about that at four in this morning with my back against a wheel, and here is the whole of what I have got: a request is only checkable if you can tell in advance what the person is going to do with the thing, and you cannot tell in advance from a sheet of paper, and the only way anybody has ever found to check a request is to make the requester say out loud what he wants to do, in a room, with other people in it, and then write that down while he is saying it. That is all a fifth clause is. It is a reason to make a man say a sentence out loud instead of writing it down on a sheet in a room by himself.”

Tomas Vale read the line again.

“I wrote that clause in a watch house at about the fifth hour of the morning of the thirty-third,” he said, “and I have been carrying it for seven days as the reason nobody can take this out of my hands, and I want to say the other half of that, and I have thought about how to say it and there is only one way.”

“Which is.”

“It is the reason nobody can take it out of my hands **and the reason anybody can ask for it at two in the morning.** Both. In the same clause. I have been carrying half of a thing for seven days and calling it the whole of it.”

He shut the yard book and put the pencil on top of it and squared it.

“You did not wake me,” he said.

“No.”

“All right,” Tomas Vale said. “You did not wake me and I was not here and you read four clauses of my receipt out loud in a yard in the dark to a man who had come to take a bell, and you produced it in nine minutes, and you entered a refusal of a name in a book that a coach inn keeps for stabling. I am not going to say that I told you this would happen. I want it entered that I did not say it and that I am not going to, and that if I had said it on the thirty-third night it would have been a man telling a woman she was slow, and you were not slow, you were asleep because you had not slept in seven days, and that is a thing I did to you and not a thing you did to yourself.”

“You did not tell me,” Tamsin Rook said. “That is correct and I want it in the book in those words. **You did not tell me and I did not ask.** If you had told me on the thirty-third I would have thought about it for seven days, and I would have written something down about it, and there would be a page in your book and not a page in an inn's, and then this would be your instrument and not mine, and a man with a pen would still have come at the second hour and I would have been a woman who had been warned.”

“You are good at this.”

“I am very good at this,” Tamsin Rook said. “That is the part I have been sitting here for four hours with. I have been good at this for eleven years and I have never once enjoyed it and I have never told anybody that, and it is not a complaint. It is a thing about eleven years. **Being good at it is not the same as liking it, and a person who cannot tell the difference will do it at two in the morning for four years and be proud and will not know what it has cost, and that person is me, and that is why I have got a bag on my belt and not a slate.**”

She said all of that to the wagon wheel and not to him, and then she got up and harnessed the barrow, and the four bolts were made fast at the place the frame was set down, and the frame never came out of the cradle, and nothing was rung, and nothing was broken, and the object was exactly as it had been at the sixth hour of the morning of the thirty-third day in a yard in Kells: the same object, in the same frame, with no clapper in it and no clapper to come.

They got to Fourmile at about the second hour of the afternoon of the forty-first day, into a real town with streets and a market and a Crown district renewal office in it with an address on the door and a letter in Tomas Vale's coat that was signed by a watch captain and had five clauses in it, and one of the two of them had not slept, and the other one had, for four hours, and had written in a yard book the only account of the night that anybody will ever have.
