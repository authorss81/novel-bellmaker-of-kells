# Chapter 0091 — What A Bell Is In A Market

He wrote five sentences on the sill of a room over a draper's shop at about the fourth hour on the seventy-seventh day of the month, in a flat hand, with the hours, and then he read the first four of them out loud to the woman sitting on the end of the bed with her stockings in her hand, and he read the fifth one after that and did not read it out, and the reason he did not read it out is the whole of the day.

The bell was in a sack on the floor by the wall. He had gone down to the smith's yard in that street at about the sixth hour of the morning of the fifty-eighth and paid a man to keep a crate with the sacking off and had not touched it since, and this morning he had gone and got it out of the crate and put it in a sack because a crate is a thing a person carries and a sack is a thing a person lifts, and it weighs fourteen pounds two ounces, and that is a weight.

**It is the only instrument I have ever had that is not a document,** he thought, and then did not write it down, because he had been in this town twenty days and he knew what his own handwriting was for.

---

What he had against himself was a sentence in his own book, and it was on the sixty-second, and he went and found it and read it standing up with his finger on the line.

*I am not going to ring it in this county at all, because there is a bar on a different bell in a different county and I am careful now about things I am not, and that is the whole of what the sixtieth has done to me and I am the one who let it.*

He stood with his finger on it for about a minute and a half.

**Here is the difficulty and it is a difficulty about a preposition.** He had written the sentence in a room in this town and he had been in this town when he wrote it, and *this county* is the county he was standing in, and the county he was standing in is not the county the bar is in, and the whole difficulty is that a man who has been careful for thirty-two years and has learned in twenty days that careful is a way of writing things down ought to have known which county he was in when he wrote the sentence down.

He had known. He had read *this county* as Kells's county, every day since the sixty-second, because Kells is the county the bar is in, and because a sentence about not ringing a thing is naturally a sentence about the county that forbids it.

**And Miremarket is not his county, and the bar does not reach here, and the sentence he wrote binds him in the place he wrote it, and he is in the place he wrote it, and there is nobody in this county who can waive a sentence in a man's own day-book except the man.**

“All right,” he said. “That is the whole of it and it took me about a minute and a half and I have not found a better way round it.”

---

“You have not asked me a question yet and you have told me four things,” said Tamsin Rook. “I am going to read the fifth one.”

“It is not going in a market.”

“**I did not say where it was going. I said I was going to read it.**”

He read it to her. She read it back herself, out loud, in the flat voice she used for other people's sentences, and it took her about nine seconds, and then she put her hand flat on the page and said:

“**No.**”

“That is the second time in a month you have said that word to me about a thing of mine and the first time it cost a man nine pitches and thirty-six pounds a quarter and it is not the same no and I am not going to ask you which one this is.”

“You are not going to ask me because you already know.” She turned the page round. “**That is not yours. It is a household's, and you have been refused twice by the household it belongs to and you went back once anyway and were refused again, and it is the only paper in your coat that is not about a document.**”

“That is right.”

“**And a thing said in a market square that a household would have said is a thing said for a household. And the fifth clause of that receipt says you have no standing to speak for any household in Kells or in any ward, and you asked a clerk in a watch house in another county to write that clause, at about the fifth hour of the morning of the thirty-third, because you were right.**

“**And you were right, and it has cost you the only sentence in this town that anybody wanted to hear.**”

---

He struck the fifth sentence out and left it on the page, in his own hand, with the hour against it, and ruled a line through it and wrote *not said* at the end of the line, and did not tear the page out, and she watched him do it and said nothing at all, which is not something she has done in twenty days.

“**You will not read that page to anybody in that square,**” she said, when he had done.

“No.”

“**And nobody in that square is going to know it exists, and about nine hundred people are going to hear four sentences, and one of the four is the whole of the apology and one of the four is a hole in the middle of it, and I am not going to improve that and neither are you.**”

---

The market was on and there were about nine hundred people in the square by the time he got the barrow to the kerb by the cross, and the barrow was the difficulty and it had been the difficulty for six weeks.

**A barrow will not go at the speed of a question,** and a barrow goes at the speed of a barrow, and there is a Crown object in it in a reinforced padded cradle with four retaining bolts made fast at every place the frame has been set down since the thirty-third, and the second clause says the frame goes into its cradle for the carriage and stays in it, and a frame out of its cradle on a road is a frame broken.

So the barrow stood in the square with a frame in it, four streets away from where the frame was, which is a thing he has got used to, and the market keeper came out of his row at about a quarter to the top of the hour and looked at it and said:

“**That has been in my square since about the fourth hour of the morning and I want it out of my square at the top of the hour, and I am not going to tell you why and the reason is that I have about nine rows and a bell on a block in the middle of them and a barrow is not a bell.**”

“Then that is all of it.”

“You are not arguing.”

“Then do not.”

“**Then put it on the cobbles by the trough and turn the handles round so that a man coming down the middle can get past, and I will say afterwards that I did not throw you out and you will say it back to me and I will not believe you.**”

He put it by the trough with the handles turned in.

---

He had a low block and a folded coat and about a quarter to the hour, and the bell came out of the sack, and there is no wax in it and no dressed groove in it and nothing on it but a caster's mark and a day, and the certificate in Rowan Ketch's hand in his coat is a certificate of metal and of a note and of nothing else whatever, and the man who certified it is a long way behind us and is not in this county and has not been for weeks, and has said in his own hand that a certificate is a promise to a stranger and the stranger has to be alive and in the same county, and that the only thing that lasts about a certificate is a person who saw it be made.

**He did not write to him.** That is on the page and it is the thing he is not proud of, and the reason is in the day-book at the ninth hour of the evening of this day, and the reason is that a man who writes to a master and asks whether he may do a thing is a man asking a room for a permission, and he has spent six weeks learning that a hall is not a person and that a person is the only thing that can be asked and can refuse, and he would have got a document.

At the top of the hour, in a market of about nine hundred people, at a block on the cobbles by the cross, Tomas Vale rang a bell he did not make, once.

**And then he said four sentences out loud, in his own name, and did not raise his voice, because a market does not need a raised voice and he had found that out in a town four counties behind him.**

> *I said one true sentence in a room in this town on the sixty-ninth day of the month in my own name about a household of five that I have no standing in, and it was true, and it cost that household the front of a row of nine pitches, and there is no part of it that anybody else did.*
>
> *I could not stop it and I could not put the row back and I have not said to anybody in this town that it was worth it and nobody is going to come and tell me it was.*
>
> *The rest of it was done by four people and every one of them was right: Elias Nabb, a clerk, who closed a line in dispute in the correct form out of the correct column in about four seconds; Hester Vowles, a district officer, who stopped a practice in this district in writing and said out loud that stopping it was not worth the ninth hour of the evening of the sixty-ninth; a man with a book of his own and no office, who took nine pitches off a market of about nine hundred because a stranger said a true thing in a room; and a woman with a slate, who has not asked me for anything and is not going to be thanked by me for it.*
>
> *Those are the known facts. Here are the unknown ones: I do not know how an office gets into a bell, and I have written that down twice in four counties and got a column and a stamp and a habit; and I do not know where a name goes when it is registered, and nobody has told me; and I have been wrong in this town in a way I cannot put back, and every word of these four sentences is about me and about a bell in my own hand.*

Then he said nothing for about four minutes.

**And he did not improve the four minutes afterwards,** which is the second time in a month and the first one was twenty-four days ago in about nine hundred people, and he has not written that down anywhere and is not going to.

---

**And the bell did not do what a bell does.**

That is the finding and it is in the day-book in the flat hand and he wrote it four times before the one that stands and struck out three of them.

A bell's whole business, the only thing it has ever been for in this kingdom, is to carry one bounded claim that a living person has accepted responsibility for to a place where nobody who rang it has ever stood. That is what a road is. That is the whole of what eleven years of a woman with a slate are for. A man makes a bell so that a thing can go further than a voice, and the going is the point, and the bell is only the mouth.

**He put a claim in a bell in a market square and the claim went to the end of the market street and stopped there.**

Not that it faded. Not that it thinned. It stopped, at the place where the crowd stopped, the way a voice stops. The row of booths down the far side of the square heard nothing at all, and the lane to the landing heard nothing at all, and the smith's yard four streets off heard nothing at all, and about nine hundred people were standing in that square and about two hundred of them heard a note and four sentences, and the rest of them heard a man talking.

**And he does not know whether that is the metal, or the claim, or the crowd, and he did not find out, and he is not going to find out in this county.**

He stood by the block afterwards for about half an hour and did not ask one person what they had heard, and the reason is in the book, and the reason is that a man who stands in a market and asks nine hundred people what they heard is a man who has begun a list, and he has a slate with nine names on it and he is not starting another one.

---

She had come to the square with the slate under her arm and the four columns drawn on it and the chalk out, and she stood at ten feet, at the distance she has used for eleven years, and at about the sixth hour she came and put the chalk in her pocket and gave him the slate, and it was blank.

“You did not write it down.”

“**No. And that is mine and I am not going to explain it and you are not going to ask me, and if you ask me I am going to say that a queue is a place and a slate is a column and a column is a stamp, and I have watched a man work that out in about four days and I have not got a better sentence than that and I am not going to look for one.**”

He took it and did not look at it, and it is blank on both sides, and that is the only blank slate in this business.

---

The market keeper found him at about the seventh hour, at the trough, and he did not say *was it worth it* and he did not say *that was a strange thing to do* and he said:

“**You put a clerk's name in nine hundred people's mouths this morning and I am going to tell you what that costs him and I am not going to tell you what it costs you.**”

“Say it, then.”

“**Four days. Maybe five. For about four days, nobody in that hall will be able to shut a line.**”

“Why.”

“**Because a clerk can be named. That is the whole of it. It has not happened in this town in my lifetime and I have been in this square thirty years and I have watched four things happen in it and none of them was a stranger with a bell naming a man who did his job.**” He put his hands in his pockets. “**There is a notice on that wall nine feet inside that door and it means what it says, which is that anybody may ask a clerk about the work. It has been on that wall since before I could read and about four men in this kingdom have ever put a boot through it. And there are about nine hundred people in this town who have been told a clerk's name this morning, and about four of them will come and ask, and two of the four will be people with a grievance and one of the two will be right, and that clerk will have to stand there and answer them in his own name, and that is the notice working. I am not going to stop defending that notice because it is inconvenient.**”

“Yes,” said Tomas Vale.

“**You are not going to take it back.**”

“**No. I put him in on purpose. If I do not, then in about four days this town will have decided that the man with a book took a row off, or the district officer sent a cart, or the man in charge of the shelves did it out of spite, and two of those three are right and neither of them did it. And a man who had about four seconds and did the only thing there was to do, in the correct form, out of the correct column, was going to spend the rest of his life in this town as the man nobody could name. I would rather he was the man I named in a square.**”

“**That is the worst argument I have heard this month and I have heard about four.**”

“It is the true one. I am not going to be told it was worth it and I am not going to call it worth it, and if anybody in this town comes and tells me it was, I will write down that they said it and the hour.”

---

He wrote the last entry of the seventy-seventh at about the ninth hour of the evening, on the step of the draper's, in the flat hand, with the hours, and he filled the first and third columns and left the second and the fourth as they were, which is a decision and is written down, and it is the longest thing he has written in six weeks.

> *The seventy-seventh. I rang it. Once, in a market of about nine hundred people, at the top of the hour, at a block on the cobbles by the cross, and I said four sentences and then I said nothing for about four minutes and I have not improved the four minutes and I am not going to.*
>
> *Let it be written flat and then I will shut this book.*
>
> ***A claim in metal is a thing a man cannot edit.*** I have spent six weeks writing sentences down and correcting them and ruling columns and improving a figure and putting the hour against it, and this morning I stood in nine hundred people and said four sentences and could not add a paragraph to them, could not add a fifth, could not take one back, could not put the hour on them, and could not show anybody the page it was on. **That is the first instrument I have ever had that is not a document, and there is nobody in any building in this kingdom who can ask to see it, and that is the whole of what it is worth, and it took me twenty days to find out and I am not going to make more of it than that.**
>
> *And the fifth sentence, which is on this page, ruled through, with the hour against it, and which is the only part of it anybody in that square wanted: **I am not allowed to say it. Not because of a rule I have not read and not because of an officer. Because a household of five refused me twice and I went back once and was refused again, and because the fifth clause of a receipt I asked a clerk to add to at about the fifth hour of the morning of the thirty-third says in words that I have no standing to speak for any household in Kells or in any ward. And I asked for that clause because I was right, and about nine hundred people have just heard an apology with the middle taken out of it and not one of them knows, and that is the correct price and I chose it.**
>
> *Third column, ruled apart, the hours flat against them, and I am merging nothing: **four people did the rest and every one of them was right, and I said so out loud, and one of the four has a name and a house and a child and he is going to be named in this town four times a day for about four days, and I did that on purpose and I am not going to be told it was worth it.**
>
> *And the bell. **It did not do what a bell does.** A bell carries one claim to a place where the ringer has never stood, and that is the whole of a bell, and the whole of a road is that it goes. I put a claim in it in a market square and the claim went to the end of the market street and stopped, the way a voice stops, and the booths at the far side heard nothing and the lane to the landing heard nothing and the smith's yard four streets off heard nothing, and about nine hundred people were standing in that square and about two hundred of them heard a note.*
>
> *I do not know whether that is the metal, or the claim, or the crowd. **I have never learned where a warning lands. I have never once in my life learned it, I said so in this book on the sixty-second, I have not learned it in this town and I am not going to learn it, and I am not going to be told it by anybody as a favour.** Nobody in that square knows what they heard and neither do I, and that is a thing that happened and not a thing that is coming.*
>
> *Nothing else was rung. The frame is four streets off with four bolts made fast in it and no clapper and a receipt in five clauses and all five of them stand. The bell was in a sack on the floor of a room over a draper's shop for about four hours this morning and it is in a sack now, and I have not written to Rowan Ketch and I am not going to, and the reason is that I would have got a document.*
>
> *And there is a woman who was ten feet off with a slate and did not write one word on it, and she has not asked me for anything, and I am not going to ask her about it, and I am not going to thank her and she is not going to let me.*
