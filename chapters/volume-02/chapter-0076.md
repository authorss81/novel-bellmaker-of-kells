# Chapter 0076 — The Method Is Right And Does Not Work Here

He laid it all out on the table in the room over the draper's on the morning of the sixty-second and it took him about an hour, and after this morning there is not going to be a second morning like it.

Ten things on the table, and ten is the number he counts and not the number there are, because four of them came out of one office on one morning and are one line in a book in a coat. And a custody receipt with five clauses in it, and a barrow's load in a yard with the four bolts made fast in a corner of it.

**One.** A dated copy of a Crown supplementary return, six years old, one household of six, a woman's own hand, out of a woman's own drawer in a lane two days' walk behind him, with a condition attached.

**Two.** Four sheets from Fourmile, three answers in an officer's own name and one refusal with a reason, and a carbon of a form with no box for a reason on it and a man's reading-out of a hundred-and-ninetieth entry on the back of it, and a sheet in the same hand about what a book of refusals is and will be in about four years.

**Three.** A sheet in a deputy's hand at a counter in another county, given to him and read twice, eleven minutes, and the only one of them not written by a man who was being careful.

**Four.** A certificate in a master's hand, six witnesses, one of whom wrote his own name, no second copy, the only certificate in the world for a bell with no claim in it.

**Five.** A sheet in an auditor's hand at about the second hour of the afternoon on the fifty-fifth, in a room over a haberdasher's shop, and the number he gave is not in any record and never has been and his head is not a record.

**Six.** A sheet in a clerk's hand at about the eighth hour of the evening on the sixtieth, on that office's own paper, with a child and a house and about eleven years in it, and a sentence at the bottom saying he has never been asked what the mark means and is not sure and is not going to ask.

**Seven.** A river-post receipt for eleven pence, a Crown document, dated the fifty-second, which is the cheapest of the ten and the only one of the ten that told him nothing.

**Eight.** A letter from a woman at a water bell, four lines and a paragraph, dated the seventh hour, refusing, and giving a date instead of a name.

**Nine.** A loose leaf folded in four, in a smith's book, which he has opened once, on purpose, in front of that smith, and it did not confirm itself, and he put it back the same way and told nobody, and there is one page in this book that says he is leaving something out and the thing left out is not on it.

**Ten.** A day-book in four columns with the hours in a flat hand and nothing merged, including the parts where he is wrong.

And the other thing, which is not a document and is not in the book either, and is a slate bought for a penny and a half with a fact on it in a woman's own hand: **a book kept in a kitchen drawer at the end of a lane, in the same woman's own writing, in which five people are named against days, and which disagrees with a roll in a hall in eleven places, and which that woman will not take out of her house for any office in this kingdom, and for a reason that is correct.**

That was the case. It had taken him twenty-nine days and it was **ten things and a slate**, and it was **entirely correct**, and he read it in from one end at about the fifth hour of the morning and it did not have a single false thing in it, and not one of the ten was a lie, and the whole of it was true, and the whole of it did not add up to anything at all.

---

He asked her to be in the room.

He asked her at about the seventh hour of the morning on the sixty-second, and he did it in the form the slate requires, and he said what he wanted it for, and he did not say it in a lane.

“I am going into that hall at about the fourth hour of the afternoon and I am going to put **the three answers in three hands** in front of a counter, and I am going to say out loud, in my own name, the question you gave me on a road on the fortieth, which I have been carrying for twenty-two days. **I am going to tell them what a woman at the end of a lane told me on the fifty-ninth, in her own words, and I am going to tell them I have not got her book and am not going to have it, because the book is in a kitchen drawer and she has given me the reason and the reason is right.** And I am going to do it in a room and I am not going to be alone in it, and I have not got anybody else, and there is nobody in this town whose name I am allowed to put in a book.”

“No,” said Tamsin Rook.

He waited, which he had been warned about in four counties.

“I am not going to explain it,” she said, “and I am not going to be asked twice, and the reason is not the fourth of last month and it is not the slate and it is not that I think you will do something stupid. **The reason is that the question is mine and you are going to put it, and if I am in the room then what happens in that room this afternoon is a thing that happened to two people, and it is going to be about two people for about nine years, and I have been the second name on a piece of paper four times in eleven years and one of them was about a child.**”

“I am not going to write your name in it.”

“You are not going to write my name in it and that is not the half of it.” She put the bag down. “The half of it is that **I have a question in a clerk’s book four counties behind us with my name at the head of it and the hour against it and nobody can take it out, and I put it there on purpose, on the fifty-fifth, at about the fifth hour of the afternoon, with my eyes open, and I priced it.** And I am not going to walk into another room in the same county and make myself the second name of the same question, and I would be doing it for a man with a barrow and I would not have been doing it the first time.”

“All right,” said Tomas Vale.

“Write that down. **All right, and the hour, and that I said the whole of it in one go and that he did not ask me a second time**, and it goes in the third column and not the fourth, because it is not a thing nobody could answer.”

He wrote it down. It is in the day-book entire and it is the sixth use of the leash counting Mercy Slee's writing of the slate on the thirty-first, and it is the first one that has cost anybody anything, and the cost was not hers.

**The cost was that he went into a room at about the fourth hour of the afternoon on the sixty-second, alone, with a question that was hers, and got back a thing he had nobody to read it back to.**

He is a man who reads everything back. He has read four columns back to a chandler, to a carter, to a dyer, to a woman with a barrow, to a woman taking in washing, to a clerk at a counter, to a master in a room with eleven people in it. **He went into a room and came out of it with the finding of his life to date and there was nobody in a town of two thousand people he could say it to, and that is the whole of what the refusal cost, and he paid it himself and he was not permitted to be consoled about it.**

---

## The counter

He went in at about the fourth hour of the afternoon and he said, before anything else, and out loud, so that nobody could say afterwards that he had not:

“**I am not asking for a document, a copy, a page, a sight of a shelf, or an entry. I am not asking for anything. I am asking one question and I am doing it in my own name, and I am told by the notice on your wall that anybody may ask, and I would like to know whether the notice means it.**”

Ivo Trant put down a pen.

“It means it,” he said. “It has meant it since before I came and it is the only thing in this building that nobody has ever used. **Do you want it answered by the hall or by the clerk at the counter?**”

He said it as though he were offering a man a choice of two railings, and Tomas Vale found that he was about four seconds behind and that he did not want to catch up.

“By the clerk at the counter.”

“That is correct and it is what I would have said. There is no person in this hall to answer it and there has not been one for about nine years.” He turned round. “**Bryn. A person with a question, and I have told him what it is not, and he is to answer it or say plainly that he cannot.**”

Bryn Pethick came out of the room behind and had ink on the side of his hand and was about as pleased as a man can be at about the fourth hour of the afternoon, and he put a chair at the wrong side of the counter, which is a thing a clerk does for a person and not for a superior.

“Sit down. **What is the document.**”

Tomas Vale did not answer, and it was not a trick and it was not a stall, and Bryn Pethick looked at him and then said, “Oh,” and put his hand flat on the counter.

“You have asked me that before.”

“You told me on the fifty-eighth that I had to ask you properly and I have been carrying it about four days as though it were a form.”

“Then I am going to say it once and I am going to say it as the office and not as me, and then I am going to do something I have never done in four years, and I am doing it because you came on your own and I have read your face and you have come to be told no and you would rather have it in about nine seconds.”

He said it in about nine seconds, and it was the third time, and it was the same sentence.

> *A record. It is a record of the answer given, entered in the answering column against the line, in the order in which the answers were given. It is the only thing it is. It is not a household’s answer, and it is not a person’s answer, and it is not a copy of either one. The office obtains an answer from a person, and the entry is made by the office, and the office is not in dispute between anybody in this room.*

And then Tomas Vale put three sheets of paper on the counter face up, side by side, in three hands.

**Pell Rill's, in a town two days up the water, dated the fifty-fifth at about the second hour of the afternoon.**

**Elias Nabb's, on this office's own paper, dated the sixtieth at about the eighth hour of the evening, with a sentence at the bottom about a mark he has filled in nine hundred times a year and has never been asked about.**

**And a deputy's, at a counter four counties back, in a room he was in on the forty-eighth, eleven minutes, in a hand that was not being careful: *it is a record of what this office holds to be the answer of a household, and it is not a record of what any household said.***

Bryn Pethick read all three, standing up, with his hands behind his back, and it took him about a minute and a half, and then he squared all three of them on the counter, which nobody had asked him to do.

“That is the office's position in three counties,” he said, “in three hands, and **all three of those men are right, and I am going to say something to you now and I have thought about it for about ninety seconds and I am going to say it anyway.**

“**I have never seen it said better than that auditor said it and I have had a room of one in this trade for four years and I have read four hundred sheets off that counter and not one of them was written by anybody who had to be right, and I have read your face for about two days and I have known who you were since the fifty-eighth and I have said nothing to anybody in this building, and I would like you to know that and I would like you to know that I was not being kind, I was being lazy, and being lazy was cheaper.**”

“Thank you,” said Tomas Vale.

“Do not thank me. **You have three documents and you have come to this counter to make them into a thing, and I am going to tell you what they are, and then I am going to show you the thing that is at the end of them, and it is not in any of the three and you are not going to like it.**”

---

## The line

He had to go and get it. That was the part Tomas Vale did not expect: that a clerk would have to go and get a thing to answer a question, and that it would take eleven minutes, and that Bryn Pethick came back with a sheet on a board and set it down in front of himself and not in front of the counter.

“**This is not the roll. I am going to show you a line, and I am allowed to show you a line, and I am not going to show you a book, and if you ask me for a book I will say no and it will not be unkind.**”

It was a ruled sheet with about forty lines on it and a column down the left with a number in it, and there were four columns to the right of that and the second of them was about the width of a thumb.

**The fourth column was the best one on the sheet.** There was no box for a reason on it. There was a box for what the office has done, and it was four inches wide, and everybody who came into that hall for eleven years had written in it, and it was the only box on any paper in this town that was big enough for what actually happened.

Bryn put his finger on the nineteenth line.

“**Household of five, the east lane, answered and closed.** That is the whole of the entry. Read it to me.”

Tomas Vale read it.

*The household was asked on the sixtieth. The household has answered in its own words in the answering column, against the line, in the hand of —*

*And then, under it, in the same hand, and the same pen, and about two inches below:*

*The household withdraws the answer given on the sixtieth. The household stands by the answer of the sixtieth and does not send a return this year.*

*And in the fourth column, four inches wide, in a clerk's hand, in ink, with an hour against it:*

*Closed.*

That is the whole of the nineteenth line. There is no more of it and there is nothing under it and there is no footnote and there is no copy of it anywhere in that hall because there is no copy of any of it anywhere in that hall.

“**Read the second entry again,”** said Bryn Pethick.

“The household withdraws the answer given on the sixtieth.”

“That is a household that was asked and did not like the question and has changed its mind **two days later**, and there is a man in this town — no. There is a person in that house who did it, in their own hand, in their own words, with the hour on it, and the office has taken it, and the office has entered it, and the office has honoured it, and the office will honour it for ever, and **that is correct, and there is nothing wrong with that, and it is the best-kept column in this hall.**”

“It is honoured.”

“It is honoured. That is what I said. It is in the column, in the name, with the hour. If the whole of this hall is torn up and burned tomorrow, that is the page a person would be able to stand in front of a district and say *I did that, on that day, and here it is, and it is mine, and it was not taken off me.*” He kept both hands behind the counter. “**And the line is closed.**”

Tomas Vale looked at it for long enough that a cart went past outside.

“Who closed it.”

“**I did.** That is my hand in the fourth column and that is the hour. **There is no household signature on the closing. There is no household signature anywhere on this sheet, because there is no box for one, and there has not been one on this form in about forty years.**”

“Then what does the closing need.”

And Bryn Pethick said it the way a man says a price, which is what it is, and he said it without any weight at all, because he has said it about nine hundred times a year for four years.

“**A signature and a stamp.** That is all. **One signature, and a stamp, and that is what a line is.**”

---

He did not shout. He had come five weeks to shout at a thing and there was nothing in that room to shout at, and he sat on a chair at the wrong side of a clerk's hall counter in a town of two thousand people and had a thing come apart in his hands, and it came apart over about eleven minutes, and the eleven minutes were the quietest he has ever had.

**And this is the finding, and it is the finding of the volume, and he wrote it out that night in his own hand and did not improve it and it is in the day-book entire:**

*An answer is a person. That has been the whole of my argument for five weeks and for six weeks before that and for most of a year, and it is true, and it is the best sentence anybody has given me.*

***And the roll does not need anybody's answer.***

*It needs one signature and a stamp.*

*There is a line in front of me with a household's answer in it, in a household's own words, in a household's own hand, and a withdrawal under it two days later in the same hand, and the withdrawal is honoured, and the column is kept, and the hour is on it, and the column is the best-kept column in that hall and the clerk who keeps it is not hiding and is not sorry and is not a coward and is right.*

***And the line is closed.***

*There is no signature on the closing. There is no box for one. There has not been one on that form in about forty years. What the closing requires is a signature and a stamp, and the signature is the clerk's, and the stamp is the office's, and **not one person in that household has to have said anything at all, ever, about the line being closed, and the roll will say that the line was answered, and the roll is right, and there is no answer on that roll, and there never has been one, and I have spent five weeks looking for a person in a column and there is not one and there is not supposed to be one.***

*And the whole of what I have brought down a river with a barrow and a cradle and ten sheets of paper for is an argument about consent. **A roll is not a document about consent. It is a document about a line being closed.** Everything the ward believes about a bell is about who has the right to say a thing and to take it back. A roll is about a piece of paper's work being finished. Nobody has to be wicked. Nobody has to be tired. Nobody has to be in a hurry. A clerk is handed a form with a box for what the office has done, and the box is four inches wide, and it is the best box on the paper, and it is for a word.*

*The word is closed.*

---

And then the second thing, which is not a finding and is not a power and which he wrote about nine days later, at the back of the book, in the flyleaf where he keeps the things he does not understand, and it is the only page in this book that is in his own hand and is about a bell, and there are nine words on it and no more:

> *A thing that comes out of a bell a long way off arrives thinner, rounder and more certain than it went in.*
>
> *A roll is the same instrument, made of paper.*
>
> *It is worse, because nobody has to stand in a lane to ring it.*

That is all. It is in the book and it is not a power and there is nothing he can do with it and it is not a perception and it is not a second sense and he did not learn where anything went. It is a thing he noticed about two objects, and he is a man who notices about objects, and it has taken him a month to be allowed to compare them and there is no clerk in this kingdom who can do it and no district officer and no auditor, and the whole of its value is that it is the only sentence he has found in five weeks that nobody else has said out loud, and it cannot be put in a form, and he has got no household and no standing and no name in any of this.

He wrote underneath it, and it is the same page and it is the sentence that decides the rest of the day:

> *That is a fact and not a power. I want that written where I cannot improve it. I have not learned where a warning lands. I have never known that and I do not know it now and there is nothing in this town that would tell me.*

---

Trant said one thing to him at the door at about the sixth hour of the evening, and it was the last thing said to him in Miremarket by anybody in a hall, and it was not kind and it was not unkind.

“You came in twice and asked for a document and got a person, and a person is a more expensive thing for us than a document and it took him about eleven minutes and I have nine hundred lines this year.” He shut the door on the latch. “**Go and do something with it, or do not, and either way do not stand in my street again.**”

He went back to the yard behind the draper's shop and the four bolts were made fast in the corner where the frame had stood since the sixth hour of the morning of the fifty-eighth, and it was still in its cradle, and nothing had been rung, and the receipt was whole.

And he sat down on the step of the draper's with a slate bought for a penny and a half on his knee and a fact about eleven days in a kitchen drawer and eleven places where a kitchen drawer and a hall did not agree, and he did not build a case, and he did not denounce anything, and he wrote for two hours, in four columns, with the hours in the flat hand, and **he filled all four of them**, and the fourth column — the column for a thing nobody could answer — had one line in it, and the line was a date and a stamp and the word *closed*, and it was not a thing nobody could answer.

> *The sixty-second. I have lost the argument and I have kept the method and I am writing them down separately because I have got them in the wrong order and have had them in the wrong order since the thirty-second.*
>
> *What I lost, and it went in about eleven minutes on the counter of a clerk's hall in a town of two thousand people, and nobody in that room did anything to me: **I lost the last thing I came here for, which was a person to be angry at who was not kind to me.** I have been refused by four buildings and every one of the refusals was a man I could work on. In this town I have met four people and every one of them was kind to me and every one of them was right, and one of them has a name at the top of a return and will not explain a column and told me so in advance, and one of them has a number that is in no record and put it in his head and said his head is not a record, and one of them has a child and a house and eleven years and put a sentence on a sheet for me that he did not have to write, and one of them is about four years into a job and showed me a line nobody asked him to show and said *that is all a line needs* the way a man says a price he has said all his life, and **not one of them has lied to me and not one of them is hiding and not one of them can be used.**
>
> *And that is not the whole of what I lost. I lost the belief, and the belief was the thing I was carrying and not the method, and I have not noticed until this afternoon what the difference is.*
>
> ***I have been carrying a method since the thirty-second and I have been carrying a belief that the method was mine.*** Four columns, a slate and a witness is the correct answer to a question about a claim. It is correct. It has been correct for five weeks and it is correct today and I have written down the whole of why it is correct in the third column tonight, and the belief was not a method. The belief was that being right was a thing I could carry into a room, and it turns out that being right is not a thing anybody can be used, and I have had that in writing from two separate men in two separate rooms in the last two days and I did it anyway and wrote it down twice, and the belief is the part that has to go and the method stays and I have got four columns and a slate and about nine days and a woman who has not spoken to me since the seventh hour because I asked her to come to a room and she said no, once, in one word, and she was right, and it is the first time in six weeks that she has refused me and the refusal has cost me the only reader I have ever had and she is the only person who was ever going to tell me I was wrong before I was.*
>
> *And here is what I have left, and I am putting it in the fourth column and I have not merged it and it is not a consolation and it is not a stage and there is nothing in this book that is a stage and there is not going to be one.*
>
> ***I can be useful to one household at a time. That is all. That is the whole of the remainder of this.***
>
> *Not nine. Not a district. One house, at a time, at a door, in their own words, in a column of their own choosing, and it changes nothing at the level of a hall, and there are about eleven thousand of them, and I have nine days, and the engine is a column and a stamp and a habit, and a habit is not a man and there is nobody in this kingdom I can be angry at, and the crate is in a smith's yard in this street with the sacking off and the bell in it has no claim in it and I have not rung it since the fifty-third and I am not going to ring it in this county at all, because there is a bar on a different bell in a different county and I am careful now about things I am not, and that is the whole of what the sixtieth has done to me and I am the one who let it.*
