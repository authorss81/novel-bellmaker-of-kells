# Chapter 0054 — The Map With the Corners Cut

The thirty-seventh day is four lines in his book and the four lines are about a bad ford and a dog.

The inn is a coaching inn with a yard, and there is a coach office in a lean-to off the yard with a counter in it and a rack of nailed boards behind the counter, and the boards hold timetables and road sheets, and the road sheets are the cheapest paper in the building and they cost a farthing.

“Can I ask you a thing about your paper,” Tamsin Rook said to the man behind the counter, “and I want to say before I do that I am not asking you about a timetable and I am not asking you about a fare.”

The man looked at the barrow in the yard, and at the frame in the cradle on it, and then at her.

“You are the courier,” he said. “You are going to ask me about the paper and the man with the bell is going to stand four feet off and look at the board.”

“He is not going to come in,” Tamsin Rook said. “I have not asked him. I asked him in the lane below the mill and he said no before I had finished the sentence, and I may not ask him again today, and I am telling you that so that you know the two of us have just used up the whole of what one of us is allowed to say to the other and that neither of us is going to be surprised later.”

“Fair,” said the man. “Fair, and I have not had a person ask me about paper in about four years, and I will tell you before I answer anything what you are walking into, which is that there is a bench outside and the bench is better than this counter and I will not sell you a bad thing and I will not sell you a good thing either, and everything on that board is the same price and most of it is wrong.”

---

She bought four. It took her the length of a conversation about the price.

The sheet is a road sheet, and a road sheet in this country is a single fold of cheap paper, printed on both sides, and it is bought by carriers, by coachmen, by anybody who is going to be answerable about where they were. The front carries the roads, the ferries, the bridges, the county stones, the coaching stages and their times, and the market towns with the days. The back carries the districts, the post runs, the tide table for the upper and lower water, and the **panels**: five small ruled boxes giving, for each district a carrier may work in, the name of the office where a *return* is to be addressed, the days it is received, and the days it goes out again.

She had carried a road sheet of this kind for eleven years. She knew the panels the way she knew a face.

“Where are they,” she said.

The man turned round and looked at her for a moment.

“They are not there,” he said.

“Where are they.”

“They are not printed,” Ruben Sallow said, “that edition is out of print, they are not printed any more, there is a new plate and I will show you the new plate.”

And he took a sheet off the top of the rack and spread it flat on the counter and put his finger on the bottom left corner and said, *there.*

It was there. The five panels were on the new sheet and they were ruled and they had a printer's name in the corner and the office names were in them, and she read them standing up, four names, and she was right and he was right, and that was the whole of the first four minutes.

Then she went back to the sheet she had bought.

---

Here is what a road sheet looks like when it has been through a week on a barrow.

The fold is gone. The creases have gone white and then gone soft. The corner of the front where a carrier's thumb goes — and every carrier's thumb goes in the same place, on the left, two inches down, because the left hand holds the barrow and the right hand holds the sheet and nobody holds a sheet by the top corner — is black with eleven years of one. The tide table at the bottom right has a thumb mark over the lower water at every page. The coaching times at the top have a fold across the times for the stage that comes through this inn, because people fold the sheet to that line.

None of that is wear. Wear is what a road does to a thing and it is random in distribution and it does not care what a thing is *for*.

What the sheet in her hand had, in the fifth panel on the back, was a rectangle about two inches by one and a half in which the paper was smooth, and uncreased, and the colour of a sheet that has been in a drawer, and in which the ink was a very slightly different black, and in which the two column rules that the other four panels had were not.

The other four panels had their column rules.

Somebody had taken the fifth panel out of that part of the map with a knife, and had done it to the plate and not to the copies, and had done it in the middle of the run and not at the beginning or the end, because the paper in that rectangle is the same age and the same colour as the paper around it and the tear is the tear of a sheet that was handled and not of a sheet that was worn.

That is not an opinion. That is the same reading a courier applies to a bell, and Tamsin Rook had traced a theft once by taking a handbell apart on a step in Kells in the first week of the month and looking at the inside of a crown where a man had been with a chisel.

**Somebody has taken out the part of the map that says where an answer goes.** Panel five is the panel for the district above this inn, and panel five is the panel that says, in three columns, the name of an office, the day a return is received, and the day it goes out again.

---

Ruben Sallow watched her hold a sheet flat on a counter with one thumb and look at nothing in particular for about four minutes.

“It's missing, isn't it,” he said.

“Yes.”

“They are not printed,” Ruben Sallow said. “That edition is out of print, they are not printed any more, there is a new plate and I will show you the new plate.”

“You said that four minutes ago,” Tamsin Rook said, “and I have been holding this sheet for four minutes and it has not changed, and you have not offered to show me the new plate again, and a man who is about to lie twice usually offers twice.”

He looked at the door. The door was open. The yard was open. There was a man with a barrow at the drinking trough and a coachman's boy on a wall and about eleven people within sight.

“If it was printed,” Ruben Sallow said, “it is not printed, and I sell paper and I do not sell a case. Mrs Sallow, next door, will tell you what I am, and she has told two Crown men what I am and one of them wrote it down, and I would like you to go and ask her.”

---

“No,” Tamsin Rook said.

“No?”

“I am not going to go and ask her, and I am not going to tell you a thing about a plate to a man who has just told me a thing about a plate that is not true, and I am not going to do either of those because I do not want a printer in a shop in a town four miles past my own one to have an afternoon about me.” She put the sheet down flat and squared it with her thumbnail. “I am going to ask you one question and it is a fair one and you are entitled to not answer it.”

“Go on.”

“Who orders a road sheet printed,” she said, “and what are they paid by — by the sheet, or by the year?”

Ruben Sallow did not answer for about four seconds, and the four seconds were the whole of the chapter, because in them a man in a shop worked out that the woman at his counter was not asking him a question at all.

“They buy by the sheet,” he said. “The district buys. The district has bought that sheet from that printer every year since the year I came in here, and they send a standing order in the spring, and they send a number, and the number has not changed in six years, and the number has not gone up with the price, and that is not my business and that is the kind of thing that is not my business.”

“How many sheets.”

“That is not my business either.”

“I am not asking you to sell me another one,” Tamsin Rook said. “I am asking whether a district that buys the same number of sheets every year wants that number in a village of about two hundred people, or whether it wants that number where a return is addressed.”

Ruben Sallow put both hands on the counter.

“They're not the same number and nobody has ever asked me,” he said.

---

She bought five more of the same sheet, and had them wrapped, and the wrapping was done in a moment by a girl who had been listening to the whole of it from four feet away and had not moved.

“You cannot ask that man to show you the plate,” the girl said at the counter, not to Tamsin, to the air. “He will not show you the plate. He has never shown anybody the plate. He showed a Crown man the plate once and the Crown man wrote a number down and he has not shown anybody anything since.”

“Thank you,” Tamsin Rook said.

“He will not show you the plate,” the girl said again, and went and did something else.

---

The map is the batch's first piece of paper that is a document about the maps.

Tomas Vale got a look at it in the yard, sitting on the barrow's shaft with it open on his knees, and he said the correct thing, which was that the road is all there and the ferries are all there and the coaching times are all there and every road a carrier can work is on that sheet with the county stone marked, and that the sheet is as good a sheet as this country prints.

He did not look at the back.

He did not look at the back because a man holding a road sheet on a barrow looks at the *roads* on a road sheet, and she held it open at the roads the whole time he was getting the horse fed, and when she turned it over to show him the panel he was looking at the tide table.

And then when she showed him the fourth panel, which was complete, he said *that is a good panel*, and she said *it is*, and he said *the district has thought of something there, naming the day the return goes out*, and she said *it has*, and he said *that saves a carrier a week*, and she said *it does*, and he said *somebody in a district office has sat down and worked out that a return that takes eleven days to get out of a building is a return that gets answered late*, and she said *yes*, and he said *that is the Crown being competent at the one thing that is actually its business*, and she said *yes*, and then neither of them said another word about the fifth panel until the light had gone along the yard wall.

It was she who put her thumb on it in the end, and she said: *Look at this one.*

And he looked at it.

And he said: *That is a gap.*

And she said: *That is a gap.*

And he said, *There is a hole in the map.*

And she said, *There is a hole in the map, and it is a hole in the part of the map that says where an answer goes, and it is in the same place in every copy, and it is not an edition, and the man in the shop has just told me a thing about the plate that is not true, and I have not accused him of anything and I am not going to, and you may write your own name under it or under the words I said first.*

Tomas Vale wrote in his book, and what he wrote was: *Fifth panel of the north district, gone, in every copy, in the same rectangle, in the middle of a run. Not an edition. Not a fire. T.R.R. saw it at the counter. I saw it afterwards, in the yard, on a barrow, four minutes late, having looked at the tide table.*

---

They crossed the county stone on the second morning, and it is a low grey block with a county letter on one face and nothing at all on the other, and there is a woman who keeps a book at a cottage by it who does not stop anybody and only stops a man who tries to move it.

The day-book entry for the crossing is four lines long and the fourth is: *Bar is on the bell and not on the licence, and the bell is in a sealed yard four days' walk behind us, and I want it written down that the line stops here and not one yard further, and that I have thought about that for two days and have not improved on it.*

And on the map, in the margin of the front, where the sheet's tradesmen put their four lines of paid space under the coaching times, there are eleven of them, and the fourth from the bottom on the far side of the county stone is a smith, and his four lines say:

*AMOS TREWIN, SMITH. METAL SOLD WITH A RECORD.*
*Bellmetal, fittings, wire, and honest work.*
*Ask for the book.*

Nobody in this batch had seen that line before. It had been on every road sheet printed for six years in a column of tradesmen's advertisements in a hand about two inches high, and Tamsin Rook found it at the fourth hour of the afternoon at the top of a rise, and she read it aloud to a man who was looking at the tide table.
