# Chapter 0056 — The Name on the Stock Book

He went back to the smithy at about the sixth hour on the fortieth day with a sheet of paper in his hand, and it is four lines long and it is the reason in writing that he promised, and it reads:

*Mr Trewin. I am a bellmaker with no shop and no stock and no licence in a workshop, and there is a bar on the only bell I have ever made and it is on the bell and not on me. I want nine years of your households column because I am trying to find out whether a Crown office finds a house by asking a question or by reading a list, and I cannot find that out without one list to put beside another, and I will not copy it and I will not give it to anybody without saying so, and if you say no I will not come back about it.*

*— Tomas Vale, maker, of Kells. Nothing here is Crown property. Nothing here is mine either.*

Amos Trewin read it standing in his own doorway at about the sixth hour, and gave it back, and said four words.

“That is the first reason anybody has ever given me for wanting a page of a book.”

He did not sell him the page.

---

But he did the other thing, and he did it unasked, and he did it because Tomas Vale had said *the reason in writing* and had put *nothing here is mine either* in it, and a man in a stone shop with nine years of his own handwriting in it took about four minutes to decide what to do with that.

He went and got the book and turned it to a year four years back and put it on the bench and opened it at a place, and stood with his finger on it, and said nothing at all, which is a smith's way of saying *look.*

It was the eighth column. *Where it was to.*

“Four years back, in the spring,” Amos Trewin said, “the district sent a cart round this county. Not a Crown man in a black case. A cart and two men and an order, and they bought gate iron and bar and handcart loads of the stuff you make a gate out of, and they paid me with a document out of a store in Fourmile with a date and a place of purchase on it, and I wrote it in the ninth column and I have never thought about that again until about four minutes ago.”

“Who was the order in the name of.”

“Now that,” said Amos Trewin, “is the interesting bit and I have never had anybody to say it out loud to, so I am going to say it out loud and you may do as you like with it. The order was in the name of no man. It was in the name of a place. It said *the district, for the ward of —*, and there were four wards on the one sheet, and four streets under each ward, and the whole of it was on one sheet in one hand, and I put it in my own book because I write what I am asked to write and the man gave me the sheet.”

He turned the book round so that the sheet-line could be read, and it was a line of a Crown paper that had been copied into a smith's book, and the hand was a clerk's, and the hand was careful.

“Do you still have the sheet,” Tomas Vale said.

“No. He took it back and he was entitled to and I let him, and I would have let him, and that is not the part that has been sitting on me since the spring.” Amos Trewin put his hand flat on his own book. “The part that has been sitting on me is this. That sheet is a list of streets. Not of houses — of streets, under four wards, and each street has a length on it and a quantity of iron against it. And it came into my shop on the back of a cart with a man on it, and it went out of my shop in my own hand into the eighth column of a book that has never left this building, and neither one of those two things is a crime, and neither of them is a secret, and I have never in my life been asked by anybody what I write in a book about a place.”

“Then somebody has your book.”

“No,” Amos Trewin said. “Nobody has my book. Listen to me, because you have got that look and I have seen that look on about four men in forty years and it is always the look of a man who has found a door and wants to go through it, and there is no door. Nobody has my book. There is one book, it is on that bench, and it is nine years old and forty years before that, and nobody has ever asked me for it and nobody ever has, and I am not frightened of it, and I want to be very clear with you about that because you are about to write something in a book tonight and I would rather you wrote the true thing than the easy thing.”

“All right,” Tomas Vale said. “Say the true thing.”

“The true thing is that the district does not need my book,” Amos Trewin said. “The district made its own. Four years ago, in the spring, a clerk came down this road with a list and a copyist and did not stop at one house. He read out names at four doors. He asked nobody anything. He filled the list in anyway, and the list is Crown paper, and the list is in Crown hands, and it was made lawfully on a Crown road, and I heard about it from my wife, who heard it from the woman at the second door, and I have never asked the district about it and I have never wanted to and I would think a very small amount of any man who came into this shop and offered me a great deal of money for a page.”

He shut the book.

“You are not going to get the page. And you are not going to go up the road looking for a clerk who stole a list out of a smith's shop, because he did not steal one, and there is nothing in this county to find.”

---

The thing that stayed in Tomas Vale's chest for two days afterwards was not the sheet of streets.

It was the sentence about the copyist.

*A clerk came down this road with a list and a copyist and did not stop at one house. He read out names at four doors. He asked nobody anything. He filled the list in anyway.*

Tamsin Rook had a woman with a bolt of cloth on the towpath on the thirty-fourth, four days ago and a day's walk behind them, saying the same words about a clerk and a satchel and a list and nine days later the same four doors, and neither of them had any idea the other existed, and both of them had been told it by a person who had no reason to join them.

Two lists. Four years apart. Two clerks, or one clerk twice, or a clerk and a man after him who was shown the method. One of them got into a smith's book by the ordinary lawful way, because a shop keeps a book of what it sold and to whom, and the shop did not know what it was being used for, and the district paid with a document. The other one got into a Crown file by the ordinary lawful way, because a Crown office makes a list and sends a man down a road with it and the man does not ask anybody anything and the list fills in anyway.

**The names are not the point.**

That was the sentence, and it came to him at about the tenth hour standing in a yard past a county stone, and it took him nine minutes to write it out and he wrote it eleven times:

*A household which has ever bought a thing is a household an office can find. That is not a thief and that is not a conspiracy. A trade already keeps, for its own reasons, and in its own hand, and without being asked, the exact three things an office needs: who lives where, who buys, and who signed. The Crown's roll is not built out of stolen paper. It is built out of the ordinary paper a trade keeps, and the Crown is not stealing anything, and the shop does not know it is a list, and the clerk on the road did not have to ask anybody anything, and the whole of the apparatus works because two entirely honest trades keep the same three facts in the same two kinds of hand.*

*And that is worse. That is much worse. A room with a plan in it has a door in it and a door can be watched and a door can be shut and a man in a room can be refused. A column and a stamp have no door. A column and a stamp only need somebody to be competent.*

---

He said it out loud in a doorway at about the fourth hour of the afternoon, to nobody, because he had been alone for nine minutes and because he had spent a month learning that a thing said in a lane is a record.

“This is a worse thing than a villain,” Tomas Vale said.

He heard himself say it and then heard how it sounded, standing in a doorway past a county stone with a barrow behind him.

“No,” he said. “No, that is not a thing to say. That is a man in a doorway being dramatic about a column, and a man being dramatic about a column is the first half of how a column works, because a column works on people who are frightened of it and a frightened man builds a story in about an hour and the story is always better than the thing and there is always somebody in it who deserves it.”

Tamsin Rook, who had been about thirty feet away at the top of a gate with her back to them both, came down off the gate and picked up her bag.

“I have been standing there for nine minutes,” she said, “because you were in a doorway with a thing in your face and I have read a hundred shoulders in eleven years and I could not tell whether you were going to throw something or write something.”

“I was going to write something.”

“You were going to do the other thing first,” Tamsin Rook said. “You were standing in a doorway telling a village that a shopkeeper's book is worse than a man, and if anybody had heard you they would have gone and told that shopkeeper, and he does not need a Crown office to be frightening, he has been frightened once already by a woman who did not raise her voice, and he is a good man, and you would have made him into something for about a year and he would never have been able to unhear it.”

“I know.”

“You do not know, because you have spent a month finding out that you are right,” Tamsin Rook said, “and being right about a column is the best feeling you have had since you left Kells and I have watched you have it twice now and I am telling you what it looks like from outside and you can do as you like with it.”

“All right,” Tomas Vale said. “I am not going to be dramatic about it in a lane.”

“No. You are going to be dramatic about it in a book, which is the correct place, and I want to read it, and I am not going to ask you to.”

---

He wrote the method on a page at about the sixth hour of the evening at an inn he had not booked into and had not paid for and was sitting in because it was dry, and he wrote it in the flat hand and it is in his book entire and Tamsin Rook read it over his shoulder without saying a word, and it took her about nine minutes, and he did not help her with it, and at the end she put her hand flat on the table about a foot from the page and said one word, which was *yes*, and he knew that meant the whole of it and he asked his question and it was the only question he asked her that evening and it was not about the method.

“If a trade's paper and an office's paper are the same paper,” he said, “what is the one question a person can put to a clerk that the clerk has to answer in his own name?”

She did not answer for about nine seconds, and he could see her working, and it was not a slow answer, it was a careful one, and she did it the way she reads a shoulder: from the outside in, and then in, and then from the outside again.

“You have been asking offices who is on a list,” Tamsin Rook said. “That is why you have nothing. Everybody asks who. Nobody answers who, because a name is a thing you can be refused, and being refused a name looks like the office protecting somebody, and in a year you will have written a page about a district that will not say a name, and that page will be true, and it will be useless, and you will have spent a year of your life on it.”

“Then what.”

“Ask them what the document is,” Tamsin Rook said. “Not who is on it. What it is. Go in and say: *is the thing you keep a record of what I said, or is it a record of what somebody else said about what I said?* And then — and this is the part you will want to write down and you may not write it down, so do it now — and then make him put the answer in a document with his name at the bottom of it. Not a return. Not an answer given to you. A separate thing, in his own hand, saying what the thing he keeps is. Because a thing a clerk holds is not a fact about anything, it is a thing a clerk holds, and it is as safe as the box it is in, and a clerk can hold a piece of paper for thirty years without ever once being asked what it is. But a clerk who has written down what it is, in his own name, with a date, has said a thing, and a thing somebody has said can be looked at by the next man, and by the man after him, and by the person you hand it to, and you cannot take it back and neither can he.”

Tomas Vale looked at her for about four seconds.

“That is better than anything I have had in a month,” he said.

“It is not better than anything,” Tamsin Rook said. “It is the method. You have had it since the ninth day of the month, in a market court in Kells, from a girl of sixteen, and I have watched you spend a month unable to use it because you have been trying to make people answer for themselves instead of making a document say what it is. You have been doing it the way a bellmaker does it. You have been trying to get at the person. **There is no person. There is a column.**”

He wrote it out in the book that night, and it is in his book in her words and not improved, and it took about nine lines, and under it he wrote the two lines that he was not going to be able to read at without feeling sick, and they are the reason this chapter is the middle of this batch and the worst afternoon Tomas Vale has had since he left a town with a wax on its door:

*And the answer I have been carrying out of Kells, in my own hand, in my own book, is the same question in a different coat. Who taught a Crown office to turn one clerk's answer into a whole settlement's consent — and where are the names being sent when they are registered. I have been asking it as though there is a person in a room who taught it. There is not a person in a room who taught it. There is a form, and a small print, and a ninth column, and a list of streets on the back of a cart, and a shopkeeper who writes down where his iron goes, and a clause at the foot of a Crown form that says a statement returned before the closing hour will be certified, and a household of six in a lane who posted six answers back without reading one of them.*

*Nobody taught it. That is the horror and it is the only new thing I have learned since the low gate on the morning of the thirty-third. It did not have to be taught. It only had to be convenient, and then it had to be printed, and then it had to be stamped.*

*And I want it written that I came four days looking for a man in a room with a plan in it and I have found a column and a stamp and a habit, and a habit is worse, because a man can be refused and a habit cannot, and a man can be watched and a habit is watched by nobody, because everybody is inside it.*

---

He wrote one more thing that night, and it is the last line in the book for the fortieth and it is about a receipt and not about a column, and it is there because he is a man who learns about a thing on the day before it happens to him, about four years too late and once:

*And now the other one, which I put here so that I cannot pretend tomorrow that I did not know.*

*The third clause of my own receipt says the object in this barrow is produced at the request of the district, or of the watch, or of the market court, and that a request may be made by any person and not only by an officer. I asked for that clause. I asked for it in the watch house at about the fifth hour of the morning of the thirty-third, in front of a copyist, and I asked for it because I was right: an office that may take a thing on its own paper is an office that may take a bell off a man because an office has decided a bell is safer in a building. And I asked for the fifth clause as well, and the fifth clause is the better one and the fifth clause is the reason nobody can take anything out of this frame.*

*And the third clause is a door with no lock on it, and I hung it, and I hung it in about eleven minutes, and I have had seven days to think about what a door with no lock on it is for and in seven days I have not once written down the answer.*

*It is for a person with a pen and an hour. That is what it is for. And there is no answer in it, because it is not a door, it is a hook, and the clause does not say what happens when a person who cannot give a name comes to the hook at two in the morning in a yard with three other carts in it. It says a request may be made by any person. It does not say a person may be asked who he is. I wrote that clause and I did not write the second one, and if I had written the second one I should have been refused by a watch captain at five in the morning, and I would have been right to be.*

*So it stands, and it will be used, and I am writing down the night before that it will be used, in my own hand, in a book nobody has to read, so that on the morning after I cannot stand up in a yard and say a man told them it would happen.*

He shut the book. The frame was in the cradle on the barrow outside and the four bolts were made fast and nothing was rung, and the county stone was behind them, and Fourmile was three days on and had a real building in it with a real address and a real door and a man in it with a name, and Tomas Vale had one question and no bell and no metal and no authority whatsoever, and it was the best afternoon he had had since Kells and he wrote down both of those things on the same page and did not improve either.
