# Chapter 0072 — The Column Can Be Certified And The Column Cannot Be Certified

The man with the handcart wanted two shillings and sixpence and the man whose yard it was wanted a shilling, and both of them wanted the money first, and neither of them wanted a name, and Tomas Vale paid both at about the sixth hour of the morning of the fifty-eighth with a shilling and sixpence left in his pockets and a column of his own in the day-book with the hour against it and the two of their names in it.

He had told her on a quay the evening before and she had not stopped him and she had not helped him, and the whole of what she had said about it was that a man who has been free for five weeks is going to be very good at paying and is not going to notice that he has changed, and she was right about that on a quay and about nine hours before he had watched himself do it.

The frame went into a corner of a yard behind a draper's shop on the far side of the town from the water, and it did not leave the cradle, and the four bolts were made fast where it was set down, and it was still bolted at about the ninth hour of the evening, and nothing was rung, and the receipt was whole.

The crate went to a smithy in the same street because there is a smithy in every town of two thousand and because a crate of a smith's goods put down in a smith's yard is a thing nobody in a smith's yard has ever asked a question about in the history of smiths.

---

The clerk's hall is the biggest building in Miremarket that is not a church, and it is not by a long way, and he found it at about the seventh hour of the morning by standing in the street outside a yard and asking a carter what it was, and the carter told him without being asked and without stopping work, which was the first of about nine things in this town that happened to him.

“That’s the hall. That’s where the paper is.”

“What paper.”

“All of it. **The round comes and the paper goes back and comes again, and the hall keeps it, and that’s the whole of what a hall is.**” He did not look up. “You’re not from here.”

“No.”

“Thought. Everyone from here knows what a hall is and everyone from four miles off asks me that, and it’s the same question every spring with the tax men.” He put a strap over his shoulder. “**The round’s the thing you want. It comes twice and I’ve had it in my yard twice this year, and the money comes with it, and the money’s the reason nobody here is unhappy, and you can put that down as a fact about this town and it’s true.**”

It was the first sentence in nine days that a stranger in a lane had said to him that he had not asked for, and it was not a fact about a document, and he wrote it down at the top of a fresh page and underlined it, and the underlining is in the book and he is not sure why he did it.

---

He stood outside the front of it for about forty minutes and then went in, because he is a man who goes in.

The man at the counter was not a counter clerk. He was a man with grey in his hair at the sides and a key on a string and a room behind him with a door that was open about four inches, and he came out of the four inches when Tomas Vale was about nine steps inside, which is further in than a man is who is asking a favour, and he read the barrow behind him in the yard through the open door, and the frame in its cradle with the four bolts in it, and then he read the man.

“Ivo Trant,” he said. “I am in charge of the shelves.”

“I have a load in the yard.”

“Then you have come to the wrong building and I am going to tell you so before you have said another word, and you will not be charged anything for being told by me at about the seventh hour of the morning.” He said it exactly the way a man reads a form out. “**There is nothing in this kingdom by which a man who is not a district officer and holds no office may ask a clerk's hall for anything at all.** Not a copy, not a page, not an entry, not a sight of a shelf, and not a conversation about a column. If a person wants to make an application there is a form, and I will give you the form, and the form is a Crown form, and I am telling you before you fill it in that there is no box on it for a reason, because every person who has ever filled one in has asked me for a reason afterwards and I have not got one.”

“I did not come to make an application.”

“Everybody who comes in here has come to make an application and about one in nine does not know it yet,” said Ivo Trant, and he gave him the form, and the form was a single sheet with a district's number at the head and a box for the person and a box for the parish and a box for the hour and a box for what the office is to do, and no more boxes than that, and Tomas Vale looked at it for a while and thought about a counter at Fourmile and a counter at Helling and a man there who had told him the same thing in the same words nearly ten days ago, and said:

“Then the answer is no, and it is the same answer as at Fourmile, and the officer there told me to my face that a person who puts a demand gets a worse answer than a person who puts a question.”

“That is very nearly what I have just said,” said Ivo Trant, “and I am going to say the last of it, and then I am going to ask you to leave, and I want it in the room that I am not enjoying this and I am not enjoying it because of anything you have done.”

He read the rest of the form without appearing to read it and then put it down on the counter square.

“**A clerk's hall is not a district office. We hold the paper. We do not decide anything and we cannot be asked to, and there is no form in this kingdom that would let a hall decide anything, and if there were I would resign into it inside a year.** You have a receipt in your coat that says you have no standing in any ward in this kingdom, and I am not asking to see it and I would not read it if you offered it, and I am telling you that I know the words because I have read them about nine times in other people's letters, and every man who writes one of them to a hall is asking a question about a bell, and I have never had an answer to one and I am not going to start with you.”

And then he said the thing that is the reason the whole of this chapter is in a book and not in a mouth, and he said it flatly, the way a man says the price of a thing.

“**You may look at the town. You may ask anybody in this town anything you like, at any hour of any day, and every person in it will answer you, and I have been in this town twenty-two years and I will tell you the whole of what that is worth: a great deal, and nothing.** A clerk's hall is the one building in a town of two thousand that a man may not walk into. Everything else — the smithy, the market, the yard, the door, the taproom, the lane — all of it is open to you and all of it is full of people who have nothing to be frightened of you about, because you are not the district and you are not the watch and you are not the market court, and you are a man with a barrow and a frame in a yard, and in about four days you will be a man with a barrow and a frame in a yard, and there is nothing in this kingdom that makes a man interesting on the fourth day.”

Tomas Vale took the form and looked at it and gave it back.

“I am not going to fill that in,” he said.

“Most people do.”

“I am not most people and I am not going to fill it in, and I will tell you why, and it is not defiance and I am not going to tell you it is skill. **I have filled in two of these and I have a carbon of one of them and I have not used it, and there is a district officer at Fourmile who explained to me that a box for what the office has done is the best thing on the form, and a man at Helling who told me there is no box for a reason and has never had one.** If I fill that in I am asking a clerk's hall to do a thing, and a clerk's hall cannot do a thing, and I have been in four buildings this month and the answer in every one of them has been a shelf, and I have stopped believing that asking anybody anything is a thing that produces a change.”

Ivo Trant looked at him for a second longer than a man needs to.

“That is the first sentence anybody has said to me at that counter in about four years,” he said, “and it is not a complaint, and I am not going to do anything with it, and I would like you to understand that I have about nine hundred lines going into that hall from this town this year and that **not one of them is a complaint.**”

---

He went out at about the fourth hour of the afternoon and did the thing he has done for five weeks, and it worked, and it got him nothing, and that was the first time.

He did it nine times between the fourth hour and the sixth hour. He did it at the market cross, where a market was not up and about nine stalls were, and at the smithy, and at a well, and at a door, and in a lane, and at the stone landing, and to a man mending a net, and to a woman going home with a basket, and to a boy who was about nine and who told him twice that his mother was not at home and then, when he had said the second time what his mother would have said, that she was at home and would not come out.

The method is this and it is five weeks old and it has never once failed: **you do not ask a building whether it is a building. You ask a person what the thing in front of them is, in words that do not tell them what you want, and then you shut up.**

The question was the same all nine times. He had got it down to about eleven words and he had said it nine times in four counties and he had never improved it.

“What is the roll in that hall. **Is it a record of what the households of this district said, or is it a record of what somebody in that building said about what the households of this district said.**”

Nine answers. Two of them were rude. Four of them were the answer you get when you ask a person in a market town what their own business is, which is a question with a smile on it. And **all nine of them were the same, and all nine of them were correct, and not one of them knew that there was a second half to the question.**

“Aye,” said the man with the net. “The rolls.”

And he said it the way a man says *the post* or *the market* or *the tax*, and he went on mending, and it was on the page and it was the same page, and Tomas Vale stood in a lane in a town of two thousand people and had a name for the book, off a man with a net, at the fourth hour of the afternoon, for nothing, and it worked, and he felt nothing at all.

---

The one that was different was at about the sixth hour, outside the front of the hall, where a young man came out with a book under his arm and stood in the street to open it in the light.

He was not dressed like a district either and he was not dressed like anything in particular, and Tomas Vale put the question to him because it goes on a man with a book the way a hand goes to a banister, and the young man answered it in about four sentences and then kept talking, which is the whole of what had happened to him for five weeks and had stopped happening at about the fourth hour of the afternoon on the fifty-eighth.

“That is a filthy way to put it,” said the young man. “That is not what it is at all. **We call them the answer rolls. That is the clerk's word for them and it has been the word for about forty years and there is nothing grand about it and anybody in this town will tell you the same and half of them will tell you it stands for something else because they would rather it did.**”

“Do you know what it stands for.”

“No. **It stands for the rolls where the answers go.** It is a book of answers. That is the whole of it and it is a shelf with a number on the spine and about eleven thousand of them in the hall behind me, and they are in here because this is where the paper is, and there is no other reason on earth.”

“How many answers.”

“**About eleven thousand a year, come back marked as a household that cannot make up its mind.**” He said it without any weight at all, the way a man says a figure he says every day. “We do not leave the line open. Nobody here leaves a line open, and I will tell you the reason and then I am going to go and eat, because it is a quarter past and I have been in this building since the fourth hour. **The office records one hand against the line and the line is answered.** The book that records it is the roll, and the roll is a book of answers, and the schedule goes on to the next house, and by the fourth hour of the afternoon the round's cart is on its way back and it has to be on its way back.”

“And whose hand.”

“That is the whole of the trade and I will not be asked it by a man at a door,” said the young man, and then, immediately, and in a completely different voice, as though he had caught himself: “**That is not a refusal. That is me being pleased with myself. Ask me the rest of it properly and I will answer it, and I have answered four people this month and two of them were from offices.**”

“Ask you what.”

“What is the column for,” said the young man. “**You can certify anything inside a column. You cannot certify the column.** And nobody has ever asked to, in the whole time this hall has been standing, and I have looked, because I am the kind of clerk who looks. And the reason nobody can is not secrecy. It is that **a column is not a household and a household is not a person, and a person is the only thing in this kingdom that can be asked a question and can refuse to answer it.** Everything else in here is paper. A person is not paper and there is no box for one, and that is not a design. That is just what is true.”

He shut the book under his arm.

“**Bryn Pethick,”** he said. “I am a clerk. I am not an officer and I hold no office and you may put that in whatever you are writing, and I have been in this hall about four years and I like it, and I am going to eat.**

“And I am not frightened of you and there is nothing to be frightened of. **The round came in this town in spring and went out in about nine days and the money came with it and about nine houses in this town that I know of had a bad winter got through it, and the clerk's hall had nothing to do with any of that and holds a shelf.**”

He went off up the street eating something out of a paper and did not turn round, and Tomas Vale stood in a lane and had the name of the building, the name of the book, and the number in it, and did not have a document, a copy, a certificate, a household, or a single thing he could act on before a meal.

---

What he wrote at about the eighth hour of the evening, in the flat hand, in four columns, is in the book entire, and the second column is the longest thing he has written in a month, and it is a list of what he asked and what he got, and there are nine lines in it and the nine lines are the same nine lines.

> *The fifty-eighth. I have the name of the town off a book on a slip, and the name of the building off a man with a net, and the name of the book off a clerk who was going to his dinner, and the number off the same clerk, and **not one of those four things cost me a penny and I have got nothing.**
>
> ***MIREMARKET. THE CLERK'S HALL. THE ANSWER ROLLS. ABOUT ELEVEN THOUSAND A YEAR.** That is a clerk's word for a set of shelves. It is a shelf with a number on the spine and it is a book of answers and it is in that building because that is where the paper is, and a clerk told me the word in a street at about the sixth hour of the evening in the tone of a man telling me where the post goes, and I want it written that **anybody in that town will give me those four facts and about nine of them have, and not one of them knows that any of it is a thing that can be asked about**, and the reason is that in this town those are not facts about a thing anybody is doing. They are facts about a market day.
>
> *I have been five weeks asking rooms what a document is and I have never once been refused the question. I have been refused the *answer* four times in four buildings and I have worked on every one of them and two of them gave me everything. **Today I asked a man with a net at the fourth hour of the afternoon and a man on a landing at the sixth and eight more people besides, and every one of them answered, and I could not use one of the answers, and I have been in a town of two thousand people all day and every one of them talked to me and I have got nothing, and I cannot work out whether that is because the method is wrong or because I am asking the wrong people, and I have not got a third thing to put in front of it, and I am not going to guess at the third thing tonight because I have been wrong at guessing in this book four times and every one of the four was a guess.**
>
> *And the man in charge of the shelves was right about all of it and pleasant about it and it took him about nine minutes, and he told me a clerk's hall is the one building in a town of two thousand a man may not walk into and that everything else in the town is open to me and full of people with nothing to be frightened of me about, and he was right, and he was right about the clerk's hall as well, and **a man who is not hiding from you is not a door, and I have had that in a book since the fifty-fifth and I have now had it twice and it has not got any easier.**
>
> *And the young clerk, at the sixth hour, in a street, eating, said the thing that is the whole of this day and I am going to write it out in his words and not mine, because I asked him and he answered and that is the first time in a month that has happened without a room in it.*
>
> ***You can certify anything inside a column. You cannot certify the column. And nobody has ever asked to, and the reason is not secrecy, it is that a column is not a household and a household is not a person, and a person is the only thing in this kingdom that can be asked a question and can refuse to answer it. Everything else in here is paper. A person is not paper and there is no box for one, and that is not a design. That is just what is true.***
>
> *I have been carrying that sentence since the thirty-eighth. A woman on a road at the sixth hour of the fortieth day of this month gave it to me and I have put it in four buildings and it has been the wrong sentence in every one of them, and I want that in the book, and I do not know yet what it is that it is the wrong sentence about, and I have four days of paper in this coat that says the same sentence in four hands in three counties and I have had it for a month and I have not been able to put it down for anybody.*
>
> ***AND THE THING I AM WRITING IN THE FOURTH COLUMN, WHICH IS THE COLUMN FOR A THING NOBODY COULD ANSWER, AND WHICH I DO NOT MERGE:***
>
> *A clerk in a street told me in about four minutes that about eleven thousand households a year in this district cannot make up their minds, and that the office does not wait for them, and that it records one hand and moves on, and he said the word **households** and he said it about eleven thousand times a year and meant a building and the people in it and did not mean either. He is not a villain. He has been in the building four years and he likes it and he is not frightened of me and he was at his dinner at a quarter past four. He does not know what he has said and there was nothing in it he could have said differently and he would say it the same way tomorrow.*
>
> *I have been in four counties looking for a man who is lying to me about a column. **There is not one in this town and I walked about nine hundred yards of it this morning and there was not one in it.***

---

He had no name, no household and no standing, and it was at about the ninth hour of the evening that he did the only thing that worked.

He went down a lane at the east end of the square, where there were houses with a bit of land and not much water, and he stopped at a gate and asked the question he had asked nine times, and the woman at the gate had it open and did not shut it.

“That is the roll in that hall,” she said, before he had got to the end of it. “No idea. Ask the Marrows.”

“The Marrows.”

“They've got a book,” she said. “**Their Bess keeps a book. She writes down what her own house said and what her own house would not say and she has had it about nine years and she has never taken it anywhere and there is nobody in this town has ever asked her for it.** I have been in this town about eleven years and I am telling you the truth and it is that everybody in this street knows there is a book at the Marrows' and not one person knows what is in it.”

“Whereabouts at the end of this lane.”

“Far end. Under the gable with the lean-to. You will not get her in at this hour, she is in till dark, and **I am telling you that before you walk down there, because a man with a barrow in a lane asking about a book is a thing about a rate, and it is not a thing about a book.**”

“Thank you.”

“No,” said the woman. “You are welcome and there is nothing to thank me for, and I have not told you the woman's name and I am not going to, and I do not know whether she has a husband or a mother or five children and I have not asked, and I have told you exactly what I know, which is a book.”

He walked to the end of the lane anyway, because it is the second day and he has nine, and a gable with a lean-to on it at the end of a lane is a house, and there was light in the bottom window and nobody at the door, and he did not knock, and he stood in the road for about four minutes and then went back up it to the yard behind the draper's shop and bolted the frame where it stood and wrote the name of the street and the gable and the lean-to in the fourth column with the hour against it, and then wrote nine more lines under it and did not improve one of them.

> *Under the gable with the lean-to, at the end of the east lane. A book in that house kept by a woman called **Bess Marrow**, about nine years, and nobody in eleven years has asked her for it, and every person in that street knows there is a book there and not one of them knows what is in it.*
>
> *That is not a household. That is a **lead**, and it is the first one in nine days, and I have had four of them in five weeks and three of them were buildings.*
