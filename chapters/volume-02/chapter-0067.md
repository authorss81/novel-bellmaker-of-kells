# Chapter 0067 — A Bell Somebody Else Made, And A Bell He Rang

He was awake at about the fifth hour of the morning of the fifty-second because the yard of a smith's shop is not a place a man sleeps well in, and because he had decided to do a thing before he could talk himself out of it, and because a decision made in the dark is very much easier than a decision made in daylight with somebody watching.

He sat on the end of a bench in the corner with the door open four inches and wrote it in his day-book on the page he keeps for things that are not about metal.

It took him about forty minutes and he tore the leaf out, which he has not done for a page before, and folded it once, because it was not a page for the book and he was not going to have it in the book.

> *Ocklebridge. The ninth day from the thirty-fifth was the forty-fourth. I did not come on the forty-fourth.*
>
> *You gave me nine days and I have had seven of them on the road since and I did not write, and I want you to have the reason in your own hand and not mine guessed at. I did not write because the term was yours and not mine, and a term that runs out has run out, and I was not going to have it back by asking for it late. That is mine and not yours. You did not change your mind. You have not sent to me. Nobody has written to you on this that I know of and if somebody has, it is not me.*
>
> *So I am not going to ask you for the name. I am going to ask you the other thing, and the other thing is the only thing there is, and it is one question and it is a short one:*
>
> *Does the nine days still stand.*
>
> *I am asking because the answer may be no, and if the answer is no then it is no and I will not come again, and I will not write again, and there will be no second asking, and you will not have to say no twice and you will not have to explain it to anybody, including me, and that is the whole of what I want and it is a smaller thing than you have been owed.*
>
> *I will tell you two things about myself in case they are any use to you and in case they are not. I have been four days in a town called Bramden with a bell somebody else made that a man has certified, and it has no claim in it, and I have not yet rung it in a public place and I have told nobody in that town what I intend to do with it. And the other is that I did not write on the forty-fifth either, and I could have, and the reason I did not is that on the forty-fifth I found out I was going to want something from you, and a man who wants something should not be the one to reopen it.*
>
> *I have written the hour at the top because you would. It is the fifth hour of the morning of the fifty-second and I am in a smith's yard four miles past a county stone and there is a woman asleep in a room over a shop in this town who is not going to be told about this and has not been asked.*
>
> *Tomas Vale, of Kells, without district. Bellmaker.*

He read it four times and did not improve any of it, which is the whole of what he is able to do on paper, and then he put it in his coat and went out to where the anvil bench was, and the barrow was in the yard with the cradle on the boards and the four bolts made fast where it was set down, and the sky over the east country was the colour it gets about a half hour before it decides.

At about the seventh hour Amos Trewin came in with his coat off and looked at him and did not say good morning, because a man who says good morning at seven in a smithy is asking for a conversation.

“The book is out,” Trewin said, “because the ninth column is out, and I am going to ask you to do the thing I asked the other two to do, which is to read it standing up, and to keep your hands behind your back while you do it, and not to touch the page. I have a man coming at eight who has never signed anything in his life and I want his eye to go to the sixth column first and I do not want the whole book disturbed.”

“That is fair.”

“It is not fair, it is a shop.” He put the book down on the bench and opened it and turned it round. “Now. You have come to buy a bell and you have not asked me for one and you have not asked me where my metal came from, and I have been waiting since the seventh hour, and here it is. **Ninth column. The place the metal came out of, the day I had it, and the man I had it from.**”

The bell came out of a crate under the counter that had a board across the top of it and a lock that was not locked, and the frame was not in it and the bell was, and it was wrapped in a sacking cloth that had been tied and untied and tied again about forty times.

“Fourteen pounds two ounces,” said Amos Trewin. “It came out of Roald Byre's hearth at about eleven miles in the spring, and I had the metal in the winter and he had it cast in the spring because that is when his fire is lit, and he lights once a year and shuts down when it is done, and he is not a man with nine fires in him like a caster at a market town. And it has stood in that crate since the spring and it is not for sale and there is nothing wrong with it.”

“What is wrong with it.”

“Nothing is wrong with it. It is the best bell I have had in this shop in about eleven years and I have had four in thirty.” Trewin unwrapped the sacking off it. “**That is the thing you have not understood about a bell. Nothing is ever wrong with it. A bell is a lump of bronze with a note in it and the note is either right or it is not, and if it is not then it is a lump of bronze, and if it is then it is a bell, and the difference is four days with a leather and an ear and nobody watching.** Every bell anybody has ever thrown away was a lump of bronze with a note in it that somebody did not like.”

He set it on the bench on a leather.

The metal was a pale, close yellow, gone darker in the hollow of the soundbow, and there was nothing else on it. That was the first thing Tomas Vale noticed and it took him about four seconds to work out why it felt wrong, and then he looked at the crown and it was right. No wax in it. No dressed groove cut round the waist of it. The lip was clean and the shoulder was clean and the whole of the outside of it was doing one thing, which is carrying a note, and not doing three things, which is carrying a note and carrying a claim and carrying a distance somebody had paid for.

“You have put nothing in it,” he said.

“I have put nothing in it because I bought it out of a stock of that,” said Trewin. “**A carrier is a bell with nothing in it. No wax in the crown and no groove cut round it and nothing on it but the mark and the day. That is not a defect. That is what a carrier is for.**”

He turned it over with one finger on the shoulder and showed him the crown.

“There. Mark. Punch of Roald Byre, and every master in the company knows that punch and I could not counterfeit it in a year. And there is the day, struck in under the shoulder where you cannot dress it off, and it is a day and a year and no month, because the year of a casting is the year and the man who cut it has never cut a month into a bell in his life and I have asked him why and he told me it takes up too much room.”

The word came out of him and he heard it, and he put his thumb on the bell's shoulder for a second as though it were nothing.

“It is a day and a year,” said Trewin, “and a bell carries about forty of them and that is the whole of a date. Now. **You are going to want to know what the fourteenth day of the spring of this year has to do with where the metal came from, and it has everything to do with it, and here is how, and it is the only thing I know that nine men in my own trade do not.**

“A mark in a crown tells you who cast it. It does not tell you one single thing about the metal, because a caster is an honest man who will put a punch in bronze that came out of a gate he did not look at. A mark gets you a man. **A date gets you the year, and a year gets you the caster's own book, and the caster's book has the where-from in it, and the caster's supplier's book has it underneath that.** So the mark and the day are the two ends of a chain, and the chain is the metal, and the chain goes back from this bench to Byre's hearth to a Crown store with a Crown document against it with a day on the document, and that is where it stops because that is where a Crown store stops, and a Crown store keeps no book of where *it* got it and never has and is not obliged to.”

He turned the book round again and put his finger on the ninth column and ran it up.

“There. My page. The metal out of a Crown store, the day I had it, the man I had it from. That is a book with my name at the top of it and nineteen years in it, and I have never sold a page of it and I am not going to, and I am going to let you read the column and not the book. And then Roald Byre has a book with the same column in it, and he will not sell me a page of that either, and I have never asked him to, and he will read it to me if I stand in his shop and put my hands behind my back. And that is where the chain comes from, and there is nobody in this kingdom who can break it, and it is not law, and it is not a registry, and it is nine men writing in nine books what a man told them on a day.”

Tomas Vale read the ninth column with his hands behind his back and the bell standing on the leather beside the open book, and the column went back, page by page, past the winter, past two Crown stores, to a place called Ardwick that he had never heard of, with a day against it in a hand that was not Trewin's and was nineteen years older than his own.

“Now the money,” said Trewin.

“How much.”

“**A pound and fourteen shillings.** The metal in it is three shillings and fourpence at my own price out and I will write that on the board for anybody who asks me, because the difference between a smith who can tell you where his metal came from and one who cannot is not the metal and it is worth about eleven shillings on every hundredweight a man brings me, and the whole of the trade is that eleven, and I have said so for thirty years. The rest is the making. Roald Byre lights his fire once a year and casts four days in it, and every one of those days is a day he cannot be doing anything else, and a bell of fourteen pounds two ounces is about two days of his, and the winter I had the metal standing in my yard is a year of my rent, and there is the striker and there is the tuner and the tuner is his brother and his brother does nothing else all year. And the crate. And the fact that it has stood in my cellar since the spring and nobody could buy it because of the season and the season has now turned. **That is a pound and fourteen shillings and I am not going to be talked under it and if you tell anybody in this county I sold it for eleven pounds I will put it about that you are the man who came over the stone with a barrow and bought it cheap, and I will say it in that exact form, and there is nobody in this county who will be able to correct me.**”

“No you will not. You will put it about that I am a fool for selling it at that, which is what you would say if you were me, and you may say it, and it will be true, and it will not be worth anything to you.”

“And there is the man who has been in my shop twice wanting a page of me and did not get it, and if he tells anybody what I sold him I will tell them what he asked me for and did not get, and that is the truth on both sides and it is what this trade is.” He put the chalk down. “**Ask me the next question. I have not shut for the day and the next question is the one I can answer properly and it is the one you have not asked.**”

“No. I have not shut for the day. But I am not going to be thanked by you in a room for a month, and there is a woman in this shop who has just been paid for a bell by somebody who is not you, and you can see the last of that, so you can do the arithmetic.”

---

Tomas Vale had the receipt in his coat and the frame in the yard and a barrow and a day-book and about fourpence, and he had said as much in a room in Helling a week earlier, in front of people, and none of it had changed.

“I can give you the barrow,” he said.

Amos Trewin put the leather down off the bell and looked at him.

“The barrow is worth eleven pounds and four and I would take it and I have wanted a barrow since the spring and the axle on that one is a good one,” he said. “And you cannot sell it to me, and I am going to tell you why in about nine words so that you do not stand there for a minute making a bargain you cannot keep, and it is not a kindness. **The cradle is on that barrow's bed.** The padded cradle is bolted to the bed of that barrow and the four bolts hold it there, and the receipt says the frame does not leave the cradle for the carriage, and the cradle rides on that bed, and the bolts are made fast at every place the frame is set down, and if you sell me the barrow tonight then at some point in the next four days there is a Crown object in a padded cradle with nowhere to be set down, and it will have to come out of the cradle, or it will have to stay on a road, and both of those are the end of your receipt and neither of them is me taking your barrow. **You have written a paper that will not let you pay for a bell.**”

“Does it say that.”

“It says it in four places and none of them is about money and I read it again on the day you came over the stone and I thought at the time, and I have not said so, and I am saying so now because you are about to offer me something else and you should know what I am looking at.” He wiped his hands. “Go on. Offer me the other thing.”

Which was the only thing left in him, and he had been carrying it since the fortieth, and it was not money.

“Once a year,” he said, “for as long as I have the bell, I will write you a page. Where it is. What it has been rung for, and by whom, and at what hour, and what anybody said it was for, in their words and not mine. In my own hand with the hour on it, and I will send it in the ordinary post and you will not pay for it, and if I stop you will know that I have stopped because the page will stop.”

Amos Trewin did not answer for long enough that the shop got quiet again.

“No,” he said.

“You have not said why.”

“You have not listened to what you just offered me, so I am going to do it in a short sentence and then I am going to do it in a long one and you are only entitled to the short one, and you may have the long one as well if you are standing still.

“**Short: I have been in this trade thirty years and I have never once believed a page from a stranger.**” He held up a hand before the objection arrived.“That is not the reason. That is the door. The reason is this.

“You have offered me a man who writes to his smith every year about a bell. **And a man who writes to his smith every year about a bell is a man whose bell has a keeper.** A keeper is a person with a duty, and a duty is a mouth, and a mouth is for a claim. You have offered me, in one page a year, the exact thing that makes a bell a mouth instead of a lump of bronze with a note in it, and you did it in the open, in my shop, at about eight in the morning, as though it were a courtesy. And I have just spent a morning telling you that the whole of what is good about that bell on my bench is that **there is nobody in it.** No wax. No groove. No claim. No keeper. No name but the caster's punch and the day. A thing that says only what the man swinging it says. And you have offered to write me an annual account of the man who swings it, and in nine years I would have a book in this shop with a column in it headed *what it was for*, and a man in nine years would be able to come in here and read that column, and he would know things about a bell that I do not know, and **they would all be true, and that is the worst of it.**”

He sat down on the bench, which he had not done.

“I would be sat in this shop for nine years holding a book that makes a plain carrier into a kept bell, and I have spent nineteen years keeping a book that says where the metal came from, and the whole of the difference between those two books is that one of them can be read by a person who is not the trade and the other one can only be read by a smith with a hand on it.” He looked at the bell. “You may think that is precious. It is not precious. It is that I sell bells. **The only thing I have ever wanted a man in my shop to have is a bell with nothing in it and no one asking me about it.**”

“All right,” said Tomas Vale. “That is refused and I will not make it again, and I am not going to argue it, and I want it recorded that I was refused and why, and you may write it on the board if you like, and you may read the sentence out to the next man who comes in here and offers you the same thing in a worse way.”

“I will not write it on the board. **I will remember it, and I have got a better memory for a thing like that than any book, and in about nine years somebody will offer it to me again and I will not need the nine years.**”

---

And then the money came, and it came from the wrong side of the room for everybody in it.

Tamsin Rook had been in the shop since about the seventh hour and had not been in the front of it once, and she had been sitting on the bench at the back with her bag between her feet, and she put her hand in the bag and brought out a purse that had been flat for about eleven years and came out square.

She counted it on the anvil bench beside the bell without hurrying and without any particular expression, and the shop stopped, and Tomas Vale put his hand flat on the bench.

“No,” he said.

“**You do not get to say that.**”

“I do not get to say what to you about money. I know. Listen to me for one sentence, because you have earned the right to put it down without listening to it, and then I will not say another word about it for the rest of the day.”

She waited with her hand in the purse.

“You put that down and there is not one person in this room who can be told why, and if I say no in front of six of them then it is a thing that happened to you and not a thing you did, and I will not be the man who makes you small in a shop, and I will not be able to walk into a market square after it.”

“All right,” said Tamsin Rook, and she counted out one pound fourteen shillings on the anvil bench in a column and did not touch the coins twice, and that was the first thing Tomas Vale had watched her do that he had a use for, and he wrote it down that night and wrote down only that, and then she put the purse back in her bag, and the bag lay flat at the front of it and square at the back, and he did not ask and she did not say and there was no way on that bench to tell.

“That is a pound and fourteen exactly,” said Amos Trewin, “and I have got no change and I am not going to be troubled about that for a couple of shillings in my life.” He did not take the money. He looked at it on the bench for about a second and then he said, to her, and not to Tomas Vale, and it is the only thing he ever said to her in that shop:

“Where did you get it.”

“Nine coppers a month off the towpath for about four years, and a carrier's day-book for eleven years that has my name in it as a place a thing was left, and about eleven parcel fees from men who wanted a thing taken to a door and did not want to say what for, and a good deal of tea. **I have never bought anything in eleven years.** I have had a bag and a bell and the bell is in the bag and I have not spent a penny on the bag and I have not spent a penny on anything, and the fee for carrying is not a rich man's money and I have not wanted it for anything, and a person can only find out what she is saving for when somebody finally needs something, and there has not been a somebody in eleven years, and there has not been one in this room until this week.”

Nobody looked at the bell. That was the thing Tomas Vale noticed and wrote down later and did not improve: nobody in that shop looked at the bell, and a man put a pound and fourteen shillings on an anvil bench to buy a bell and the first thing about it was that not one of the six of them glanced at the bell to see what it was worth.

“Thank you,” said Amos Trewin, and he took the money, and he did not improve it either.

“Do not thank me,” she said. “**And do not let him thank me in a room for a month. He will try to. He has a day-book and you have seen what he does with it.**”

“I have tried to,” said Tomas Vale. “I am not going to try again, and that is not because you told me not to and it is because I do not know what the sentence would be, and I have written about nine hundred pages this month.”

---

He wrapped the bell himself, in a coat and a sacking cloth, in about four minutes, and there was straw in the crate for it and he put the straw in first and put the bell into it and tied the sacking round the crate.

And then he did the thing he had come to the shop to do and had not done, and Tamsin Rook, who was standing at the bench with the empty purse going back into her bag, saw him do it and did not say anything about it, and neither did Amos Trewin until after.

The stock book was on the bench, open, with the ninth column showing.

Tomas Vale put his hands flat on the two pages that held it apart and said, “Mr Trewin. I am going to do something at your bench and I am going to tell you about it and I would like you to look at it with me, and I have never told anybody that there is a thing in it.”

“There is a leaf in it,” said Amos Trewin. “I have known there is a leaf in it for about two years. It falls out when the book is opened at that width and it has fallen out about nine times and I have put it back the same way nine times and I have never read it because it is not my paper and it is not in my hand, and a man who reads a thing in his own book that is not his own writing is a man who is asking a question about somebody who does not know he is being asked one.” He wiped his hands. “Take it out.”

He took it out. It was folded in four and it had been folded in four for a long time, because the fold had gone soft and the corners had gone furry and it had been folded by somebody who folded the same thing the same way and used the same thumbnail.

He opened it on the bench and did not touch it after that, and it was two lines copied off a Crown form, and under the two lines five words in a small upright hand with a wax mark beside them the size of a thumbnail.

*I have not taken this.*

Amos Trewin came and looked at it standing up, the way he had made Tomas Vale read the book, and he read it, and then he read it again, and then he went round the bench and looked at it from the other side, which nobody would have expected him to do and which was the only useful thing anybody could have done with it.

“That is not copying wax on a form,” he said. “That is wax on a form and a thumb on the wax. A copyist presses with the flat of a thumb and leaves a broad flat mark and it comes up like that. **That is the end of a thumb, and it is a small thumb, and it is a person who was not being paid to be copying.**”

“Or a person who was copying for nothing.”

“Or a person who was copying for nothing, and I am not going to tell you which, and neither are you, and that is the whole of what I can do with a thumb.” He straightened up. “And I will tell you the other thing, and it is the reason I have not read it in two years, and I am telling you now because you have not read it either and you are about to. **I have never seen a page in that state of dust in any of my customers' hands. That leaf has been in that book since the shop before mine had the name over the door, and it was folded in four by somebody who thought it was worth four folds.**”

He put two fingers on the book at the top of the page and said, “Put it back the same way, and put it back in the same place, because the crease is the thing. If you fold it in three I shall know, and if you put it in on the other side of the leaf I shall know, and there is nothing else about that piece of paper that anybody could ever know.”

“Do you want it copied.”

“I have a fire and a trade and I do not need it copied.” He looked at the book rather than at him. “I am going to say a thing and I want it understood that I do not know what it is about. A man has stood at my bench twice and asked me for a page of this book twice and I have refused him twice, and I have a leaf in my book that I have never read and cannot account for, and it has been in the shop since before my name was over the door. **I would rather have neither. A man is not a good keeper of a page of paper and he is a worse keeper of one in a book.**”

He left it there, and it lay on the bench for about a second, and then it went back into the two pages, folded in four, in the same place, by the same thumb, and the book was shut, and the bell was in the crate on the bench, and it was about the ninth hour of the morning of the fifty-second and they were eleven hundred yards from a town and four miles from a landing, and Tomas Vale wrote nothing about it in the day-book and told nobody about it, including the woman standing in the shop, and the reason he gave himself, in the flyleaf at the back where he keeps the reasons, is the reason he gave himself the first time: the last time he put a page of somebody's in her hands it was his own and he chose who got it, and she does not need his sister's wax.

---

They walked the four miles into Bramden with a crate on a hand-barrow that Trewin would not lend them and a man who would not let Tamsin Rook take the near end, and they were on the square at about the tenth hour, and the river post slip was at the bottom of the landing, and a man with a slate took his letter without reading it and put it in a satchel and gave him a receipt with a date on it, and the receipt is a Crown document and it is the ninth document that has come into his hands in a month and it is the only one of the nine that cost him eleven pence and told him nothing.

“Will it go?” he asked the man with the slate.

“It goes down at the eleventh and it does not wait and it is at Ashcombe by the evening and it is wherever it is by the fourth,” said the man. “I would not send a letter by it for anything that has to be answered this week. **It is a parcel boat and it is very fast and nobody on it is reading your post.**”

He put the receipt in the inside of his coat on the left, and it was the eleventh pence, and a person on the water had told him it was the fastest thing on the reach, and a man in an office four counties back had told him a different thing about machines, and both of them had been right on their own subject and neither of them had ever been wrong about a machine and neither of them was a liar.

---

The room over the saddler's was the same two rooms and the same bad fire and about six people in it, because it was the second of three market days and the third is the day the town comes in.

Rowan Ketch was at the table by the window with a leather and a set of small hammers, and he had the book from the day before out and shut again in front of him, and he did not get up.

“Yesterday you asked me a question and I answered it and you signed your own name to it,” he said. “That was well done and I have thought about it more than I have thought about most things, and I am going to be harder with you today than I was yesterday, and I want you to know that before we start.

“**Yesterday I told you what the law is. Today I have to find out whether you can use it, and the only way I can find that out is to ask you the question that finds it out, and I am going to ask it in front of six people, and you are going to answer it in front of six people, and if you would rather I did it in a different way then say so now and I will do it in a different way and you will have lost nothing but four minutes.**”

“Ask it,” said Tomas Vale.

“You have put a bell on this table and it is not mine and it is not yours. It has a mark in it and a day in it. Say where the last one came from.”

And the room was quiet, and it was not a hostile quiet, and that was the thing that made it hard, because a hostile room would have given him something to work against and this one had nothing in it but a master with a leather and six tradesmen who had all of them, in their own trades, been asked the same question about where a thing came out of, and had all of them answered it.

So he answered it.

“My name is Tomas Vale. I am a bellmaker. I hold a licence in a county four days from here and I may not practise in this one without a house and I have no house, and I have not made anything in about six weeks.

“The last bell I made I cast myself. It came out of a hearth in a yard in Kells in the spring before last, and I bought the metal in the ordinary way from a man in a market town who was glad to sell it to me, and I did not ask him one question about where it came out of, and I would not have known how to phrase it if I had, and there was no way on earth of finding out afterwards, and that is not because anybody concealed anything from me. **Nobody in that transaction lied to me. Everybody told me the truth about the price and the weight and the quality, and there is no law that a man in a market town must tell a bellmaker where his bellmetal was dug.**”

He turned the bell so that the crown was up.

“It has no certificate and it cannot have one and no certificate can ever issue for it and I made that the same way a man makes a bad road. The Measure found it on the twenty-sixth of this month. I may not ring it in a public place in this county. **The bar is on the bell and not on my licence, and I want that said here because a room of tradesmen has heard the other half of it about nine times: I am not a man who has been put out of a trade. I am a man who made a thing he could not account for and the thing is still not accountable for and it is in a sealed yard in the town I left and I have not stood inside that yard since the twenty-eighth of this month, when they put a district wax on the gate over a bench I had been working at since I was a boy.**”

The man with the lasts said, “Then you are the man in the notice.”

“I am.”

“And I am sorry for it,” said the man with the lasts, and he was not asking for anything, and he said it the way a person says a thing at a door.

“I know you are,” said Tomas Vale. “I am not going to be helped with it. If it is any use, I have been told sorry for about four times in a month and I have not once been able to do anything with it, and I would rather be asked a question.”

“Then I have a question,” said the man with the lasts. “You are past the stone. Why is your bar not past the stone with you?”

“**Because it is on the bell and not on me, and the bell is in a yard four days from here, and a bar is a thing about a bell.** I have walked out of a county with a barred bell in a frame on a barrow and the bar came with it in a receipt, and the receipt is a piece of paper that says so in five places, and if there is one thing I have learned in a month it is that a piece of paper is not a wall and a man in another county is not obliged to be frightened of my signature.”

“That is the law.”

“That is the law and the trade is the other thing, and a smith four miles up this road told me an hour ago, before I had any of it, that a bar follows a bell out of a county in about four months and there is no court in it, and he is right and it is the only reason I can stand in this room at all, because there is no wall on the law and the habit on the trade is the wall, and the habit will close over this bell inside a year and I will not be able to sell it again and I will not be able to give it away, and I have told you that out loud in a room so that it is on a thing somebody heard me say.”

Ketch had not moved through any of it. He had the leather in front of him and the small hammers beside it and he looked at the bell, and then he said:

“All of that is the right answer and I want to tell you what it is worth, which is nothing, and then I am going to sign the certificate, and the two things are not connected and that is the whole of my teaching this morning.

“**The right answer is worth nothing. That is what nobody tells you and every man finds out alone.** A man at that table could tell you where the last one came out of and be right and it would help him not at all. It is not a test. It is not a gate. There is nothing on this side of it and nothing on the other. **I asked you so that six people in this room would hear the answer, and not for me, and the reason I asked it in front of six people is that a thing a man says in a room is a thing a room can be held to, and in about nine years one of them may remember it, and that is worth more to you than anything in my book.**”

He set the bell on the leather and struck it twice, an inch apart, with a wooden shaft, and the note came out of it and filled the two rooms and the bad fire made no difference to it whatever, and Tomas Vale, who has heard a bell in a yard and a bell in a lane and a bell in a market and has not heard one in about six weeks, shut his eyes for the length of one note and did not say anything and did not write anything down.

“Two things for a carrier, that is right,” said Rowan Ketch. “One for a ring that means something, that is right as well. And that is the whole of what I can hear and I have been listening to bells for thirty years and I could not tell you a maker’s ear from a beginner’s ear if you had them side by side on this table, and neither could you.”

---

The certificate is on a sheet of the circuit paper and it is four lines and it is in his own hand and it has the hour in it.

> *Certified under the Bellwrights' Measure, this town, this date, the fifty-second day of the month, at the third hour of the afternoon.*
>
> *The making is complete. Mould and pattern are entered in the book of Roald Byre of Ardwick at the pages against the day struck in the crown, and the mark and the day are in the metal, and the hand is the punch, and the five are joined at the mark.*
>
> *The metal is traced: the bellcaster's supplier is a Crown store holding a Crown document of a day set against it, and the store keeps no further record, and the tracing is good as far as it goes and no further.*
>
> *Signed, Rowan Ketch, master, for this company. Witnessed in this room by six persons of this town whose names are on the circuit sheet below.*

And the circuit sheet below has six names on it, and one of them is the man with the lasts, and he got up and wrote it himself, and he wrote it well.

“Take it,” said Ketch. “There is no second one. **That is not a thing I have done before and I have done it because you asked for the one in your hand and I would have signed two and sent one to a district office and by now four people in this town would know your name and none of them would have asked you anything.**”

“No,” said Tomas Vale. “There is no second one.”

He folded it into the inside of the day-book and it went in beside a receipt he had carried for eleven days, and it lay flat against it, and he shut the book.

Then he said the thing he had come to say, and he said it to a master and not to a room, because the room had heard enough.

“Yesterday you told a woman in this room that a traceable bell is a bell that can be proved, and that it can be stood in front of later, and that it is only worth the making to the maker and never once to the man who has it. And it is a good sentence and I have been carrying it about a day.”

“It is a sound sentence.”

“**It is a quotation and you did not know I had been in the room.** I was not. She was. She came up an outside stair at nine in the morning and got it out of you in four minutes and signed her own name to it and paid for it in a way I am not going to describe in this building, and she is in the shop below with a bag and a bell in it and she does not know I am saying this.”

Rowan Ketch put his hands flat on the table.

“Then you have something a man usually does not have at this stage, and I am going to ask you the last question and then I am going to let you go and buy your metal.

“**You have a certificate, a chain of two shops, a mark, a day and a name in a book. What are you going to do with it.**”

“I have not worked that out.”

“Then do not ring it.”

“I am going to ring it,” said Tomas Vale.

“That is your business and it is not mine and it is not the company’s, and I have said so in a room with six men in it who will have heard me say it, which is the second time this morning I have said a thing in front of six men who will have heard me say it.” He put the hammers away. “And I will tell you what you have and it is not what you think you have, and then you may go and I have not got anything else this week.

“**You have not got a bell that can be proved. You have got a bell that somebody has already proved, once, in front of six witnesses, and those six men are in this town and they will remember it for about a year, and after that it is a piece of paper.** A certificate is a promise to a stranger and the stranger has to be alive and in the same county, and in a year you are a document and a lump of bronze with a note in it, and the only thing that lasts about a certificate is a person who saw it be made. That is why a company is a company and not a filing office, and it is the only thing I know that is worth telling a man who has just been given a sheet of paper and been told it is a certificate.”

“I have a book of my own that I write every day in,” said Tomas Vale, “with four columns in it and nobody in the county has ever read it and I have been told about that by a district officer in his own building in about nine hundred words.”

“Then keep writing it,” said Rowan Ketch, “and do not come back to this room about it.”

---

They were in the room over the shop above the draper's on the north side by about the fourth hour of the afternoon of the fifty-second, and he had the crate on the floor and the sacking off the bell and the bell on the sill in the open window with a leather under it, because the sill was the only flat place in the room that was not somebody's bed.

“You have not rung it,” said Tamsin Rook. She was sitting on the end of the bed with her shoes off, which she did about twice a week and always without explaining it.

“No.”

“It has a mark and a day and a certificate in a book and a chain of two shops, and it has stood on a bench and in a crate and on this sill for about two days and nobody has rung it, and the man in the room over the saddler's struck it twice to hear the note and that was a different thing and both of us know it was.”

“Yes.”

“Why.”

He thought about it for long enough that the room got late.

“**Because the first thing that comes out of it is the first thing it is for.** And I do not know yet what it is for, and if I find out in a square in this town with about nine hundred people in it, then the square decides, and the square is not going to decide anything, the square is going to fill in a gap, and whatever the gap fills in with is in my mouth for the rest of my life and I cannot get it out again. And if I ring it in here on a sill with a leather under it, with you in the room and the door shut and the man next door asleep, then the first thing it says is a note, and a note is not a sentence, and I can hear what it is before I have to say what it means.”

“That is a good reason and it is not the one you are using.”

“It is the one I am using.”

“It is not, and you are allowed to say a thing out loud once in a day, and I will wait.”

He put his hand flat on the bell.

“The other one is that I have never in my life rung a bell I did not make, and I have never in my life rung a bell with no claim in it, and I have been told by a master in his own town that a bell like this one is worth nothing to me and everything to whoever can prove it, and he is right, and I have had thirty-one days to think of a better plan and I have not got one, and a man who has not got a plan should at least get the first note out of a bell in a room with the door shut and one person in it who has already heard every sentence he is going to say.”

She said, “Then ring it,” and moved her feet out of the way of the sill.

He took the leather off the sill, put it on his knee, set the bell on it, and put his hand on the soundbow, and he rang it once.

The room took the note and gave it back smaller, and the window had the sacking out of the bottom of it and half of the sound went out into the street and could not be got back, and the two of them did not say anything for a while, and a man next door turned over once and did not wake.

“That is a carrier,” said Tamsin Rook.

“Yes.”

“You put a waxed crown in a handbell in a yard in Kells that a man paid for, and it carried past the low drain gate, and you told me about it in a lane and I have been walking it ever since. And that thing in this room has **nothing in it**, and it made a bigger noise than a bell half its size, and I can feel it in the sill and in the floor and in the wall behind the bed, and I cannot hear it, and I have not been able to hear a note since the fourth of last month and I am not going to be given it back by this.” She put her hand flat on the wall. “It is here. That is not nothing. I want it written somewhere that it is not nothing, and I am not going to write it, because it would be the second time this month I have put my name on a piece of paper and the first one cost me more than this room is worth.”

He wrote it down in the day-book on the page for things that are not about metal, with the hour, in the flat hand, and he read it back to her before he shut the book, and she corrected one word and he made the change, and neither of them said anything else about the wall.

Then she said, “Tomorrow is the third day.”

“I know.”

“It is the third day of the market and it is the day the town comes in, and it is a bell day as well because there is a parish thing at the low end of the square in the afternoon that wants a bell rung for it and there is no bell in that parish that anybody has the use of, and I asked the carter who told me about the post, and he told me about the bell, and I did not tell him why I asked.”

“You have not asked me whether to ring it.”

“No.”

“Then why are you telling me.”

“**Because you had it in your mouth to ask me, and you did not, and I want you to know that the not asking was noticed.**” She put her stockings on. “You have never asked me once. There has been more than one time there has been a thing you wanted me to do, and every time you have written it down in your own book and not said it to me, and twice I said no to your face and once I said no to a thing you had already been turned down about, and this is the first time I have had to tell you what I would have said. And I would have said yes, and it would have taken about four seconds, and instead I have spent an hour of daylight on the low end of this town finding out whether a parish wants a bell rung for something in the afternoon, and I would like that on the two sides of the slate in your book as well.”

“You spent an hour on a parish that has nothing to do with me.”

“**A parish that has nothing to do with you is the cheapest thing on this road.** It is nine hundred people and a square and a fiddle and no claim in anybody's mouth, and I have been in rooms all month where a man with a receipt is looked at and the receipt is the whole of what anybody can see. Go down to that square and look at it, and then come back up and be asked by a tradesman where your last one came from, and tell me which of the two is harder to answer.”

And she got her shoes on and went out and down the outside stair, and he heard her stop on the bottom step, and then he heard her go down.

He wrote for about a quarter of an hour after that, in the flat hand, with the hour, and the day-book was in four columns and he kept the four columns and did not merge them, and the entry is in the book entire, and it is the fourth sheet of paper he is carrying and the first one he got by asking for a thing to be made rather than for a thing to be unlocked.

> *The fifty-second. I have a bell. It weighs fourteen pounds two ounces, it was cast at the eleventh mile in the spring by a man whose punch every master in the company knows, it has a mark and a day in it and no wax and no dressed groove and no claim of any kind, and a master has certified it in his own hand with the hour, and the metal is traced back through two shops to a Crown store with a Crown document and a day on it, and there is a crease in the world after that and no more of it and there never will be.*
>
> *I did not cast it and I cannot and I did not ask for it to be cast and there is no fire on this road that I could put it in and I have not wanted one for six weeks and I did not want one this morning.*
>
> *I paid for it and I did not have the money. I want that in the book in the plainest words available, because if it is not in the book in a month I will have turned it into something else, and I did not pay for it. A woman paid for it, out of eleven years of not spending anything, in a smith's shop at about the ninth hour of the morning, and she counted it on an anvil bench and told me not to thank her and I am not going to, and if I ever say that I paid for this bell I will be telling a lie about a thing that happened in front of six witnesses in a shop and one of them is the man who sold it to us and he will remember it for the rest of his life.*
>
> *And here is the thing I have not got past in a whole day of walking about with it.*
>
> *I have been proud of it. I have been proud of it since about the eleventh hour and I have not admitted it to anybody including the wall, and I want it in the book in the flat hand because if I take it out of this page I will be a man who has bought a lump of bronze with a note in it and a piece of paper, and that man is not a fraud, he is just a man, and he is not going to be able to do the thing I am going to try to do tomorrow, and the thing I am going to try to do tomorrow is to stand in a square with nine hundred people in it and say something to them with a bell, and the bell has nothing in it, and the whole of what the bell is for is what I say while it is moving.*
>
> *A master in this town told a woman in a room yesterday that a traceable bell is a bell that can be proved and can be stood in front of later and is worth the making to the maker and never once to the man who holds it. I believe him. I have believed him for a day and it has not gone the way those things usually go.*
>
> **A bell that can be proved can be used against whoever proved it. That is not a danger to me. There is nothing in this bell to be used against anybody, because I have no household, no claim, no keeper and no standing in any ward in any county, and my own receipt in my own coat says so in five places and I wrote the fifth one. And the day that is a comfort to me is the day it becomes a mouth, because a mouth that belongs to nobody is a mouth that anybody standing there can put a sentence into, and tomorrow there will be nine hundred people standing near it.**
>
> *I have rung it once, this afternoon, on a sill, in a room, with the door shut, with one person in it who has already heard everything I am going to say. It went out of the window and I did not get it back and I could not have got it back from the sill either and that is the first honest sentence I have written about a bell in six weeks.*
>
> *A parcel boat took a letter to a woman at a water bell at the tenth hour this morning, and the receipt is in the coat, and I have written down what I am owed and what I am not owed and I am going to leave it there and I am not going to write to her again whatever comes back.*
