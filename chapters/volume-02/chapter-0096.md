# Chapter 0096 — A Fragment Of The Name Ledger

He was out of the room over the draper's shop at about the fourth hour on the eighty-second day of the month, , because a man had told him the day before in one sentence and had not said it to be unkind, and there is no arguing with a man who tells you a thing early.

What he had at about the fourth hour was a yard behind a shop, a barrow, a frame in a reinforced padded cradle with four retaining bolts made fast in it and no clapper in it, a bell of fourteen pounds two ounces in a sack, a book, five receipts, thirteen documents, a slate with a ruled-through line on it, and about four hours before the rain.

**And he had a list of about nine names in a coat, in his own hand, and a receipt with five clauses in it, and a question he had been carrying for forty days.**

The question was not *where do the names go*. He had asked four people that in four counties and got a column, a stamp, a habit, a man in charge of shelves who has never been upstairs, and a form with four fields and none of them the words.

**The question was whether there was a book.**

Because if there is no book then there is nothing to find and he has been a man looking for a shelf in a building that has never had one, and there is nothing anybody can do about that and it is a dead end four days long. And if there is a book then it is in a room, and a room can be asked, and the whole of what he has learned in six weeks is that a room cannot be asked and only a person in a room can.

There was a room. It was on this street, up an outside stair, with three books open on a table and a fire out of about a fortnight.

---

He did not ask the woman who lets them.

That is on the page and it is the first thing he wrote down about the eighty-second and he wrote it before he did anything else, because he had asked that woman to put a thing in a box on the seventy-first and she had done it and asked for nothing and had told him twice for nothing what she keeps, and there is no version of the eighty-second in which he asks her again about a table she has already described to him out of her own mouth.

**And there is one more thing and it is the reason, and it is the whole of six weeks: a person may be asked and may refuse, and he has been refused six times since the thirty-fourth and not one of the six cost her anything, and the next one is not his to spend on a woman who has never once asked him for anything and who keeps about nine hundred names in four years and has never been asked what her book is for.**

So he went and found out who owned the house, and the man who owned the house was a dyer, and he was in a vat room at the back of it and he was not a servant and he was not an officer and he had no office and he was not on any list that Tomas Vale had ever seen.

**And he had a key to his own upper rooms, and about four years of a room over his own vat room that he could not let.**

---

He said that last part himself, in about nine seconds, before Tomas Vale had got to the second sentence, and he said it standing in a passage with his hands wet.

“**Four years. They have had the two top rooms four years and they pay me for them whether anybody is in them or not, and there was a woman in them on and off for about nine years and I could not let them to anybody, and there has been nothing in them since the sixty-seventh and I am still paying for them. I have not had a wage out of those two rooms in four years and I have had about nine pounds a quarter out of them.**”

“That is about thirty-six pounds.”

“**That is about thirty-six pounds and it is four years and it is correct and I am not asking you for it and I have not got anybody to ask.**”

“You are not being asked about the thirty-six pounds. I am asking you about the table.”

“**Then ask me about the table and I will tell you what I know about the table, which is that there are three books open on it and a fire out and they have been like that a fortnight, and I went up about four days ago and shut the fire and I did not touch the books and I am not going to touch the books.**”

---

He thought about it for about four minutes on a step by the vat room, and the thinking is on the page because it is the only part of the eighty-second that was difficult.

**What he wanted was a sheet out of a book that was not his, in a room that was not his, in a building that was not the Crown's, from a set of papers belonging to an office that had shut on the morning of the sixty-seventh and would not open again until the spring.**

And there was no law against it, and no form against it, and no clause of a receipt that touched it, and no notice on any wall that said it could not be done.

**And there was also no way whatever to show that a man had not done it, which is the whole of why he could do it and also the whole of why he could not afterwards put it in a book and have anybody believe him.**

He went back and asked the dyer one question, and it was his own chore and it was about nine seconds long.

“**If you went up those stairs and took a page out of one of those three books and put it in your coat, and nobody had asked you to and nobody had paid you and you were not going to be thanked, would you do it.**”

The dyer looked at him for a while.

“**No,**” he said. “**I would take the whole of the fire out and I would not touch the books. I have been up there four times in four years and I have not touched anything, and I would like to be able to keep saying that.**”

“All right. That is the right answer and I am not going to argue with it and I am not going to come back to it.”

“**Then why are you standing on my step.**”

“**Because you have a key to your own house and nobody has asked you to go up there and I am not asking you either, and the difference between the two of those is about four inches and it is the whole of what I have learned in six weeks, and I have not got it right yet and I have noticed that about four times today.**”

---

The dyer went up at about the seventh hour, on his own account, and Tomas Vale did not go with him and stood in the passage with a barrow he could not park anywhere, and Tamsin Rook was at the bottom of the outside stair with a slate under her arm and he did not ask her to go up and she did not offer and neither of them has mentioned it since and the reason is in the book for the eighty-second and the reason is that the seventh refusal cost him and the eighth would have cost her a fortnight of a district's regard in a town of two thousand.

It took about nine minutes and it came down the outside stair in a man's coat buttoned over one pocket.

**And the man who came down did not claim to have any right, and said so before he was asked, and did not say what it was for.**

“**I have no right. I want that said in a room before anything else. I am the owner of this house and I have a key to my own rooms and there is a thing in them that is not mine, and I have taken nine lines out of a book that is not mine, and I have done it because a man with a barrow has been standing at the bottom of my stair for six days and because I have been paying for two rooms for four years, and if you tell anybody I have said the second of those things I will say it again and I will say it in a room.**”

“Nobody is going to be told anything by me.”

“**Then I will tell you the third thing, which is the one that matters to me. I have been up there four times in four years and I have not touched anything, and I have just touched something, and I am going to be a man who touched something, and I could have been a man who did not, and I would have been able to say that at the end of my life.**”

That is the whole of it and it took about a minute and a half and he never put it better than that and he did not try.

---

**And the witness wrote the hour down, in her own hand, on a sheet of her own, and she was asked to do it by a man with no standing, and she could have refused, and he had about nine seconds to hear whether she was going to.**

The woman who lets rooms had come out at about the seventh hour because a man was in her passage and there is a rule about that in a house with rooms in it, and she stood at the bottom of the stair and she did not come up.

“**Say what you are about to do,**” she said, “**and say it where the person at the top of that stair can hear you, and then I will do the thing or I will not do it, and I am telling you now that I have not decided.**”

And Tomas Vale said it out loud, on the bottom step, with the dyer at the top of it, in about nine seconds, and he said where he was and what the day was and what was being taken out and how many lines, and he said the words *a district's business*, and he did not say what he thought it was for.

“**I have kept a book four years,**” she said. “**About nine hundred names in it and a column for the nights and a column for what was owed and a column for the room, and I have never once been asked what it is, and a man with a slate asked me what it was on the sixty-first day of this month and told me in the same breath that she did not know either and that nobody has.**”

“I have.”

“**So I am going to write the hour down. I am not doing it for you and I am not doing it for him and I am doing it because a woman of mine put her hand on a line in this town four days ago in a market and the district shut that evening, and there is not one written thing anywhere in this town that says a thing like that was done, and I have four years of people in a book and about nine hundred of them could be called a witness to something and none of them has ever been asked.**”

She wrote it on a sheet of her own and in her own hand and with the hour against it, and she read it out, and it names a day, an hour, a stair, a man and a number of lines, and I have not got it and I am not going to ask for it and I am not going to pretend to know the shape of it.

**And she kept it.**

“Do you want it.”

“No,” said Tomas Vale. “**And I want it written down that I did not ask for it and did not try, and that I am not going to have it, and that the reason is that a person can be asked and a piece of paper cannot, and I have spent six weeks learning the second half of that and I have never once been able to use it.**”

She looked at him for a moment and then said, “**You are the fourth person in six weeks who has refused something in this house and you are the first one who said why.**”

---

He read the fragment in the passage, standing up, in about two minutes, and it is a fragment and it is nine lines and it is the whole of what this batch got out of a building that had been shut a fortnight.

**A column of numbers. A column of days. And a third column that is empty from end to end, nine lines of it, ruled in the same hand and never once used.**

That is all it is. A hand, a column, about nine lines, and a book of numbers.

**And a district number is a filing reference and not a man.**

He had that on the forty-second day of the month, in a district office in another county, in an officer's own hand and signed, and the officer had said that a guess written down stops being a guess in about a year, and had refused to guess, and had been right. **He had carried it for forty days and had used it about nine times as a way of not asking a question, and it turned out this morning that it was a true statement about a piece of paper in his own hands and he had not once thought of it that way.**

The numbers on the fragment were in a sequence. Two of the nine were the same number and had two different days against them. One was struck through in a hand that was not the ruling hand, and the striking had a day, and there was nothing against the striking.

**And that is the whole of it. It does not say where a name goes. It does not say a name at all. It says a reference goes, and a reference going somewhere is not a person, and I have known that for forty days and I have never once seen it printed nine times in a row by somebody who was not paid to print it.**

He wrote that down and then wrote under it, immediately, in the same hand, because he knew what he was about to do:

> *And I am not going to join this to the six certified returns in a drawer two counties back that have one name and one hand in them and five different days on them, and a man in that house has not been in it for six years. **A person who joins them has joined them because they are both dates.** I have had that in this book since the seventy-third and I am writing it out again on the eighty-second because I had the page in my hand and I felt the thing start, and it is the oldest habit I have got.*

---

He tried to thank them at the bottom of the stair, both of them, in about four seconds, and was refused twice, in the ordinary way, by people who were not angry and were not pleased.

The dyer said: “**You did not pay me and you did not ask me to and I would have said no to the money. Do not do it again. If you say thank you to me twice I will begin to think the thirty-six pounds is coming back and it is not, and I have made my peace with that and I do not want it unsettled.**”

And the woman with the book said: “**No. And you are not to say it to the man at the top of that stair either, and if you do I shall take my sheet out and burn it, and it is the only thing I have written down in four years that anybody asked me for, and I would rather be a woman who has written one thing down than a woman who has been thanked.**”

And neither of them would give a name, and he did not ask for one, and he has not been back to that stair, and **neither of them is owed a visit and there is nothing he can do for either of them and there is no form on which he could do it and there is no column for it and no clerk can enter it.**

---

He wrote the last entry of the eighty-second at about the ninth hour of the evening, in the yard behind the draper's shop, on an upturned crate, in the flat hand, with the hours, and he filled the first and third columns and left the second and the fourth as they were.

> *The eighty-second. I have a fragment of a book of numbers. It is nine lines, a hand, a column, a column of days and a third column that is empty from end to end, and it is the whole of what was in the two rooms over a dyer's, and it does not tell me where a name goes and I am not going to write down that it does.*
>
> ***A district number is a filing reference and not a man.*** A district officer in an office in another county said that in his own hand on the forty-second day of the month and refused to guess at it and was right, and I have carried it for forty days and used it nine times as a way of not asking a question, and it has turned out to be a true statement about a piece of paper in my own hands and I have not once thought of it that way until this afternoon. Two of the nine numbers on the fragment are the same number with two different days against them. One is struck through with a day and nothing against the striking. **And a reference going somewhere is not a person and that is a smaller thing than I have been carrying and it is the first time I have seen it nine times in a row in a hand nobody paid me to read.**
>
> *And what it does not do. **It does not tell me how an office gets into a bell. I have written that I do not know it twice, in four counties, in my own hand, and I do not know it this afternoon and I have not got nearer it in six weeks and I have had a book in my hands for the first time in this business and I have not got nearer it, and I am not going to write a paragraph about that because a paragraph about that is a thing I would be able to carry.**
>
> *And the two of them, and I am writing this flat because it is the whole of what the day was: **a man took nine lines out of a room in his own house that was not his, and said so before anybody asked him and said what it was for and it was not for me. A woman with a book of about nine hundred names wrote the hour down in her own hand and kept the sheet and would not give it to me and I did not ask for it and did not try. I tried to thank them both and I was refused twice in about four seconds and I am not going back to that stair and neither of them is owed a visit and there is no column for either of them and no clerk can enter it and there is no form on which I could do anything for them at all.***
>
> *And the finding, and it is the finding of six weeks and it is four inches long: **this is the first time in six weeks that I have got a thing that I did not have to be right about.** I did not persuade a man with a barrow; I said a thing out loud on a bottom step and a man in a vat room had a grievance of his own, which was about thirty-six pounds and four years, and a woman who keeps nine hundred names had a grievance of her own about not being asked, and neither of them owed me a thing and both of them were owed something and I was the man who was standing in the passage. **I have spent six weeks finding out that I have no standing and I have not once found out what a man with no standing is for, and it turns out he is for standing in a passage while two people do a thing for their own reasons, and then getting out of the way.***
>
> ***All five clauses stand and none of them was touched today and I did not use any of them. Nothing was rung. The frame is in this yard with four bolts made fast in it and no clapper in it. The bell is in a sack in this yard and it has been rung once in a market in this town and it is not certified for anything and there is nothing in it, and I have not touched it since the seventy-seventh and I do not know where it went and I am not going to find out.***
