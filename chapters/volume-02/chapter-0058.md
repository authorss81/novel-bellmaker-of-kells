# Chapter 0058 — An Office With an Address

The Crown district renewal office at Fourmile is on a street with three shops and a yard behind it and a door that has been painted four times and is now the colour of a duck's egg, and the district's number is on a plate by the bell, and the bell is a wire with a knocker on it and not a bell, and the plate is legible from the middle of the street.

They got there at about the ninth hour on the forty-second day and Tamsin Rook stopped at the door and read the plate, and read it twice, and then went and sat on a wall about forty feet off with her bag on her knees, and she said that if he went in alone he would be a man asking about a bell and if he went in with her he would be a man and a courier, and she was not going to stand in a street being a third thing a clerk had to write down.

That was not the leash. She said it as her own decision, out loud, before he could use any part of it, which is what she has done since the thirty-first.

---

He got as far as the first desk, which is the whole of the room on the left of the passage, and the desk is a bench with a man behind it and a book open on it, and he put his hand on the counter with the four questions on a sheet in his hand and the clerk looked at the sheet, and then at his face, and then at the yard behind him where a barrow stood in the sun with a frame in a cradle on it and four bolts in it.

“Who sent you,” said the clerk.

“Nobody sent me. My name is Tomas Vale. I was in your district on the night of the twenty-eighth and on the thirty-first and I have a custody receipt with a district officer's district wax on the file and I have a sheet here with four questions on it, and I would like to give it to District Officer Cope if he is in.”

The clerk did not take the sheet. The clerk put his hand on it and did not lift it, the way a man does with a paper in a yard.

“Vale,” he said. “Tomas Vale, of Kells.”

“Yes.”

“There is a file in this building with that name in it,” said the clerk, “and it is not mine, and I am not going to tell you what is in it, and I would like it understood that I did not tell you and that you did not ask, and that if I had been asked I would have had to go and stand somewhere else.”

He went and stood somewhere else and came back with a man.

---

Marten Cope is not what a man looks like when he has sent a rider with a spade, which is what Tomas Vale had been expecting for a month, and what he had got instead was a district officer in a good coat with a pen in his coat and a great deal of paper on his own desk, and he read the four questions standing up.

“Come in,” he said. “Not into that room. Mine. Leave the barrow where it is and leave the frame in the cradle, and do not take that thing up a staircase, because I have read a notice about it and I would like my stairwell to be a place where nothing has been carried that I cannot account for.”

The room is a room with a window and a table and about eleven shelves, and four of the shelves are bound files and the seven are loose papers in squares, and every square has a label on it, and the labels are in the same hand, and there is a chair on the other side of the table for a person who is being answered.

Tomas Vale sat down in it. He noticed the chair. He noticed that there was one chair for a person who was answered and that it was not covered and that the room was not the room a person is put in when a district does not want to be argued with.

“Right,” said Cope. “I have read your four questions. I am going to do three things and I am going to do them in the order I am going to do them, and I am going to tell you the order first, because the order is the only part of this you could complain about and I would rather you had it now than later.

“The first is that I am going to answer three of the four in writing, in my own name, within the hour. The second is that I am going to refuse the fourth in writing, in my own name, with a reason, and you are going to get the refusal in the same envelope and in the same hand as the three answers, because a refusal handed over with the answers is a fact about this office and a refusal handed over on its own is an insult. The third is that if you argue with me about any of the four, I will stop, and not as a tactic. If you argue, I have nothing further to do with you today and I would rather not have had the argument and would rather be told I was wrong and left.”

“I am not going to argue with you about any of the four,” Tomas Vale said.

“You have not heard the fourth.”

“Then I will not argue with you about the first three and I will listen to the fourth and then decide.”

---

**His first answer**, in his own hand, with the date and the hour on it, and signed:

*Q. Is the water bell at Ocklebridge entered upon any schedule held in this office? Under what line, and with what standing against the field for a keeper?*

*A. It is. It is entered in this office's sheet of water bells for the north river, under the line for Ocklebridge, entered on the day of the return of the year then current, in a clerk's hand, as a bell of that ward. The field for a keeper is empty, and I am going to say why, because the why is the answer and not the empty box: the district's form for this sheet has four fields, which are the bell, the place, the hour it is rung, and the person who keeps it, and the form was made for a bell that is rung on a public route by a named keeper, and a water bell at a river village is not that, and this office has never had an instrument for a water bell, and where an instrument does not exist the field is not left empty by a person. It is left empty by a form. I have this morning entered a marginal note on that line asking the district for an instrument, and I have entered it in my own name, and if it is not granted I will enter that it was not granted.*

**His second answer**, in the same hand, an hour later:

*Q. Does this office hold, or has this office ever held, a document in which one hand signs as the answer of more than one household?*

*A. It does. I have personally seen four such returns in one file, in this building, over eleven years, from four different districts, in four different hands of the person who received them, and all four bear at the signature line the same hand, and in each of the four the signatory is described in the receiving clerk's note as the householder, and in no case have I asked whether the person signing is of the house, because this office's form has no column in which that could be entered, and a thing that cannot be entered is a thing nobody asks about. I will describe the hand, because you are the second person in eleven years to ask me and I would like it in a document that is not mine: it leans forward. The descender of the g hesitates and comes back. The downstroke of a d and of a g stop and start in the same place. And it is the same in four counties and in four receiving clerks' hands, and that is the part that convinced me, and I will tell you why, because it is a trade point and you are a craftsman and you will know what I mean — a clerk's hand is never the same twice and a signer's hand is the same every time, and I have been reading four different clerks' copies of one person's writing for eleven years and I have never once been able to see him. I am not going to describe the households to you. I am going to tell you why, and the reason is not modesty: a document in a file is not a person, and I have watched a person become a document in this office in eleven years, and the road to that is a question put in a corridor, and I am not going to put it in a corridor. I will tell you the file, if you will give me a sheet to write it on and I will give you the file and not the houses. — M.C., District Officer, this office, this day, at the tenth hour.*

**His third answer**, in the same hand, and it is the best piece of paper Tomas Vale was given in four days:

*Q. Does this office transmit any schedule, roll or list of names to any other office, and if so to which, and by whose order?*

*A. This office transmits nothing. This office's copy of a schedule stays in this building, and I have checked that in the last eleven years with the man who keeps the outward bag, who is not in my office and whose name I am not putting on paper for a question of mine, and he has told me the outward bag has gone out twice in eleven years and both times it was a packet of receipts for money. I will not put his name on it and I will not be asked twice. — and then, on the same sheet, in the same ink and about four minutes later, and I am recording that the two sentences are by the same officer and were written four minutes apart and Cope had not written anything to sign it. — I have put that question in writing to the district twice. Once at the district's own request and once because I asked it and the district did not object to being asked. I have had no answer to either. I record the dates of my own two letters, which are in the file with the file, and I record that I have had no answer, and I record that I will ask a third time, and I record that I am not going to speculate in a document about where a name goes, because a document is a thing that people believe, and I do not know, and I will not be the man who writes a guess down in a building where the guess is then true.*

Then, on a fourth sheet, separately, and Tomas Vale has it still:

*And one further thing that you did not ask me and that I am setting down because you are in the room. Your third question is about names, and you have been carrying a mark for a month that you have told me about in four sentences and have not once asked me about, and I am going to say the only thing about it that I know. An office mark in a return is a reference to a filing place. It is not a man. In this district they are two letters and four figures and they refer to a desk in this building or a press in a store in this town, and I can name the desks to you this morning if you want them. I cannot tell you what a mark in a district that this office does not work in refers to, and I am not going to guess at it in writing, and I will tell you why I am not going to guess: because I would be guessing in a room where a file is kept, and a guess that is written down stops being a guess in about a year, and you have carried that mark for a month and I would rather you carried it a year longer than have me hand you a sentence.*

---

**And his refusal**, in the same hand, on the fourth sheet, and he gave it to Tomas Vale with the other three and did not hand it across a table separately, which was the promise he had made about the order and he kept it:

*Q. Is the schedule for route L-4, of which this office holds the Crown copy, a return?*

*I decline to answer this, and I give the reason, and I give it in my own name and I will sign it and it may be read by anybody.*

*The reason is this. This office's instruments require a return. A return is a thing a household has sent in, in answer to a question put to it, and this office certifies a return and stamps a return and files a return, and the whole of the trade of this office is in the word. A roll is not a return. A roll is a list the office has made out of returns it has already had, and the difference is not a difference of wording and it is not a difference of degree, and I know that because I have this office's form open in front of me and it has four columns and every one of them is a thing a person did.*

*So: to answer your fourth question properly I would have to write down, in a document, in this building, that the largest instrument this office holds upon one route is not one of the things this office's own forms recognise. And that document would go to a district office. And I am not yet willing to say that in a document that leaves this building, and I am not going to give you a braver reason than that, because I have given this district the best of myself for eleven years and I have got a room with a chair in it and I have answered three of your four questions within the hour and I am not going to pretend to you that a man who will do that is a man who does not know what saying the fourth one costs. I know what it costs. I have known since about the ninth year. That is not cowardice and I am not going to have it called that in a document either.*

*If it is a return, say so and this office will act on it at once and I will be glad to be wrong this afternoon. If it is not a return, then the answer is not mine to give, because the person who can give it is the person who has made it into a roll, and that is not this office. And I will tell you the thing that is underneath that, and then I will stop: this office has no instrument for a roll. Not a wrong one. None. I asked for one in the ninth year and I was refused, and I did not ask again, and that is the whole of my own part in it and I have put it in this sheet in my own name so that it is in a book somewhere and not only in me.*

*— Marten Cope, District Officer, this office, this day, about the sixth hour.*

---

He read it twice and then put it down on the table square with the other three, which was a thing a man does with paper he has decided to respect.

“You have not argued,” Cope said.

“No.”

“I had a speech ready and I want you to know that I had one ready, and I have not had to use it, and I am not going to pretend that I am disappointed. I am, a little. I have answered three of four inside an hour and refused one in the same ink and I would have liked to be argued with about the fourth, because being argued with is a thing I have got quite good at and it is a thing I can survive.” He put the pen down. “I will say the other thing, because you came with four questions and a chair and a bell in a yard and you did not once tell me a story, and I have had a month in which everybody in this district has been told a story by somebody.”

“All right.”

“Your letter of the thirty-second was a bad letter and I wrote it because I could not answer a question the district had put to itself, and I have not apologised for it and I am not going to. I put a man in a room with an object and I did it in the correct form and I would do it again. The custody review on the thirty-third was mine, and the review is the only reason that object is on a barrow and not in a store in a building I closed. **The withdrawal of the finding of the twenty-eighth was not mine. It was a surveyor's and she did it on her own paper and I filed it and I am not going to stand in another man's clean water and say I did it.** And the thing that came back to me out of that ward is not a win. It is a document that says my district was wrong about a set of bells, and there are about nine hundred bells in this district and nobody has asked for it.”

“Nobody has asked for it,” Tomas Vale said.

“Then it is worth nothing to me and it is worth nothing to you and it will still be on the file in nine years, and I would like you to hear me say that, because I think you came to this building with the idea that I withdrew a finding against my district and that makes me a man on your side, and I am not a man on your side. I am a man who would like the next question I am asked to be smaller.”

“Do you want that to happen? The next question being smaller.”

“No,” said Cope. “I want the next question I am asked to be the **right** one. There is a difference and the difference is the whole of this office, and it is about four lines long, and nobody has ever put it in a frame or a bell, and it is this: that a question a person can check in an hour is worth nine questions a person cannot check at all.”

---

There was one more thing, and Tomas Vale asked for it in one breath, and it is the last thing in this chapter that he will be thanked for and it is not the last thing in the day.

“District Officer.”

“Mr Vale.”

“May I have the refusal — that sheet, the fourth one, the one with your reason on it — filed where a person can ask for it.”

Cope looked at him for slightly longer than a man needs to.

“That is not a usual request,” he said.

“I know. A person can ask for the three answers because the three answers are about a person. There is no instrument for asking to see a refusal, because a refusal is not held. It is kept in a man's head until he is asked about it again, and a man who is never asked about it again has never really refused anything. He has just declined, and a decline is not on paper, and a paper is the only thing anybody can be held to in nine years.”

“Eleven,” said Cope, “in my case.”

“Then eleven years.”

Cope wrote a second sheet. It is half a page and it says that on the forty-second day a person asked, in this room, for the district's refusal of the fourth question to be filed in this office's book of refusals, where any person may ask for it and may have it copied, and that the district has filed it, and that the person who asked is Tomas Vale, of Kells, without district, and that the district makes no objection to a person asking, and has never had any objection to a person asking, and regrets having described a schedule as non-response in the second week of the month past.

“There is a book,” he said. “It has about two hundred and forty entries in eleven years. I have never once had anybody ask to see one. The two hundred and forty are all of one kind: a district has declined to supply a document on a ground of privilege or of some other district's business, and the ground is entered and the person who asked is not, because that was the practice until about four years ago and it stopped for no reason anybody can now account for, and I have never found out why it stopped and I have never asked, because I was the officer who stopped it and I do not remember deciding to.”

He signed that one too.

---

Tomas Vale wrote the four questions out again on his own page before he left, in four columns, with the hour against each, and the answer or the refusal in its own column, and the columns not merged.

*First question, the ninth hour, answered, signed, in his own hand, and the hour is on it.*
*Second question, the tenth hour, answered, signed, in his own hand, and the hour is on it.*
*Third question, the twelfth hour, answered, signed, in his own hand, and the hour is on it, and there is a second sheet, four minutes later, and it is the better one.*
*Fourth question, about the sixth hour, refused, signed, with a reason, in his own hand, and filed in a book of two hundred and forty entries where any person may ask for it.*

He did not write *answered* three times and *refused* once in one sentence, and he did not write *the district answered three of four questions*, because a sentence like that is a response and a response is the thing this whole business has been about for six days, and there is a book in Kells that says that *an empty column is a number that is missing and a sentence is the one thing a Crown office has never had to check*, and he is not going to be the man who merges a colleague's four answers into a paragraph in a book that may be read by somebody hostile in nine years.

Nobody merged them, including him.

And on the last line of the page he wrote the thing that is the real discovery of this chapter, which is nine words long, and it is the first time in a month that the man who lost everything has said a sentence about his own trade that he is not ashamed of:

*Being right is not the beginning of an exchange. I came four days for a door and there is a man in a chair in a building with an address and he answered three of my questions in his own hand inside an hour and refused the fourth in the same ink with a reason that cost him something, and he did not convert, and he did not repent, and he is not a door and he did not open one, and I would walk four miles back into that room tomorrow.*

*And I have learned nothing about how an office gets into a bell, and I have learned that a man with a desk has become a person this month without anybody doing it to him on purpose, and I have learned that he is the reason the fourth question is now on paper in his own name, and I have learned that I am further from the right to speak for one household in Kells than I was on the thirty-third, and I have learned that a man can be further off and be more useful, and I do not know what to do with that and I am going to write it down and I am going to leave it.*

---

At about the sixth hour, in the passage, with the duck's-egg door open behind him, he said the thing to Tamsin Rook that he had been carrying down four counties and did not improve.

“I am not going to be able to answer it,” he said. “The fourth one. It is the only one that matters and he has written down in his own name that he is not yet willing to say it, and I am not going to be the man who makes a district officer say it, and I want to be at the yard book about the four columns and the two signatures before I say anything else, because I have got four pieces of paper off a man I have never met and I am not going to be the man who leaves that room and does not write down exactly what they say.”

“Four pieces of paper,” Tamsin Rook said. “In four columns.”

“In four columns, with the hour against each, and the answers and the refusal in their own, and nobody merging them, and I did that myself and I want you to know I did that myself and that I did it because you told me about an empty column on a towpath four days ago, and I am writing that down as well and it is the same page.”

She took the four sheets off him in the street and read them standing up against a wall, in the open, in front of about eleven people going to market, and it took her until they had gone past, and she did not ask him a single question while she read it, which she has done four times now and which is the highest thing anybody in this batch has been given.

Then she folded them in order and gave them back and said:

“He is the first person in six days who has refused you something you needed and written down why, and the reason he gave you is worth more than the three answers, and I want you to understand that the three answers are in the world and the fourth is not, and that the fourth is the one that can only be got household by household, and by nobody else at all, ever, because a household is the only thing in this kingdom that can say a thing a document may not say for it.”

“Then we go and ask households.”

“**Both of us,**” Tamsin Rook said. “That is the whole of what that refusal is worth and it is worth four days of road. It can only be answered household by household, one door, one hand, one answer that belongs to that house and to nobody else, and the two of us are the only two people on this road who can put a question at a door in a form a clerk has to answer, and that is not because of anything we are. It is because there happens to be two of us and the district has a chair with one man in it.”

“And the leash.”

“And the leash,” she said. “Which neither of us has used in nine days, and which I have started to think about about four times a day, and which is going to come out of my mouth at a door today in a way you are not going to like, and you are not going to be able to do anything about it, and that is the entire point of it and it is Mercy Slee's doing and I have told her so and she said she knew and would have it in the ward's book.”

She stood up off the wall and put her bag on her hip.

“I will not carry your question for you,” Tamsin Rook said, “and I will not put your name at a door, and I will not use a thing that came out of this building this morning as a reason to be let in. And you will not put my name at yours. And in about nine days one of us is going to be asked to do the other's, and the answer is going to be no, and the person who asked may not ask again that day. That is the slate and Mercy wrote it and neither of us did and I am telling you now so that it is not a surprise and so that it is a rule and not an accident.”
