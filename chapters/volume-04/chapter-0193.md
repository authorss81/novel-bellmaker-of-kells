# Chapter 0193 — Pell Okell Gave a Day and Would Not Say Why He Gave It

It was the fourth hour of the hundred and seventy-ninth day and Tomas Vale made the four bolts fast on the flat of beaten earth above the water, which took him four minutes and is the whole of the second clause of a receipt in five clauses, all five standing, and it is a hundred and forty-six days since the frame has been in a building, and a flat of beaten earth above nine foot of river is not a building, and there is nothing in that cradle that can strike anything.

Pell Okell was on the flat with a barrow of nothing, having walked out to meet a man and been told there was nothing to carry, and he put the barrow down against the stone and looked at the water coming over the boards of the landing stage.

"That is the fourth mark," he said. "Two days ago that was the second one and I would have bet money on the second one."

"You would have lost the bet."

"I would have lost it and I would have said so." He put his hands on his hips and looked at the low quarter through four hundred yards of morning. "What have you got, and I am asking because I have been asked one question in nine days and it was whether the time was."

Tomas told him.

He told it standing on the flat with the barrow behind him, and he told it the way he would go on telling it for the next four days, in the same words, and the words were these.

"The water on this reach went up nine foot in four days. It is four inches over the mark that was two years back at the seventh hour. The second rise is on the turn."

"That is a number and a mark."

"That is a number and a mark and it is true, and it is the whole of what I have got." Tomas did not put an order on the end of it. "I have had four different people in two days tell me what they are going to lose and all four of them were right, and if I put get out on the end of that number then a man who is owed eleven days hears a landlord and a man in a flat that is going to be worth something hears a landlord, and a woman at a door who has been out of her house once already hears the man who shut a church."

Pell Okell looked at him for a while and then at the low quarter and then at the water.

"Right," he said. "I will say it that way. What I am not going to do is say get out, and I want you to hear me say that I am not going to, because I could have said it this morning at the upper landing where there are eleven men on a flat with a boat and four of them are asking me, and I have not said it to any of them, and I am not going to, and I have not told you why and you are not going to ask me and I am not going to wait for you to."

"I am not going to ask you."

"Good." He turned the barrow a quarter round. "Then here is what a day is. A day is a carter's walking. It is nine hours on a road and everything in it belongs to somebody, and I do not own any of it, and when I give one of them away somebody is short and it is not me that decides which one. There is a lane above the ferry road two miles up from here with a gate at the top of it, and there are two carts of mine on that lane that I put there on the day before yesterday, and nine foot of water is going to be in it by the second hour, and the man at the top of it does his own loading when the carter is not there when the carter said he would be, and he did it on the day before yesterday, and he is not going to ask me again this week, and he has not asked me and I am not going to tell him I am not coming, because a man does not come."

---

He worked it out on the flat between the fourth hour and the sixth hour and it is the plainest arithmetic of the four days, and there is not a single part of it that is clever.

A warning has to be told to somebody by somebody who is standing in front of them, because every other instrument in this kingdom is a thing with a space on it for a person to write in, and a person can write, and what a person writes can be left out of a copy, and a copy can be left out of a book, and a book can be shut. So the whole of the instrument is a mouth, and a mouth has to be attached to legs, and legs take a day.

So: from the flat down the bank and along the low quarter's ground and through the passage behind the fourth house to the yard. Four legs and a turn, and no paper anywhere in it at all, because paper is the thing that can be edited and a person on foot is the thing that cannot.

Leg one was his own and it took him an hour and a quarter. Leg two was a carter's day and Pell Okell took it without being asked and had not been asked.

Leg three, from the fourth house to the top of the passage, was a boy of thirteen called Wick, who came out of the front door of the fourth house because a carter he knew was standing in the passage, and Pell gave him the words in the exact order Tomas had given them and made him say them back twice, and the boy said them back twice and got them right and could not have told anybody where he had learned them.

Leg four, the last two hundred yards, was a man at the upper landing with a boat, and Tomas went and gave it to him himself at the fifth hour because a leg that is done by the man who invented the words is not a leg.

And then the four of them stood about the top of the steps and worked out the thing nobody had worked out, which was that the passage behind the fourth house fills before the low quarter does, because the ground there is a foot lower and the water comes off the market ground into it as soon as it is over the market ground, and at the sixth hour the passage would be about a foot and a half deep at the top end and three foot at the bottom end.

"That is your last leg," Pell said.

"That is my last leg and it is the yard I cannot see from the flat and nobody on it can see the yard from anywhere else." Tomas looked down the steps at the water, which was at the third one and going for the fourth. "I have a warning and four legs and one of the four does not have anything in it, and I have not got a fifth, and I am not going to write one on a piece of paper, because the moment I write it down it is a thing somebody can carry past me and be right about in the wrong direction."

"Wick can swim," Pell said, and it was the only thing anybody said for two minutes that was not about the water, and it was not a question.

"Wick can swim and I am not going to send a boy of thirteen into three foot of moving water in the dark with a sentence about a mark."

"That is right," Pell said, and meant it, and neither of them said anything else about it, and the two of them stood on the flat with the light going and a boy of thirteen somewhere between them in the middle of a day he had been given.

At about the seventh hour Tomas went back down the bank himself, leg one, again, out loud, the same words, to a woman at a door who had shut hers at the fourth hour and had come out again at the seventh because a barrow went past twice in a day in a ground where nothing goes past twice, and she listened and said nothing and went in, and he did not knock at the next one and he did not knock at the first one and he did not say the words to either of them twice, and there are two hundred and forty people in that ground and he got to eleven of them before the light went.

Eleven out of two hundred and forty, on the first day, in nine hours, and he had known it would be eleven before he started, and the only thing he had that was new was the fact that eleven people had heard it in his own words from four different mouths and none of the four had changed it, and a thing four people have said the same way is not a signal and it is not a route either. It is four sentences.

He went back up the bank and made the four bolts fast where the frame was standing on the flat of beaten earth, and the water was four inches over the highest iron and holding, and Abel Trope was on the boards with his launch on a painter and had been on them since the seventh hour and had not gone up the reach at all.

At about the ninth hour of the evening he was on the flat with the barrow behind him and the four bolts made fast, and nine hundred yards below him and a step and a passage off, a woman of about forty people was going to be told nothing at all about what to decide and was not going to be told by anybody, and there was a boy of thirteen asleep in a house in the low quarter with a sentence in his head and no idea that he had done a day's work for nothing.
