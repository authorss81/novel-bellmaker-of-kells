# Chapter 0171 — The Timber Yard Above The Bridge

It was the fourth hour of the hundred and fifty-seventh day and Tomas Vale made the four bolts fast on the bank above the road where they had slept, and the flat was hard with last night's cold and the barrow sat level on it, and below them the road went back three miles to the bridge.

The frame has not been in a building since the thirty-third and it is a hundred and twenty-four days, and a bank and a flat and a road are not buildings.

---

At about the fifth hour they walked. Pell Okell had his barrow up first and went down the slope sideways the way a man does with a load he knows, and Mab Sarter went after with her basket held off the wet grass, and Isa Trevail took the yoke across her shoulders and settled the lime sacks with a lift of her knees, and Ned Adley carried the shovel across his shoulder like a man carrying a ladder.

Tomas walked with his own barrow and watched the wheels. The road here was old stone with grass between, and the ruts held water from the night.

At the sixth hour Pell stopped where the road bent and looked back up it.

"Three miles back," he said. "You want to settle it before we get too far."

Tomas set the barrow down. "Say it."

Pell counted on his fingers, loads and not reasons. "Two stringers. Twelve foot each. Deck boards, fourteen of them if you lay them close. That is what is missing. Timber cut nine years and left in Coram's yard past the turning."

"Have you asked him," Tomas said.

"Asked him last year for a cart side. He laughed. Said he had forgot he had it and now he remembers and it is worth double." Pell spat to one side. "He will not sell one board. Wants the lot gone together or none. No cart here can pay for the lot."

Isa shifted the yoke. "I went to the yard," she said. Her voice was flat, naming weight. "Lime dust gets in your throat and you learn to say short. He showed me the stack. Grey on top. Sound under. He said take all or leave all. I left all. Eleven times round the bridge is cheaper than his price."

Mab had stopped counting and was looking at the road. "Two hundred and eleven," she said. "That is this stretch. Not finished."

Ned put the shovel down and looked at the water in the rut. "Soft under," he said. "Lip at the edge you could catch a heel on. That is what I mend. Wood I do not mend."

Tomas looked at the four of them. "Then it stands," he said. "Boarded. Walked round. Nobody buys the timber because no single man can pay what the man asks for the lot, and no district will buy what it cannot put a line against."

Pell nodded, once, the way a carter ends a count. "It stands."

They walked on. Nobody wrote anything. There was no book between them for a bridge, and Tomas had said on the flat the day before that he would keep no terms, and he kept that by saying nothing more.

---

At about the ninth hour of the evening he was on a flat of beaten earth beside a dry ditch with the barrow behind him and the four bolts made fast, and she was on the flat about nine feet off his left shoulder with her back to a fence post, her boots on stone.

> *The hundred and fifty-seventh, at about the ninth hour of the evening. A bank, three miles of old road walked, a ditch, a fence post, a flat.*
>
> *Second column. Two stringers twelve foot each and fourteen deck boards, grey on top and sound under, in a yard past the turning. The man wants the lot gone together. No cart can pay for the lot and no district will buy timber it cannot set against a new line. So the boards stay at three and the road goes round.*
>
> *Third column, ruled apart. The day's ruling: a price can shut a bridge as surely as a board, and a man who will not divide his stack is not wicked for it.*
>
> *All five clauses stand. Nothing rung, by me or by her, and nothing bound, and nothing counted into iron, and no stage, and no perception attempted and none had, and the warrant stands. Four bolts made fast, and the frame has not been in a building since the thirty-third. The carrier in the sack, not rung, eighty days from the seventy-seventh, and I have not put my hand on it and the hand was finished on the hundred and twenty-seventh. My own bell is in a sealed yard in Kells and the bar is on the bell and not on the licence, and it does not travel. Her bell is in her bag and it is not rung today and nobody asked her for a reason at any hour, including me.*
>
> *The register is thirteen. I have produced nothing and asked for nothing and been given nothing and taken nothing, and nobody was thanked today.*
>
> *Which office is it. It is a yard with nine-year-old timber in it, a bridge with three boards, and a road walked round.*
