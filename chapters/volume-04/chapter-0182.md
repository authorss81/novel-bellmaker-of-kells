# Chapter 0182 — The Bench Beam and the Set of Foundry Weights

It was the fourth hour of the hundred and sixty-eighth day and Tomas Vale made the four bolts fast in a bench yard behind a low building with a stone step and a long table inside it and a swept threshold, and the sweep was fresh, and a bench yard where nobody has swept since the last bench that sat is a bench yard that has not been used, and this one had been used in the last two days.

The frame in its cradle sat on the yard's flagstones. It has not been in a building since the thirty-third, and a yard is not a building, and it is a hundred and thirty-five days.

---

Pell Okell was sitting on the low wall with a heel of bread and the yoke across his knees and the sacking already gone up the road, having gone on at dusk the night before with a load for a man at a pitch above the lock, slept in a barn there, and come down again at the second hour with nothing to carry and nothing owed, which is a carter's good morning and a bad one at the same time.

"He had already done it himself," Pell said, before anything was asked. "The hops. He had it half down on the cart when I got there and he did not say anything about it and he did not need to. So I have had a night in a barn and no load, and I am here at the fourth hour because there is a woman at the second bench who wants a barrow at the sixth and I am not going to be late to that either."

"That is a thing you do often."

"That is a thing I do every day of my life and I have never once been thanked for it in nine years." He tore the bread. "You have not asked me a thing since the bridge except whether I was busy, and you are not going to."

"No."

"Then that is two bridges." He put the heel of the bread in his pocket. "Go and see Merrit. He has been in there with the beam since the second hour, and if you want anybody in this building to look at a piece of paper without being asked first, that is the one. I am not telling him you are coming, because I have not asked him anything and I do not intend to."

---

At about the fifth hour Merrit Sallow had the beam out in the middle of the bench room with both pans down and his weight-box open on the table beside it, and he was a wide man with a habit of pushing his thumbs in his belt when he was thinking, and he was thinking.

"This is a bench beam," he said, without greeting. "It weighs carts. The rate is on that wall in a frame and the rate is set by weight, and a cart over the nine at nine stone pays one thing and a cart over the nine at eleven pays another, and a cart over thirteen pays the same as eleven because the bridge was built for eleven, and above that we are asking it a question it cannot answer."

"And you are out on the second of the month with a book of weights that is not a bench book."

"That book is a foundry book." He did not open it. "Those are foundry weights. They came out of the pit at the upper bench when the foundry shut, and the upper bench has had them in a press ever since, and there are eleven of them and four are duplicates of bench weights and seven are not anything else on this river. The last man who poured anything out of that pit is dead. I have never seen him. I know what he weighed because I have carried the weights."

Nobody in the room said anything for a moment. There were four other men in there and two of them were eating and one was asleep sitting up.

"Nine years ago I asked this bench to have those weights entered in the bench's own book," Merrit said. "The clerk said the bench book is the rate book and the rate book is for rates. He was right. So the weights are in a press in a shed and everybody at nine benches knows where they came from and no man at nine benches has ever put it in a book, and that is not a scandal and I am not asking anybody to make it one. I am telling you because in about four days you will be a man who has been told a thing, and a stranger costs less than a neighbour."

"It is not fair, it is arithmetic," he said, before the answer came. "Now. What do you want."

"I want to put a paper on your table."

"You can put it on the table." He moved the weight-box two inches to the left and did not offer to help and did not stop Tomas doing it, which is the correct thing to do to a man with a barrow. "I am not going to read it, and I want that said out loud in the room, because there are four men in here and one of them will tell somebody by tomorrow that a man with a barrow put a district's paper on the bench table and had it read, and I would rather the room heard it from me. I cannot read a road. I have never had a reason to."

"I have not asked anybody to read anything."

"Then you are doing something nobody has ever done on this river and I would think about it a while." He turned round. "Sarai, get out. Take your bread. You are all out, and tell Ferry I want the whole of the four weight-boxes on the wall by the sixth hour and not one of them on the floor, and tell him I do not care what the last one did."

They went. The sleeper went with them, awake.

---

At about the sixth hour there were four carts on the bridge outside and none of them moving, and Merrit Sallow put the corner of the sheet in his left pan and a sheet of his own out of a drawer in his own pan and balanced them against each other with two of the small weights in a dish.

He put the little weights in. Then he took them out and put three back and one out, and then he took the sheet off the pan, turned it over, and put the other corner in.

"You may watch and you may not talk," he said. "There are four carts outside and I am about to make it worse and I want to do it once."

"A district sheet is a quarter of a pound," he said. "That is what a store buys it at and what it sells it at, and it is the weight of every sheet of that paper a district has ever had. This one is heavier than a quarter by about a ninth of a hundredth, which is about nine sheets in a hundred, and a sheet gets heavier when it is wet and heavier again when it is dry out, and this one has been wet and dried a great many times and has not come back to the quarter."

"Which means," he said, "that a sheet folded and opened nine hundred times ought to be a hair lighter than a sheet folded and opened nine times, because paper loses a little at every crease and loses the most at the crease it is put in hardest. This one is not lighter. I cannot tell you why, and neither can the store, and the store does not care, and I have just held a bridge up for a third of an hour to find that out about a piece of paper I was never going to look at."

He put the sheet down in the middle of the table and squared it with one finger, which is a thing a man does to a paper he has just been surprised by.

"That corner there," he said. "Third crease along. It is heavier than the rest of it by more than a corner's worth. I put it on the pan and I felt it. I have been weighing sheets for twenty-two years and I know what the corner of one feels like, and that corner is not the weight of the corner of a sheet that has been folded."

Outside, the four carts began to move, one at a time, and the beam went back on its post and the weight-box shut, and Merrit Sallow wrote a figure in the rate book, because the rate book was open and the beam had been out and that is all a rate book is for.

"I am not telling you what is under that crease," he said. "I have got twenty-two years of a thing in my hands and I am telling you it is heavier than a crease is, and I am not going further, because I do not know, and I have never once in my life put in a book a thing I did not know."

"There is a man about four days back who looked at a sheet flat on a kerbstone in the open air for about nine seconds," Tomas said, "and told me the two blacks either side of the rectangle are one ink that has been wet and dried, and that a man who has not handled ink in a hundred and fifty days sees two. He is right. It is the same black. It has not been overturned today and it is not overturned by what you have done, and I would have told you that before you started."

"Then that is a smaller finding than the one I have in my hands a moment ago and I would rather have the smaller one." He squared the sheet again. "Nobody has ever put anything on this table. I am going to have to tell the clerk I did it and he is going to have to write in the book that I did it, and I am going to have to be asked why, and I am not going to have an answer that is about nine hundred times as long as the reason."

"Then say you weighed it."

"I am not going to say that, because if I say I weighed it they will think I weighed paper, and I did not, I weighed a crease." He stood up. "You will not be thanked for this. You have taken a third of an hour off a bridge that nine carts a day cross, and not one of those nine carts will know it was you, and one will be behind a carter who will say the beam was out, and that will be me, and I will be the man who weighed a paper in the middle of a working morning, in a bench, for the rest of my life."

---

At about the seventh hour Tomas walked the nine hundred yards down to the lock ground with the sheet back in the inside pocket of his coat, and he had put it on two tables in two days and had been told nothing about the capital, nothing about the plate, nothing about the district, and two true things about paper.

What he had was none of it about the sheet. A carter who was once on a beam. A gate that had already been loaded. A bench room swept in the last two days. A press of weights in a shed. A book that is for rates. A corner heavier than a crease. And a beam held up for a third of an hour by a man who was not going to be thanked for it by anybody including him. He was thirty-two days on this road with her and he had not asked her once what the road was for and he was not going to.

He wrote the two questions down in his own book in the same words he had written them thirty-four days before, and put one line under them: that two men in two trades have now looked at it and neither has said a word about the rectangle, and that a thing two men in two trades did not remark on is either very plain or very much not a thing they can see, and that he does not know which, and that the two questions are narrower by one line and are no nearer an answer.

At about the ninth hour of the evening he was on a flat above the lock ground with the barrow behind him and the four bolts made fast, and there were two men on the lock house step and a third walking the top of the far wall, and a fourth was somewhere out of sight shouting about a sill, and they were all about nine years of age, which is a thing a man's arms tell you at a distance and is not a thing anybody says out loud.

> *The hundred and sixty-eighth, at about the ninth hour of the evening. A bench yard, a swept step, a bench room, a long table, a beam, a weight-box, four men eating, four carts on a bridge, a flat.*
>
> *Second column. Nine benches on this water and the rate set by weight, and a cart over thirteen pays the same as one over eleven because the bridge was built for eleven. Eleven foundry weights in a press in a shed at the upper bench since the pit shut, four duplicates and seven not anything else on the river, and the last man who poured out of that pit is dead and nobody living has seen him. **He asked nine years ago for them to be entered in the bench's own book and was told the bench book is the rate book and the rate book is for rates, and he was right, and that is the whole of a bench in one exchange.** Nine years of a thing nobody wrote down, told to a stranger, because a stranger costs less than a neighbour.*
>
> *Second column, the paper. **A district sheet is a quarter of a pound at the store. This one is heavier by about a ninth of a hundredth, and a sheet folded and opened nine hundred times ought to be a hair lighter and this one is not, and the corner at the third crease is heavier than the corner of a sheet is.** He weighed it in front of me with two small weights in a dish, turned it over, put the other corner in, and said he has been weighing sheets for twenty-two years and knows what the corner of one feels like. He said the store cannot tell him why and does not care, and that he is not going to tell me what is under that crease because he does not know. **He held a bridge up for a third of an hour to find that out, and four carts stood there, and he is going to have to tell his clerk and be asked why.**
>
> *Nobody in that building was thanked. Two tables in two days and I have not asked anybody to read anything, and I have been told two things about paper and nothing whatever about a district. The two questions are narrower by one line and no nearer an answer.*
>
> *Third column, ruled apart. **A bench is a rate, a beam, a press of weights nobody can enter, and a step somebody sweeps.** A carter is a gate he was not asked to. A weighmaster will hold up a thing he keeps for the sake of a thing he does not understand. **What comes off these tables is not a sentence and not a document: it is a fact about the man the table belongs to.** He is right and I am not going to be able to use him.*
>
> *All five clauses stand. **Nothing rung, by me or by her, and nothing bound, and nothing counted into iron, and no stage, and no perception attempted and none had, and the warrant stands, and a fitting is not called anything it is not.** Four bolts made fast, and **the frame has not been in a building since the thirty-third, a hundred and thirty-five days, and a bench yard is not a building; the sheet lay on the bench table for the length of a weight and came off in my own hands.** The carrier in the sack, **not rung, ninety-one days from the seventy-seventh, and I have not put my hand on it.** My own bell is in a sealed yard in Kells and the bar is on the bell and not on the licence, and it does not travel. **Her bell is in her bag and it is not rung and nobody asked her for a reason at any hour today, including me.**
>
> *The register is thirteen. **I have produced nothing and asked for nothing and been given nothing and taken nothing, and nobody was thanked today.**
>
> *Which office is it. **A beam, a rate, a press of weights in a shed, a book that is for rates, and a man who will not put a thing in a book he does not know and will not be thanked for holding a bridge up for a piece of paper.**
