# Chapter 0179 — The Fifth Walker

It was the fourth hour of the hundred and sixty-fifth day and Tomas Vale made the four bolts fast on a hollow of turf beside a ditch gang's heap, and the gang were cutting a new run through clay and stacking the spoil neat.

The frame has not been in a building since the thirty-third and it is a hundred and thirty-two days, and turf and a spoil heap are not buildings.

---

At about the fifth hour a young woman of the ditch gang straightened from the cut, stuck her spade in the clay, and looked at the two barrows.

"You are the lime," she said to Isa. "You went round the bridge eleven times."

"Twelve now," Isa said. "And will go round till it is open."

The woman pulled the spade out and cut another square. Her hands were quick, naming nothing, doing the work. "I am Corrie Vane," she said. "I cut ditch by the foot and I am paid by it. Feet can be counted. That is the whole of my book."

At about the sixth hour they walked together to the end of the cut, and she showed Ned how the clay broke, short and clean, and he nodded.

"Good cutting," he said. "Edge true. That will hold water."

"It holds if nobody walks the lip," she said. "Lip you could catch a heel on if you are careless. So do not be careless."

Pell counted the heap the way he counted loads. "Twenty feet cut since morning. That is wages and food for twenty-five. Good rate."

Mab looked at the cut and then at the field beyond. "No fences," she said. "Ditch instead. I have two hundred and forty-one of ditch feet in my head now and I have not finished."

Tomas said nothing to her about joining or walking. He had not asked any of the four for anything and he would not ask a fifth. He told her what he told everyone, once, in the ordinary voice, because it was true and not because it was an offer.

"I go up the road with a barrow I cannot pay from and a paper I cannot show. No standing. No thanks either way."

Corrie put her spade on her shoulder. "Then I walk a day with you to the ferry," she said. "Not for you. The ditch here is cut and the next cut is past the ferry and I have to walk there anyway. I walk with you because the road is there."

Nobody thanked her. She fell in behind Isa, and there were five behind and beside the barrow besides the woman nine feet off, and he was not the clerk of them and kept no terms.

---

At about the ninth hour of the evening he was on turf beyond the heap with the barrow behind him and the four bolts made fast.

> *The hundred and sixty-fifth, at about the ninth hour of the evening. Turf, clay, a cut, a heap stacked neat, a flat.*
>
> *Second column. Twenty feet cut since morning for twenty-five mouths, paid by the foot. A fifth walker joining for a day to the ferry because the next cut lies there, not asked and not thanked.*
>
> *Third column, ruled apart. Ruling: a fifth pair of feet is still not a company, and walking the same ditch is not agreeing.*
>
> *All five clauses stand. Nothing rung, by me or by her, and nothing bound, and nothing counted into iron, and no stage, and no perception attempted and none had, and the warrant stands. Four bolts made fast, and the frame has not been in a building since the thirty-third. The carrier in the sack, not rung, eighty-eight days from the seventy-seventh, and I have not put my hand on it and the hand was finished on the hundred and twenty-seventh. My own bell is in a sealed yard in Kells and the bar is on the bell and not on the licence, and it does not travel. Her bell is in her bag and it is not rung today and nobody asked her for a reason at any hour, including me.*
>
> *The register is thirteen. I have produced nothing and asked for nothing and been given nothing and taken nothing, and nobody was thanked today.*
>
> *Which office is it. It is clay cut by the foot, a lip to mind, and five walkers who are not a company.*
