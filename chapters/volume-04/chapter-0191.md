# Chapter 0191 — Abel Trope Kept a Mark on a Post Above Four Days of Water

It was the fourth hour of the hundred and seventy-seventh day and Abel Trope was standing in nine inches of river at the edge of a flat of beaten earth with a stick in his hand, and he was doing it the way a man does a thing that has no name for what he is doing, which is once every hour and then again at a different hour, and Tomas Vale came up the flat behind the barrow and stopped nine feet off and did not come closer, because a man standing in a river with a stick is a man whose work is in his hands and there is no room in a man's hands for a stranger.

The stick was a length of ash five feet with the bark off and a nail in the top of it, and Trope had been putting it in and taking it out at the same place on the flat for a number of years that nobody on that flat had counted, and the post beside him was an oak post four foot out of the ground with three iron marks let into it at different heights, the lowest of them nearly rusted through, and beside the post there was a board on two stakes with notches cut in the top edge of it, and the notches were a day apiece.

"Four," Trope said, before he had been asked anything. "Four days I have cut one, and I should have cut one a day since the water came up, and the reason I have not is that I do not believe a thing until the water is on it, and the day before yesterday it was not on it, and I was right the day before yesterday, and here we are."

He seemed to hear himself say it, and put his thumb on the board over the four notches, and did not cut a fifth, which was a thing a man could have done in four seconds and chose not to.

Tomas made the four bolts fast on the flat, where the frame sat on a level place two paces wide, and the barrow stood on beaten earth with the water nine foot below the edge of it. It is a hundred and forty-four days since the frame has been in a building. Beaten earth is not a building, and a post with three iron marks let into it is not a building, and there is nothing in a barrow's cradle that can strike.

The river came up out of the west along four hundred yards of the flat and did not stop anywhere on it that Trope could see, and at the far end it went under the boards of a landing stage on legs, and the boards were two foot under and the legs went down nine foot to mud.

---

At about the fifth hour the two of them were on the boards and Trope was pointing with the stick, which is what the stick was for.

"That is the fourth one," he said. "The high one. The iron that is left." He put the stick in and took it out. "It is the water from two years back in the wet of the year before last, and the year before that it stood about a hand above that one, and I have not cut those because the iron is gone and a mark that is gone is not a mark, it is a hole with a nail in it."

"Two years back is not much."

"It is two years back for nine foot of a river, and this river has done nine foot in four days, and you are standing on a flat that is nine foot above a town." He said it without any weight on it, the way a man says what a stone weighs. "I have had that post nine years and I have never once had anybody ask me for it, and you have asked me for it in the time it takes to look at a post."

"You have never had anybody ask you for it," Tomas said. "You have never told anybody what it is."

"I have told four people in nine years, and two of them were asking about something else." He shifted his grip on the stick. "I will tell you what it is for and then you can do what you like with it, and I have never said this out loud. It is for a boat. A man who brings a flat-bottomed thing up this reach with nine foot of freeboard on him needs to know at the top of the flat whether he can come on. That is all it is. I do not know what the Crown calls it. There is no form. I asked once, at the bench, whether the mark goes on a sheet, and the man said the sheet is for rates and the mark is not a rate, and he was right, and I have not asked again in nine years."

"Then it is not on the sheet."

"Nothing on that sheet is about a river," Trope said. "That is not a complaint. A rate is about carts and a reach is about carts and the reach has had a name on a sheet for two hundred years, and the water in it has never had anything, and that is correct, because a river does not want paying for and a rate does. I have got a board on two stakes with a notch a day on it and I have got a stick, and if I am not here on the hour then there is no mark for that hour, and nobody in this town has ever once sent anybody to see whether I came."

"What is the town."

"Hallowmere. Six hundred of it by the count that is not a count, and two hundred and forty of them are down below the bank, and the bank is that." Trope put the stick under his arm and pointed with his chin at a line four hundred yards off where the flat gave up and there was a bank with grass on it, nine or ten foot high, and a set of stone steps going down it, and below the steps a wet grey roofscape with nine chimneys in it and a thread of smoke off one. "You can see the low quarter from here. You cannot see the yard behind the steps from here and neither can anybody."

"You have been up the reach since I came up it," Tomas said, and did not make it a question, and Trope looked at him.

"Nine days. I am at the top of it for an hour and then I am here. There is a launch at the seventh hour at the upper landing and I am the only one aboard who can get her up over the flat, and if the water is where it is at the sixth hour I am not going to get her up, and the man at the upper landing will write a line about a launch that did not come." He turned the stick over in his hands. "That is my own and not your business, and I am telling you so that you know I am not standing here for nothing."

---

At about the sixth hour Tomas Vale walked down off the flat to the bank and stood on the top of the steps, and a schedule folded in three had been in his inside pocket for forty days and he had asked about this reach twice in nine days and been told the same thing both times, which is that the reach is on the sheet and nothing else about a river is.

The water at the foot of the steps was about a foot and a half down the stair and the bottom four steps were under. The low quarter from the top of them was not nine hundred houses. It was a low, close, wet place of two hundred and forty people on ground the same height as the flat he had come off, and the bank between them was the only thing in nine miles that was nine foot high, and it was a natural bank with grass on it and nothing on it of any kind, and a natural bank nine foot high that a river has gone round for two hundred years is a thing a man stops noticing the way he stops noticing a wall.

He took the schedule out and unfolded it along the three creases it already had and held it flat against the top step with both hands, and the wind came up off the water and tried to take it, and he put his knee on the corner.

There is no box on that sheet for a town. There is a panel for a reach with a rate in it, and there is a column that runs the length of the reach, and about a third of the way along a column the rules stop short and do not cross a rectangle two hands together in which there is nothing at all, not torn, not cut, the paper smooth and uncreased, the numbers either side of it going on without a break.

He had carried that rectangle for forty days. He could see wards and numbers and a scale. He could not read a run and he was never going to, and the reason for that is that there is no form in this kingdom on which anybody is taught, and a man in a coat had not been asked to teach him and there was not one to ask.

He could not tell whether the town at the foot of the steps was off that sheet because it was not owed anything, or because the sheet had never had a column for a town. One of those is a finding and the other one is a hole in a piece of paper, and he had no instrument in the world that told the two apart.

He folded the sheet in three along the creases it already had, and the second crease did not want to take, the way it had not wanted to take in a market of about two thousand days ago, and he put it back in the inside pocket of his coat, and he stood on the top of the steps with a barrow above him and did not write anything down for four minutes.

Then he went back up.

---

At about the seventh hour Abel Trope was on the boards two foot under, standing in the flat-bottomed thing with a pole, and Tomas stood at the edge of the flat and watched and did not go down, because the launch was his own and there was a freeboard question in it and no room in the world for a man with a barrow.

The mark was on the iron. The stick went in and came out level with the line of the rust.

"That is the first time in nine years it has been on that one," Trope said, without moving the stick. "That is not a thing I am going to write on a board, because a board with one notch on it is a board with a day on it, and a day is a number, and a number is the beginning of a thing somebody asks me about. I have told you where it is and that is me telling you, and I would be obliged if you did not come back and ask me a second time, because the second time it comes out of my mouth it will be the same and I will not know whether it is."

Tomas said he would not come back and ask him a second time, and that was true, and it was the only promise he made to anybody in six days, and it cost him a question he had been holding since the sixth hour.

Trope got the launch up over the flat with a pole and a great deal of swearing, and the water came off the boards, and he did not look back at the man on the bank.

At about the ninth hour of the evening Tomas Vale was on the flat of beaten earth with the barrow behind him and the four bolts made fast, and the water was nine foot below the edge of it and going up, and a man was not on a launch and had gone up the reach on foot, and two hundred and forty people were under a bank four hundred yards off with about a foot and a half of water at the foot of their steps, and a yard behind those steps that nobody on that flat could see.

> *The hundred and seventy-seventh, at about the ninth hour of the evening. A flat of beaten earth, an oak post with three iron marks, a board on two stakes, four notches, a stick, a landing stage with two foot under, a bank with grass on it, nine stone steps, a low quarter, nine chimneys, one thread of smoke, a man on a launch, a barrow, four bolts.*

> *Second column. **Up four days and I have cut four notches and I should have cut one a day.** I do not believe a thing until the water is on it, and the day before yesterday I was right, and here we are. **A mark is for a boat. There is no form for it. The one time I asked anybody, nine years ago, the answer was that the sheet is for rates and a mark is not a rate, and the man was right.** I have told four people in nine years and two of them were asking about something else.
>
> *Second column, the town. Hallowmere, about six hundred, two hundred and forty below the bank, and the bank is nine or ten foot of grass with stone steps on it and nothing on it of any kind. **The high mark is where the water was two years back, this river has done nine foot in four days, and there is a yard behind those steps that cannot be seen from the flat or from the bank.** A launch at the seventh hour at the upper landing that only I can get over the flat, and the man there will write a line about a launch that did not come. That is my own and not your business.
>
> *Third column, ruled apart. **A day is the beginning of a thing somebody asks me about, and I am not going to give a day to a mark kept in pencil for nine years.** Two things I cannot tell apart, and one of them is the whole of what I came for: a place not on a sheet, and a sheet with nothing to put that place in. **I have nothing in the world that tells those two apart.** Four minutes on the top of those steps and nothing written down, and I came up that bank with one number in me and it is his number and not mine.
>
> *All five clauses stand. **Nothing rung, by me or by her, and nothing bound, and nothing counted into iron, and no stage, and no perception attempted and none had, and the warrant stands, and a fitting is not called anything it is not.** Four bolts made fast, and **the frame has not been in a building since the thirty-third, a hundred and forty-four days, and a flat of beaten earth and a post with three iron marks in it are not a building, and I made the bolts fast on a place two paces wide.** The carrier in the sack, **not rung, a hundred days from the seventy-seventh.** My own bell is in a sealed yard in Kells and the bar is on the bell and not on the licence, and it does not travel. **Her bell is in her bag and nobody asked her for a reason at any hour today, including me.**
>
> *The register is thirteen. **I have produced nothing and asked for nothing and been given nothing and taken nothing, and nobody was thanked today. A man told me where his water is in four words after nine years of not being asked, and I promised him I would not ask twice, which is the only promise I have made anybody in six days and it has cost me a question.** I was on this road with her forty-one days today and I did not ask her what it was for, and I am not going to.
>
> *Which office is it. **A post with three iron marks in it, a board on two stakes with a notch a day, a stick, and a man who has told four people in nine years.**
