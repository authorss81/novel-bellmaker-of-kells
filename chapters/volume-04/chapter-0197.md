# Chapter 0197 — A Sheet With No Day on It and the Second Half of a Sentence

It was the fourth hour of the hundred and eighty-third day and Averill Crome was working a stone store, which has one door and the door is at the wrong end of the shed, and Tomas Vale came up the yard behind the barrow and put it down nine feet off it.

"You are the one off the flat."

"I am."

"You have got nine people out of two hundred and forty who have said the four words to somebody else, and I have counted them, and I am not going to tell you I have not counted them." Crome set the bar down. "There is water going to come over that bank and the district has a man who stands where the water is, and I am that man, and I have been it for about eleven years, and I will be at the top of those steps at about the sixth hour."

"You have counted nine."

"I counted them in a district's book, in the margin, in pencil, because there is a column for a date and a column for a ward and there is nothing in it for a man off a flat saying four words. That is not a thing I am supposed to do. I have told my clerk and he said nothing, which is a thing he is not supposed to do." It came out in one breath and it was a thing he had been carrying since the second hour. "You have come to ask whether you may say it again this evening. You may say it. I have looked twice for a form on which a man may not say a number on a bank in a county, and I did not like the looking."

"I was going to ask you the other thing."

"Then say it."

"There is a sheet," Crome said, and did not go into the shed for it. He took it out of the inside of his coat and held it out by the top edge with two fingers, the way a man holds a thing that is not his. "It is not the warrant. It has your name at the top of it and a word I am not going to say because you did not ask for it, and it is what a district writes when a man with no standing has addressed a reach, and I did not write it and I have to file it."

"How many boxes is it."

"Four. Ward, day, number, ground, and the day box is empty, and I have looked at that for about a quarter of an hour." He turned it about so Tomas could see the top of it. "A day is a thing you can wait out. You have a day on a thing and the line under it is the end of it. There is no day on this, so there is nothing to wait out, and I do not know what the end of it is, and neither do you, and neither does the man who wrote it. **That is what makes it worse than a hearing, and I am telling you so from the man who is filing it, because the man who wrote it does not know either.**"

"You have to file it."

"I have to file it, and I am sorry in the ordinary way, and a man's being sorry is not a box and there is no form on which it goes in. I am a district's man. I sell stone. I have counted your nine in pencil in a margin, and I have read you a sheet, and I am going to file it, and none of those three is a kindness and all three are what I am."

He put it back in his coat.

"I was going to say what people ought to do," Tomas said, "and I am not going to, and I wanted to know before this evening whether I am allowed to, so that if a man stops me I can tell whether he is right or he is being cruel."

Crome took his time. He walked nine paces to the door and stood in it with his hand on the frame.

"You are allowed to say the water. Nobody can stop a man saying a number about a river, and I would not try, and there is nowhere to put it in anyway. You are not allowed to say what a person should do about it, and I will tell you why, and it is not because of anything I have been told." He came back. "**If a man with a barrow stands on a bank in front of forty people and says the ones behind these steps should go up the bank, then nine do it because a stranger told them to and four do it because they decided to on their own account, and in a fortnight a man at the district will ask me which of those two happened, and I will not be able to tell him, and neither will you, and there is no form on which either of us can write down that we do not know. I would then be the man who stood on a bank and caused a thing, and you would be the man who told forty people to go up a bank on a word and cannot say where the word came from.** That is the whole of it and I have thought about it since the second hour and I am right and I would be right if you were the best man in this county."

"Then I will not."

"You were never going to. You have watched four people say three different things about the same number this week and none of them was lying." He sat down on a pallet. "Say the number and get off the bank and be somewhere else."

---

At about the sixth hour there were forty people on the top of the steps, and the water was on the grass two hundred yards down, coming off it the way a thing comes off when it is not in a hurry.

Tomas Vale said the words, in the order he always said them, to forty people on a bank in the open. About nine said the four words back to him and the rest listened and said nothing, and four at the back started to go up the bank while he was still in the middle of the fourth one.

And then he said the fifth thing in the middle of a sentence, and the sentence was this.

"And the ones in the yard behind the fourth house, if anybody in that yard is standing within nine foot of the low corner of it, they should —"

A man in a coat with a district's satchel said, loudly, in front of forty people, "You have got no standing in this county and you cannot say what a person should do."

It was not a shout and it was not unkind. It was said at the volume a man uses when he wants the nine people in front of him to hear it as well as the one he is talking to, and Tomas Vale heard his own sentence stop, and it stopped because the man was right, and it stopped in his mouth.

He did not finish it. The five words he had said about the low corner of a yard that thirty people had never been told anything about were in the air and were not an order, because they had no end on them. The thirty people in that yard were two hundred yards off and behind nine buildings and a passage and three foot of moving water, and they were not going to be told anything by anybody at any hour on this day or any day after it, and he had been about to put four inches of an order on the end of a true sentence, and a man he had known for about an hour had stopped him from doing it in front of forty people, and the man would have been right to stop a better man.

---

At about the seventh hour the water was down off the grass and in the rooms where it had been the night before, and Tomas Vale walked the low quarter and did the only accounting available to a man with no standing, which is to look.

Nine houses of the front row were wet. Nine had been wet the day before. A house at the fourth door had the words in it, said twice, once by a carter at the top of the steps on the day before yesterday and once by him at the fourth hour on the day before that, and it was under a foot and a half and the woman in it was on the second floor with a neighbour's dog and a neighbour who had gone up the steps at the seventh hour and not come back for her.

A house at the eleventh door had not been warned by anybody, and the man in it had gone up the bank on his own at the fourth hour of the day before yesterday because he had had a wet spring nine years ago and knew what a river did, and his house is dry, and he is the reason nine foot of that bank did not come down at all.

A house at the sixth door had been warned, by a woman over a wall about a foot high with no gate in it, and it was dry, and the man in it was in a chair by his own fire and had not moved, and in about a year somebody will ask him and there is no form on which he can say what he was told, and he will say it wrong.

He could account for which of the two hundred and forty people were wet. He could not account for why, and neither could the man with the satchel, and both said so in the same words about a quarter of an hour apart, and neither thanked the other and neither blamed the other, and they were the only two people in Hallowmere with any idea at all and they were not on the same side of a question.

At about the ninth hour of the evening he was on the flat with the barrow behind him and the four bolts made fast, and the water had stopped coming down two hours before, and the plain carrier in its sack was against the cradle where it had been since the fifty-second. There were two hundred yards of open bank between here and the top of a flight of steps, and a thing four foot long would have done that whole quarter in nine seconds, and the second clause of a receipt in five clauses, all five standing, is the reason he cannot pay for anything with the barrow. There is no clause in it that lets a man get a bell out of a sack and stand on a bank for an evening, and he was not going to, and he wrote that down in the ordinary way and not as a noble thing.

He wrote the sentence down as far as it went, in his own hand in his own book, and the last two words on the page are *they should*, and then a line, and under the line the word *stopped*, and under that: *Out loud, in front of about forty people, and he was right, and I would have been stopped by him or by a man in a coat with no satchel at all. Five words with no end on them are not an order. I was not allowed to say the rest and I am not going to write it down, because a sentence in a book is a sentence somebody can read in forty years and act on, and I did not have the standing to make it true then and I have not got it now.*

> *The hundred and eighty-third, at about the ninth hour of the evening. A stone store, one door at the wrong end, a pencil figure in a margin, four boxes and an empty day box, a bank, nine saying the four words back, a satchel, a sill, a second floor, a neighbour's dog, a chair by a fire, four inches, a sack, a cradle, nine seconds, four bolts.*
>
> *Second column. **Nine counted in a district's book in the margin in pencil, which is not a thing I am supposed to do, and the clerk has been told and said nothing, which is a thing he is not supposed to do either.** A man may say a number about a river on a bank in this county and there is nowhere to put it in anyway.*
>
> *Second column, the sheet. **A district's sheet with my name at the top of it and four boxes, and the box for the day left empty, which is a thing I have not stopped looking at since the fourth hour.** A day is the end of a thing when it is on it. This has none, so it has no end that anybody in this county can name, including the man who wrote it. It is worse than a hearing, and I am telling him so from the man who is filing it, and filing it is the whole of what I am.
>
> *Second column, the sentence. **About forty people, nine of them saying the four words back, four going up the bank before I reached the last one, and then the fifth thing, said in the middle of a sentence, about the low corner of a yard that thirty people have never been told anything about.** A man in a coat with a district's satchel stopped it out loud in front of all of them, and what he said was that I have got no standing in this county and cannot say what a person should do. Not a shout and not unkind, and said so that the people in front of me heard it as well as the one he was talking to. **I did not finish it, and it stopped because he was right, and the man would have been right to stop a better man.**
>
> *Third column, ruled apart. **I can account for which of the two hundred and forty are wet and I cannot account for why.** The fourth door had the words in it twice and is under a foot and a half, and the woman is on the second floor with a neighbour's dog. The eleventh door was never warned by anybody, and the man there went up the bank on his own account because of a wet spring nine years ago, and that is why nine foot of that bank did not come down at all. The sixth door was warned over a wall a foot high with no gate in it, and the man in it has not moved from a chair by his own fire, and in about a year somebody will ask him what he was told and he will say it wrong, and I will not be told which way. **A man with a satchel said the same words about a quarter of an hour apart, and we are not on the same side of a question.**
>
> *All five clauses stand. **Nothing rung, by me or by her, and nothing bound, and nothing counted into iron, and no stage, and no perception attempted and none had, and the warrant stands, and a fitting is not called anything it is not.** Four bolts made fast, and **the frame has not been in a building since the thirty-third, a hundred and fifty days, and a stone store is a building and I stood in its yard and did not go in.** The carrier in the sack, **not rung, a hundred and six days from the seventy-seventh, and a thing four foot long and two hundred yards of open bank would have done that quarter in nine seconds, and there is no clause that lets a man take a bell out of a sack and be a keeper of a bank for one evening.** My own bell is in a sealed yard in Kells and the bar is on the bell and not on the licence, and it does not travel. **Her bell is in her bag and it is not rung and nobody asked her for a reason at any hour today, including me.**
>
> *The register is thirteen. **I have produced nothing and asked for nothing and been given nothing and taken nothing, and nobody was thanked today.** I was on this road with her forty-seven days today and did not ask her what it was for.
>
> *Which office is it. **A pencil figure in a margin, an empty day box, and four inches of a sentence a man was not allowed to finish and did not write down.**
