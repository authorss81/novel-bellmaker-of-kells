# Chapter 0183 — Isa Trevail Swapping Hands at a Stone

It was the fourth hour of the hundred and sixty-ninth day and Isa Trevail put the barrow of lime down against a stone at the edge of a widening in the bank, and swapped hands, and then swapped them back again, and the second time she swapped them she did it because she had decided to and not because it hurt.

"I have got four loads to take off this stand and I have been paid for three," she said. "The fourth is against that wall and the price on the fourth is the price on the third, and the standkeeper is a good man and he is going to pay me the fourth, and if I am not on that wall at the sixth hour I will not get it, and I will not be able to tell him why I did not get it, and he will not ask me why I did not get it, and that is the arrangement and it is the whole of my trade and it has been since I was old enough to lift a yoke."

The stand was six boards and a lean-to roof and an open front with a heap of burnt lime under a sacking cover held down with stones, and a scoop on a pole, and a basket that was not one basket but a size, and a man in a shirt sitting on an upturned pail who did not get up when they came up the bank because getting up would be a thing that had to be undone again.

Ned Adley came up behind them with the hod over his shoulder and put it down and looked at the ground where the carts turn to come off the bank, which is the ground the stand's barrows have been turning on for longer than the stand has been here.

"That is going," he said. "Not at the lip. Four foot in, on the inside of the turn, where the wheel comes round twice."

"You have done it once," Tomas said.

"I have done it once and I am not doing it twice, and I told you why and you did not argue, and here we are at the same place four days later." He picked the hod up again. "I have got to be at the cart bridge by the second hour tomorrow for a thing that will not wait and I am not telling anybody what it is, because it is a soft place I found and I found it and I am not going to be asked about a thing I found and did not put in a return."

"You do not have to tell me what it is."

"I know you do not have to. That is what I am telling you, at a standstill, in advance, so that it is said."

---

At about the fifth hour they were on the plank and the plank was over mud and the mud was a foot under it, and Isa loaded the first basket herself because the standkeeper's hands are for lime and not for lifting, and she set it on the barrow and the lime settled about a hand's depth because lime settles, and that is the one thing about burnt lime that a person learns in about nine seconds and never forgets.

She put her right hand under the yoke, and then took it out and put her left under it, and said nothing about that, and Tomas said nothing about that.

Tamsin Rook was about nine feet off at the water's edge with her bag on her shoulder and her boots on the stone kerb where the bank meets the water and there is a mooring post with an iron ring in it that has been in the water since before anybody. She had her hand on the post. She stayed there long enough that the man on the pail looked at her once, and then he looked at the heap.

Nine feet further along there was a step up to the stand's door frame, and at some point while Isa was on her second basket she was on the step instead, with her palm flat against the jamb and her boots on stone and her eyes on the scoop, watching the man lift a basket of lime and set it down and then lift it again and set it down a hand's width over, which is a thing a person does with lime and a thing another person can see through a door frame.

Nobody spoke to her. Tomas did not go over. He had been on this road with her thirty-three days and he had not asked her once what the road was for, and he was not going to, and he was not going to put a second question about a road either, because the one he had put in the last four days had been put and had been refused and had been walked past, and a man who puts a question twice is a man who wants the answer.

At about the sixth hour the first load was on the barrow and the second was being filled, and the standkeeper said, without getting up, that the lime at this stand came up the water and the lime at the upper stand was made nine days' walk further up and burned nine days longer, and that it was a different lime, and that there was a good deal of money in the difference and a great deal more than nine days in it, because nine days' burning is the whole of the difference between a lime that sets in a morning and a lime that sets in a week.

"And nobody can say which is which on a piece of paper," Isa said. "I have carried both. There is one column for it and no way to put which one in it, and I have never met a man who wanted to know, and if a man wanted to know I could not tell him, and the man at the bench rate would not ask me because a bench rate is a bench rate and it is for carts."

"You could tell him it was a different lime."

"I could tell him it was a different lime in about four seconds and he would put a mark against it in his own head, and his own head is a poor place for a mark that has to last forty years, and he knows that, and that is why he does not ask." She set the basket down. "That is the thing I have that you would want if you could use it. I can tell a thing apart from another thing in four seconds, out loud, to a man, and neither of us can put it anywhere, and there is no form in this kingdom that wants to know which lime came off which stand. If there were one, I would be rich and I would not be standing in a yard at the seventh hour waiting to be paid for a load I have already carried."

"You have not said that in order to be thanked for it."

"I have said it because I am carrying a barrow and I am going to be here for another hour, and a person talks."

---

At about the seventh hour the fourth basket was on the barrow and the price was paid, in coin, out of a leather bag on the pail, and Isa counted it and put it inside her coat and did not say how much it was. Then she hooked two more baskets onto the yoke and went up the bank, and the bank is a bad bank and a yoke of lime goes up a bad bank at a walk and not at anything else, and Tomas took the other end of the bar with his hands without saying anything about it, and they went up forty feet of bank in one go and set the baskets down on the flat, and he did not thank her and she did not expect it and neither of them mentioned it again.

Ned went down a third time, alone, with the hod, and came up with it half full and did not say what he had put in it and Tomas did not ask.

The two baskets were for a hearth in a house at the third bench, and the house had a man in it who had been to the stand before and knew what the price was, and Isa said she had two more loads to take that day and did not say where, and the man said take them and do not come back before the water turns, and she said she was not going to before the water turns anyway, and the man said he had not doubted it, and that was the whole of that transaction.

They were on the flat above the bank with the water about nine feet below them and the wind off it, and Tomas found out of the day, sitting on the barrow's shaft, the one thing he had wanted from it, which was that a lime stand has a heap and a scoop and a price and no book at all, and that the price is the price of that day, and that the day is the book, and that a thing measured once a day and sold once a day has never once in the history of the world wanted a ledger, and that there are at least nine benches on this water buying lime by the load from a shed with six boards in it and none of them knows what the other eight paid and none of them has ever asked.

That was not a sentence and it was not a document. It was a fact about a shed, and it was on the page, and it belonged to Isa, and she had put it on the page while carrying a barrow and had not been asked.

At about the ninth hour of the evening he was on the flat with the barrow behind him and the four bolts made fast, and she was nine feet off with her hand on a low stone and her boots on the hard edge of it, and he was thirty-three days on this road with her and had not asked her once.
