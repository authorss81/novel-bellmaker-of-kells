# Chapter 0153 — A Man With A Book And No Office Who Has Nine Years Of The Same Board

It was the fourth hour of the hundred and thirty-ninth day and Tomas Vale had the barrow on the flat at the edge of a market town on a crossroads, and the four bolts were made fast, and a board stood under a roof at the crossroads with about two hundred people at it, and nobody was shouting, which is what about two hundred people at a board on a market morning is.

The man at the board had a book.

**He had a book and no office and about nine shillings a quarter, and he had been at that board nine years, and I knew both of those things before I was thirty feet off him because a man who keeps a book at a board is a man whose whole trade is a book and not a counter and his face does the rest of it.**

He read out a notice about a culvert and a notice about a cart's weight and a notice about a meeting at a place with a name, and about two hundred people stood and listened, and one of them asked what a word meant, and the man at the board explained it, and the two hundred went.

His name was Row Kellow.

At about the fifth hour the board emptied and Tomas Vale was at the end of it.

“**You keep a book of what you read out.**”

“**I keep a book of what I read out.**”

“**How many.**”

“**About four hundred.**” He shut it and did not lock it. “**Nine years, about four hundred notices, and nine marginal notes that say I said it out loud at the board, and the marginal notes are mine and I put them in because of the last one.**”

“**You want to tell me about the last one.**”

“**No.**” He said it the way a man says a thing he has said. “**You have a barrow and no office behind you and you have about half a sentence of your own in your mouth already and I can hear it, and I am not going to help you say the rest of it, and that is not unkind and it is not a door. It is that a man with a barrow in a market town is a thing that happens about four times a year and in nine years three of them have come to me with a paper and every one of the three wanted to be the ninth of something.**”

**He is right. I have been the ninth of something four times in five counties and I know the shape of it from the inside and so does he, and he has not been thanked for the three and is not going to be thanked for this one, and he is not a door and he is not sorry.**

---

Then he told Tomas Vale something he had not asked for, which is the only thing anybody has done for him in six days and it is the reason the hundred and thirty-ninth is worth a page.

“**A bell on a post above a board is a notice.**” He said it to the empty road and not to Tomas. “**That is the whole of what it is. The sheet under it is not a copy of the sound, the sound is the thing and the sheet is what the sound was for, and the sound arrives before the man with the book, and I cannot get to two hundred doors in a day and a bell costs nothing to send, and I have said that out loud at this board twice in nine years and the second time a man wrote it down and I was not thanked then either.**”

“**You do not know who decides when it goes.**”

“**No.**”

“**I have said that to four people in four counties and a city and you are the fifth and the fifth one says it in the same words.**”

“**It is not the same words. It is the same shape and every one of us says it like it is ours.**”

---

At about the sixth hour he asked his one question. He asked it the way he asks, which is the same words in the same order, and he said plainly that he had no standing in this county and that he was asking anyway because there is no form on which a man may ask and be refused, only a form on which a man may ask and be answered.

“**Is there anybody in this kingdom who keeps a record of the ground between two points, and not of the two points.**”

The man at the board did not answer for about four seconds, and then he answered for about nine minutes, and there were nine answers in it and not one of them was the same.

The **district keeps a book of the wards and the days and the numbers** and no book of what is between them. **A carrier keeps a book and his book has a place and a day and how many miles** and a carrier's book is the oldest thing of the three and a carrier may not write three things in it, **which are the owner, a debt and a price,** and he said the three of them out loud in the order they go on the form without being asked what the order was for. A **wheelwright keeps a book of the roads his carts have been on** and it is not a record of the road, it is a record of his carts. A **poor-law man in a district four days east keeps a book of the people on a round.** A **bell-foundry** — and he stopped there, and said *there is one of those and I am not going to say where, and you have not asked me that and if you had asked me I would have said no.* A **surveyor with a plate** — and he said *a plate is not a map, a plate is what a map is cut from,* and would not go further. A **man who walks a road for a living and sells what he finds on it.** A **district surveyor who lays a new line when a road is to be made good**, and whose lines go on paper and not on the ground. And a **man in a town four days west who has a book of every bell in nine counties** — and he stopped again, and said *I have not seen that book and I am telling you it exists because a man came through here about two years ago and said so, and I did not ask him to prove it and I am not going to.*

Then he shut the book.

“**That is nine and none of them is the thing you asked about and you knew that when you asked, and you asked because you wanted the count.**”

“**I wanted to know if there was a count.**”

“**There is a count and it is nine, and the nine is mine, and it is not on a form anywhere in this kingdom, and if you write it down and come back in a year with it I will deny having said it.**”

“**I am not going to come back.**”

“**Then write it down anyway. I have nine of these in a book under a roof and nobody has ever compared notes with me, and I have wanted to say them to somebody for about four years.**”

**He is not the only man in this kingdom keeping a book of things that do not add up. There are at least nine kinds of it in this county and a wheelwright and a carrier and a poor-law man, and not one of them has ever sent a line to another one of them, and that is not because they are enemies of one another. It is because there is no form on which two men who both keep a book may put one book next to another, and I have had that written on me in a form about four times in four days and I have never once been on the other side of it until this morning.**

---

At about the seventh hour he asked his second question, and it was the one he came for.

“**The district's own road sheet. The one that comes with the notices. Does it show the district above the coach inn.**”

“**No.**” The man at the board did not open the book. “**I am not answering that for a man with a barrow and no standing, and I would not answer it for a man with a standing either, and I am telling you both of those so that you do not come back a second time and I do not have to have this conversation again. The sheet is a district's and I am not a district and I do not read it and I have never been asked to and there is nobody in this building who could be.**”

“**You keep a copy.**”

“**I keep a copy of the notices I read out. That is the whole of what I keep.**”

And that was one sentence and it closed the door and he did not put his shoulder against it.

---

He did not ask the third question. It was the only one he wanted and it was this: what is in the rectangle, and is it a thing done to every copy or a thing done to the plate.

He asked it in his own book that night and wrote down the two he had asked and the one he had not, and he wrote which of the three it was that he wanted.

**A sheet is a district's and there is no form on which a man with a standing may look at it either, and that is why a man with a barrow is not the problem and never was. A district hands a sheet to a notice-reader once a year with a bundle of notices in it and does not look at the sheet again, and about nine hundred pages of it are not read by anybody at all, and the man who reads them reads a bell, and the bell is the sheet, and the sheet is the bell, and I have been trying for a hundred and thirty-nine days to get at a piece of paper and there is no door in the paper. There are doors in people and I have been through nine of them and nine people have said no in nine ordinary voices and none of them was sorry.**

---

At about the ninth hour of the evening he was on a step at the edge of the market with the barrow on the flat behind him and the four bolts made fast, and he wrote the last entry of the hundred and thirty-ninth and filled the first, second and third columns and left the fourth as it was.

> *The hundred and thirty-ninth, at about the ninth hour of the evening. **A market town on a crossroads, about two hundred at a board under a roof, nobody shouting, Row Kellow with a book and no office and about nine shillings a quarter and nine years of the same board, and about four hundred notices in the book and nine marginal notes in my hand.**
>
> *Second column, and the nine. **I asked one question in the same words in the same order: is there anybody who keeps a record of the ground between two points and not of the two points. I got nine answers and none of them was the same and none of them was the thing I asked about.** A district keeps wards and days and numbers. A carrier keeps a place, a day and a mileage and may not write the owner, a debt or a price. A wheelwright keeps a book of his carts. A poor-law man keeps a book of a round. A bell-foundry — he stopped and would not say where. A surveyor with a plate, and a plate is not a map. A man who walks a road and sells what he finds on it. A district surveyor whose lines go on paper. A man four days west with a book of every bell in nine counties, named by a man who came through about two years ago and did not ask him to prove it. **Nine, counted twice, and the count is his and not on a form anywhere and he told me to write it down and said he would deny it in a year. I am not going to come back.**
>
> *Third column, ruled apart, and the thing nobody has ever done. **Nine kinds of book in one county and not one line has ever gone from one of them to another, and the reason is not that any two of the nine men dislike each other. It is that there is nothing to write the line on, and a man who rules a column for himself is a man who has to be right about it, and he would rather keep nine years of his own arithmetic than risk being wrong once in a book.** And he said the bell thing before I asked for it: **a bell on a post above a board is a notice, the sound is the thing, the sheet is what the sound was for, and the man who decides when it goes is not the man who reads it.** That is the fifth man in four counties and a city and the fifth one said it in the same shape. **And I did not ask the third question and the third question was the only one I came for, and I have written down here that I did not ask it, and I have not written down what it was.**
>
> *And one column there is not going to get. **He has wanted to rule one about how far ahead of the ringing a sheet is dated, about nine times in nine years, and he has not, and the reason he gave me in the ordinary voice is that a column a man rules for himself is a thing he has to be right about.** Nobody in this kingdom can be checked on a column a man wrote himself. He is right and he is not a door and he is not going to be thanked for the nine.
>
> *All five clauses stand. **Nothing rung, by me or by her, and nothing bound, and nothing counted into iron, and no stage, and no perception attempted and none had, and no document produced, given, shown or asked for, and no sheet taken off any board and nothing put up on it, and no name transferred and no child found by a document, and no Crown office defeated and nobody relieved of anything, and the warrant stands.** Four bolts made fast, **and the frame has not been in a building since the thirty-third and a flat, a roof over a board and a step at a market are not buildings, and the market has a roof over about a third of it and I have not been under any of it.** The carrier in the sack, **not rung, sixty-two days from the seventy-seventh, and it is not struck.** My own bell is in a sealed yard in Kells and I am not going to it, and no bell I have ever made is unbarred anywhere in this kingdom tonight. **Her bell is in her bag, and she stood about nine feet off my left shoulder at that board for about nine minutes and read every mouth in it, which she does, and nobody has asked her for a reason at any hour of a hundred and thirty-nine days including me.**
>
> *The register is thirteen. **Thirteen documents in a coat, none of them about him except the oldest one, and a refusal in one sentence and nine answers, and not one of them is a fourteenth. I have produced nothing and asked for nothing and been given nothing and taken nothing, and I have not thanked the man, and I am not going to, because he did nine of the ten things on that list for himself and for a board under a roof and not for me.**
>
> *Which office is it. **It is a bundle that comes once a year with a bell on the top of it and a sheet nobody reads inside, and a man under a roof with a book who is the person through whom a schedule becomes a sound, and he told me the shape of it before I asked and then he would not look at the sheet I asked about and he was right.** Nobody in it is wicked. **A schedule is a drawing of a road and a road is a fact about the people who walk it, and I have asked nine men in four counties and a city about the drawing and I have not once been asked anything about the road, and the man at the board is the first one who has told me something true about my own errand without being asked and without wanting anything, and I am not going to make that into a door.**
