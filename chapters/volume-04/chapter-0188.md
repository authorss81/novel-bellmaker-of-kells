# Chapter 0188 — The Man Who Shuts the Cart Bridge Gate at Dusk

The fourth hour of the hundred and seventy-fourth day belonged to a man whose name was not on the page and who was on a cart bridge with a stool, a lantern, a can of oil and a bar, oiling the hinge of a gate that came across the middle of the deck and that he shut every evening and opened every morning, and had done for eleven years, and the oiling was a thing he did in the daylight because there was no other light.

Tomas Vale came up onto the deck with the barrow behind him and stopped about nine feet short, because a man oiling a hinge at the hinge end of a gate is a man with his hands full and a gate that a stranger can walk into.

"That bar has gone stiff," the man said, without turning round. "It has gone stiff in the wet and it was not stiff in the dry in the spring, and I have been at it with a bucket and a lever twice this week and I am telling you now so that you do not stand where the bar comes across."

"What is the bar for."

"It is for the carts." He put the can down and worked the hinge with the heel of his hand and got a noise out of it. "This bridge is a cart bridge and a cart comes off it in the dark and there is nine hundred yards of bank above it with nothing at the top of it, and a cart in the dark on that bank goes into the water and I do not have to be told that twice. So it is shut at dusk and opened at dawn and it has been shut at dusk and opened at dawn for eleven years and I do it and there is a man at the top of the bank who has a lantern and knows what a shut gate means."

"And at the hinge end there is a box."

The man looked at the hinge end for a moment as if he had been asked about a part of the bridge and had to work out which part.

"There is," he said.

He did not say anything else about it, and he went back to the hinge, and the oil went on, and it was about nine minutes before Tomas said anything else and about a quarter of an hour before either of them went near the other end of the gate.

---

At about the fifth hour Pell Okell came across the bridge from the far side with a barrow of nothing, having walked out to meet a man and been told there was nothing to carry, and he put the barrow down against the parapet and looked at the gate.

"That gate is going to be a problem this week," he said.

"It is going to be a problem in the next hour," Tomas said. "The bar binds in the wet and he has got it to lift one-handed."

"He has always lifted it one-handed." Pell looked at the bar, and then at the hinge end, and then at Tomas, and he did not say what he was thinking, which was unusual enough that Tomas waited for it. "I was on the far side of this at dusk four days ago and got over the rail, and it is nine hundred yards of bank, and I did it in about a quarter of an hour in the dark, and I have not told anybody that and I am not going to."

The man at the hinge end had heard all of it and had not turned round, and when Tomas looked at him he said, without any particular emphasis, "He is not the first. There was a carter on this bridge in the spring who came up out of the water at the second gate and I was not told about that either and I would have been glad to have been told, and he did not tell me and I have not asked and I am not going to."

"Then the gate is worth something," Tomas said.

"The gate is worth the four steps I do not have to walk in the morning," the man said. "That is what it is worth to me and I have never once pretended otherwise to anybody, and I will tell you the whole of it, because you are the first person in about nine years to come up onto this deck and not ask me the time.

"It came in the spring. A man came up off the bank with a sheet and a number at the top of it and two other men and a ladder, and they were on this deck about two hours, and they put it on the end of the frame at the hinge, and it is a box about a foot long and a bracket and a coupling with a jumper soldered across it, and there is a wire that goes down inside of this post." He took the can and put it on the stool. "That is the whole of what I know and I have had all spring to know more and I have not got more. The man had a number and a day and he said it was for the gate, and I said go on then, and he went on, and he took the sheet away with him."

"And you did not ask."

"I did not ask what it was for because it is for the gate." He said it the way a man says the price of a thing he has been buying for nine years, and then he stopped and turned round, which he had not done all morning. "That is the whole of my defence and it is a true one and I would like somebody to try and say it is not. It is on the gate. It is for the gate. A man comes up with a sheet that says a number and a day and puts a thing on my gate and says it is for the gate, and what am I going to say to him? I have got a stool and a bar and a can of oil, and no form in this kingdom on which a man at a cart bridge gate can write down that something has been put on his gate, and no form on which anybody can ask him whether it is still there."

"That is a bad place to be."

"It is the only place there is." He got the bar and tried it in the socket and it went about a third of the way and stopped, and he put his shoulder into it and it did not move, and he did it twice. "That is the other thing and I have not got over it. It was not stiff in the spring. It went stiff in about the fourth week of the wet and I have been at it twice a week since and it has got worse, and I have never known what has changed about a bar that has not changed, and I have got a gate on this bridge that I can shut from this side and I cannot open from this side, and I go round and lift it on the other side like a man opening a wicket, and if I had somebody's ear about it there is not one to have."

Tomas went to the hinge end and looked at it.

He had spent a hundred and forty days in four counties and a city being the only man anybody could ask about a thing like this, and there had been three of them, and each of the three he had been able to say something about: a coupling and a jumper and a bracket, not the bell's own, not a part of it, and he did not know what they did and neither did the man at the counter, and there was a book on the counter.

This was a box about a foot long on the end of a gate frame at the end of a bracket, with a coupling in it and a jumper soldered across the coupling, and a wire that went down the inside of the post and under the deck and off towards the bank, and there was no bell on it, and there was nothing on it that he could see that was going to be struck, and there was no sheet and no notice and no board, and the man at the hinge end had been told it was for the gate.

He looked at it for about a minute and he said nothing, and it was the first time in a hundred and forty days that he had stood in front of one of these and had nothing whatever.

"You are a maker," the man said. "You have been looking at it a while."

"I have."

"Well?"

"There is nothing on it I know," Tomas said, "and I have said that to four men in a hundred and forty days and every one of them was a man who already knew there was nothing on it. You do not know that. That is the difference between today and the other four and I cannot make it any plainer than that, and I am not going to stand on your bridge and tell you what it is, because I do not know, and a man who tells a gate what a thing on it is for is a man who has been told by somebody in a corridor four days' road away and is passing it on."

"That is fair," the man said. "That is the fairest thing anybody has said on this deck in nine years, and I have had a great many people on this deck."

At about the seventh hour the light went and the man put the bar in. It went about a third of the way and stopped, and he got his shoulder to it and it did not move, and he stood there with his foot on the lower socket for a while and then went round and lifted it from the other side, and the gate came across the deck, and there was a chain and a staple and he locked it, and the lantern was on the stool and the box on the frame was about nine inches from it and there was no light on the box that showed anything, and nobody opened anything, and the wire went down inside the post and under the deck and off towards the bank and nobody followed it.

Pell was on the far side of the gate by then and Tomas was on this side, and neither of them said anything about that either, and Pell waited about a quarter of an hour and then said he was going up the bank in the dark because the bank is nine hundred yards and he knows it.

At about the ninth hour of the evening he was on a flat above the bridge with the barrow behind him and the four bolts made fast, and below him the gate was across the deck with a lock on it and a box on the frame and a wire going down the inside of a post, and a man who could shut it from one side and not open it from the same side, and nobody in this kingdom knew who had put the box there, and Tomas had looked at it for about a minute and had said nothing, and he was thirty-eight days on this road with her and had not asked her once what the road was for.

> *Second column, the fitting. **A man came up off the bank in the spring with a sheet with a number and a day at the top of it, and two other men and a ladder, and they were on the deck about two hours, and what is on the end of the frame at the hinge is a foot of box, a bracket off the frame, a coupling with a jumper across it, and a wire that goes down inside the post, under the deck and off towards the bank.** He said the man told him it was for the gate and he said go on then and the sheet went away with the man. **I did not ask what it was for because it is for the gate, and he said that is the whole of his defence and it is a true one, and I am not going to be able to argue with it.** He has got a stool and a bar and no paper, and no form anywhere on which a man at a gate puts down that a thing has been put on his gate, and none on which a stranger comes four days' road away to look at it and see whether it is still where it was.

> *Third column, ruled apart. **I have had three of these in a hundred and forty days in four counties and a city and every one of them I could say something about: a coupling, a jumper, a bracket, not the bell's own, and a book on the counter, and a man at the counter who does not know either. This one is on a gate frame and there is no bell on it and nothing on it I can see that is going to be struck, and I stood in front of it for about a minute and said nothing, and that is the first time in a hundred and forty days I have stood in front of one of these and had nothing.** I said so to him in the ordinary voice, and said I was not going to stand on his bridge and tell a gate what a thing on it is for, and he said that was the fairest thing anybody had said on that deck in nine years. **Nobody opened anything, nobody went inside anything, nobody followed the wire, and I am not going to say in this book who put it there because I do not know and I am not going to find out this month, and there is no form on which I could find out.** I have called it a box and a bracket and a coupling and a jumper and a wire, which is what it looks like, and I am not putting a name for it in this book, because I do not have one and there is no form in this kingdom that would give me one.*

> *All five clauses stand. **Nothing rung, by me or by her, and nothing bound, and nothing counted into iron, and no stage, and no perception attempted and none had, and the warrant stands, and a fitting is not called anything it is not.** Four bolts made fast, and **the frame has not been in a building since the thirty-third, a hundred and forty-one days, and the deck of a cart bridge is not a building, and the gate is across the deck and I went round it and up the bank in the dark.** The carrier in the sack, **not rung, ninety-seven days from the seventy-seventh, and I have not put my hand on it.** My own bell is in a sealed yard in Kells and the bar is on the bell and not on the licence, and it does not travel. **Her bell is in her bag and it is not rung and nobody asked her for a reason at any hour today, including me.**

> *The register is thirteen. **I have produced nothing and asked for nothing and been given nothing and taken nothing, and nobody was thanked today.**
