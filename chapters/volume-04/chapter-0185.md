# Chapter 0185 — Perry Nunn's Book and Nine Benches That Turn Up

It was the fourth hour of the hundred and seventy-first day and Tomas Vale made the four bolts fast on a strip of gravel at the head of the lock, and the lock was nine hundred yards below the bench and the second pair of gates was on the far side of it with two men on the coping and one of them up a ladder with a cold chisel, and it made a noise in the middle of the day that could be heard a quarter of a mile off.

The frame in its cradle sat on the gravel. It has not been in a building since the thirty-third, and a strip of gravel at the head of a lock is not a building, and it is a hundred and thirty-eight days.

Pell Okell was there with a barrow and a coil of rope and a standing arrangement with a man at the sixth bench, and Ned Adley was there with the hod, and Isa and Mab were a mile up the flat counting fence and were not on the page that day at all, which Tomas had not arranged and could not have arranged.

Pell put his barrow down and looked at the gravel.

"That is not gravel," he said. "That is a boat's ballast. Somebody has tipped about nine tons of stone out of a boat there and packed it and let the rain get into it, and if you set anything down on that and you want it to stand for a week, dig. I say that to everybody. It is the only thing I have ever said to a stranger that has cost me anything, and it has cost me about a shovel."

"That is twice in two days," Ned said. "First the board, now this. You are putting that man's ground right under everything he touches before he has asked to be touched."

"I am putting his ground right under a thing I cannot lift," Pell said, "and that is not the same as putting it right, and you have not asked me why and I am not going to tell you."

---

At about the fifth hour there were eleven men on the lock coping and a clerk in a room at the top of the steps with a book open on the sill of the window, and the clerk was Perry Nunn and he had a pen behind his ear and a blotter that had gone furry.

"You will not read the middle of that," Tomas said, "and I have not asked you to, and I want to say that out loud because there are eleven men on that coping and one of them will have said something by the time I have gone up to the top bench."

Perry Nunn took the pen from behind his ear and put it down in front of him, which is a thing a clerk does when he is going to be careful.

"I have not read a road in my life," he said. "I have been clerk of this bench for eleven years and what I read is rates and what the bench has had from the water and what the bench has paid out, and those are three columns and a day, and there is a fourth column for a reason and a person with a standing has to fill that one in, and I do not have one, and I have never filled one in, and I have never once been asked to and I have never once offered."

He looked at the sheet for a while.

"I am not going to read your middle. I am going to do the only thing I do, which is look at the foot of a paper, because that is where a clerk's eye goes first and it goes there before anybody speaks." He crouched and put his hand out flat above the bottom edge of it without touching it, the way Harlan Loxley had done, and Tomas thought: every trade in this river has a way of looking at a piece of paper without touching it, and not one of the four ways is the way this thing wants to be looked at.

"There," Perry Nunn said. "Bottom right. That is a figure, and that is the sheet's number, and a district numbers its sheets the way a man numbers his hooks, and I have seen four hundred feet of sheets in my life and the foot of a sheet is the only part of it that never changes. Ward. Number. Day. That is three things and it is on every sheet from here to the coast and it is in the same hand every time because one hand cuts the plates."

"And the other thing."

"There is another figure." He shifted his hand about a foot to the left, to the head of the column nearest the top of the sheet. "Same hand as the sheet's number. Same figure, near enough, and near enough is close enough that I would not put my name to the difference on a wet day and I would put it to the difference on a dry one. It is at the head of the column, not the foot, and it is not a number of a sheet, and I cannot tell you what it counts, and I am not going to."

"You have seen it before."

"I have seen that figure in the corner of a rate book," Perry Nunn said. "Once. About six years ago, in a rate book from the second bench, at the top of the page where the bench writes down what it has taken in for the reach. The reach is not a sheet. The reach is about nine miles of water and it is a thing a bench has a rate for and a rate is per cart and is not a figure of anything you can put your hand on. And there is no form in this kingdom on which a sheet is a receipt, and there is no form on which a receipt is a sheet, and I have never had a reason to want one, and I have had six years to want one and I have not wanted one once."

He stood up and looked at the eleven men on the coping and the one on the ladder with the cold chisel.

"I am going to tell you three things and then I am going to shut my book, and the three things are all of what I have got. One: the figure at the foot is a sheet's number and it is the same figure at the head of that column and the two of them are in one hand. Two: I cannot say what the head one counts and I have had a rate book with that figure in the corner of it for six years and I have never been able to join them, and joining them is not my work. Three: if it were mine I would take it to the second bench and put it in front of the man there, and the man there would say it is a sheet number, and I would say it is at the head of a column and not the foot of a sheet, and he would say that is a sheet number, and I would come back here and put it in front of you, and that is how it would go, and in nine years it would be two men and a book and a stranger, and there would be no more to it than there is now."

"Do not take it to the second bench," Tomas said.

"I was not going to." Perry Nunn picked up his pen. "I have told you I have not got a standing and there is no form on which I could carry it, and I am not going to build one out of a courtesy. I have not filled the fourth column in eleven years and I am not starting on a paper I did not write."

At about the sixth hour the two men came down off the coping and the one with the chisel sat down on the step and would not sit within about nine feet of Tomas, and the chisel man said the second pair of gates wants two new heel posts and forty feet of new iron and a stone sill that has to be cut while the water is down, and that is a fortnight of work and that no one bench can do a fortnight of work.

"Then the benches will do it," the other one said. "The second pair has been on the list since the water came up in the spring and nobody has done it, and everybody at every bench has known about it since the spring, and this is what happens: the gate gets bad, and then a man from the third bench says it out loud in a doorway, and then it is said at the fourth, and then somebody at the seventh says he will do it on a day, and then nine benches turn up on that day, and there was never a day anybody decided it and there is not going to be."

Nine benches, and a shared river, and a rate that is one rate for the water and not nine. And eleven foundry weights in a press in a shed. And a swept step.

At about the seventh hour Tomas sat on the coping with his back to the water and did the thing he had come out of the last four days to do, which was put four tables together, and it took him longer than four minutes and it came to very little.

Table one was a beam on a cart bridge and a carter who would not touch paper he had not been asked about, and what came off it was a crease and a quarter of an hour of a morning he did not have. Table two was a bench beam and a man who would not put in a book a thing he did not know, and what came off it was a corner heavier than a crease and a bridge held up for a third of an hour. Table three was a lime stand with six boards and no book, and what came off it was that the price is the price of the day. Table four was a bench clerk's sill and a figure at the head of a column.

Four tables, four trades, and four men, and not one of them owed him anything, and not one of them was thanked, and not one of them said a word about the rectangle. What came off the four tables was a carter's morning, a weighmaster's quarter hour, a shed with no ledger in it, and a figure in a hand. He could not put those four things into anything. He could not take them anywhere, because there is no form in this kingdom on which a man with a barrow may say *a man at a weighbeam told me this about a piece of paper*, and being believed by somebody is worth exactly nothing, which he had been told twice in forty days in a market town and at a bench and had written down both times and had not been improved by either.

And the fifth thing was a gate that nine benches were going to mend on a day that nobody had chosen.

At about the ninth hour of the evening he was on a strip of flat above the head of the lock with the barrow behind him and the four bolts made fast, and the water was making the noise the second pair of gates made, and the chisel man was still on the coping, and Tomas was thirty-five days on this road with her and had not asked her once.

> *Second column, the paper, the fourth table. **The foot of a sheet is ward, number and day, in one hand because one hand cuts the plates, and the foot is the only part of a sheet that never changes, and he has seen four hundred feet of sheets.** The figure at the head of the column nearest the top of the sheet is the same figure and in the same hand, and he cannot say what it counts. **He has seen that figure in the corner of a rate book once, six years back, where a bench writes what it has taken in for the reach, and a reach is nine miles of water with a rate per cart, and there is no form in this kingdom on which a sheet is a receipt or a receipt is a sheet.** He would not take it to the second bench, because he has no standing and no form to carry it on, and he has not filled the fourth column in eleven years and is not starting on a paper he did not write. **He is right, he is not sorry, and I did not thank him.**
>
> *Third column, ruled apart. **Four tables in four days and four trades, and what came off them is not a sentence and not a document: a carter's morning, a weighmaster's third of an hour, a shed with no ledger under the price, and a figure in a hand at the head of a column.** And the fifth thing came off the coping: **nine benches are going to mend a gate on a day that nobody chose, and the reason it will not be written down is not secrecy, it is that no bench has a book that is for this and every bench has a clerk who would have to fill a fourth column, and a fourth column wants a person with a standing, and a bench is not a person.** A thing can be agreed by nine rooms and there is no instrument of the agreement, and the instrument is that it gets mended.*

> *All five clauses stand. **Nothing rung, by me or by her, and nothing bound, and nothing counted into iron, and no stage, and no perception attempted and none had, and the warrant stands, and a fitting is not called anything it is not.** Four bolts made fast, and **the frame has not been in a building since the thirty-third, a hundred and thirty-eight days, and a strip of tipped ballast at the head of a lock is not a building and I moved it nine feet onto ground a carter told me about before I put it down.** The carrier in the sack, **not rung, ninety-four days from the seventy-seventh, and I have not put my hand on it.** My own bell is in a sealed yard in Kells and the bar is on the bell and not on the licence, and it does not travel. **Her bell is in her bag and it is not rung and nobody asked her for a reason at any hour today, including me.**
