# Chapter 0173 — The Market Table Under Canvas

It was the fourth hour of the hundred and fifty-ninth day and Tomas Vale made the four bolts fast on trodden grass at the edge of a market under canvas, and the stalls were already up and the smell of hot fat and cut turnip hung low.

The frame has not been in a building since the thirty-third and it is a hundred and twenty-six days, and grass and canvas and stalls are not buildings.

---

At about the fifth hour Pell led them round the outside, counting carts.

"Six carts in. Four out before noon, if the rain holds," he said. "That is a market day's load. Not a big one."

The market was perhaps forty stalls, cloth and pots and salt fish, and at the far end a long table where a woman in a grey shawl was writing in a book, taking coin, giving chits. People queued with sacks.

Isa went to look at salt and came back. "Salt is dear," she said. "Lime is cheaper and nobody wants it. That is why I carry it."

Mab stood at the edge counting the guy ropes. "Thirty-one," she said. "Ropes, not fences. Different ground."

At about the sixth hour Tomas went to the long table to ask the road, and waited his turn behind a man with onions.

The woman did not look up. "Name of road you came by."

"The old road above the ford," Tomas said.

She wrote something, tore a chit, pushed it across. "Two coppers for the stall row if you sell. Nothing if you walk through."

"I walk through," he said. "Is there a place here people live, or is it stalls only."

Now she looked up. "Live?" She jerked her head at the canvas behind. "Tents behind. Crew sleep there. Porters, sellers, my two lads. We are here three days in seven."

At about the seventh hour he walked behind the stalls with Pell, and saw it: two rows of tents, fires, children, a woman washing pots, a man mending a pole, near fifty people living in the lee of the canvas. A village that packed up twice a week and moved a mile and put itself down again.

Pell counted tents the way he counted loads. "Fourteen tents. Fifty mouths, near enough. That is a village that walks."

"And the book," Tomas said. "What does she put them down as."

Pell shrugged. "Ask her and she will tell you it is stalls and porters. She counts coin, not mouths."

They went back. Nobody asked the woman anything more. She had answered about the road and that was the whole of what she owed a stranger.

---

At about the ninth hour of the evening he was on trodden grass beyond the last tent with the barrow behind him and the four bolts made fast, and the market noise came thin through the canvas.

> *The hundred and fifty-ninth, at about the ninth hour of the evening. Grass, canvas, forty stalls, fourteen tents, a long table, a flat.*
>
> *Second column. Fourteen tents behind the stalls with near fifty people living in them three days in seven. The woman's book takes coin and gives chits for the row. Porters and sellers by day and sleepers by night in the same canvas.*
>
> *Third column, ruled apart. Ruling: a village that walks is still a village, though no paper has a line for one that moves.*
>
> *All five clauses stand. Nothing rung, by me or by her, and nothing bound, and nothing counted into iron, and no stage, and no perception attempted and none had, and the warrant stands. Four bolts made fast, and the frame has not been in a building since the thirty-third. The carrier in the sack, not rung, eighty-two days from the seventy-seventh, and I have not put my hand on it and the hand was finished on the hundred and twenty-seventh. My own bell is in a sealed yard in Kells and the bar is on the bell and not on the licence, and it does not travel. Her bell is in her bag and it is not rung today and nobody asked her for a reason at any hour, including me.*
>
> *The register is thirteen. I have produced nothing and asked for nothing and been given nothing and taken nothing, and nobody was thanked today.*
>
> *Which office is it. It is a table, a book of chits, and fourteen tents behind it.*
