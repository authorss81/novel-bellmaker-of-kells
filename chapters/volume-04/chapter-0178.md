# Chapter 0178 — The Question About The Road

It was the fourth hour of the hundred and sixty-fourth day and Tomas Vale made the four bolts fast on a flat of ash and cinder beside a repair shed, and the shed gang were mending handles and coiling rope, and the morning smelled of tar.

The frame has not been in a building since the thirty-third and it is a hundred and thirty-one days, and ash and a shed yard are not buildings.

---

At about the sixth hour, for the shed was busy at the fifth and there was no room to speak, Tomas stood with Tamsin Rook at the edge of the yard where the ground changed from cinder to stone, and she had her hand on the shed corner post and her boots on the hard edge, reading the yard through it.

They had walked twenty-two days and he had not once asked her what the road was for. He had noticed it twice, on the flat below the ford and on the bank above the turning, and had not asked either time, because a man who fixes a reason on a thing is building a case and he had none.

He did not ask her for a reason now. He asked about the road, which was a different thing and was his to ask.

"Is this road worth walking," he said. "The nine places are dwellings and the return says work, and I have four people and not one sentence, and the bridge stands. Should I go on up it."

She looked at the yard, not at him. The hammers rang through the post into her palm. She took her hand off, set it again, nodded once to herself about the weight of something.

"No," she said.

It was the first plain word she had given him in twenty-two days about anything that mattered, and it was about the road and not about him. He accepted it the way he had accepted every refusal on this road, by not improving it.

"Then I go on," he said. "Because nobody else will carry it and the return will stand forty years if I stop."

She put her hand back on the post. That was the whole of it. No reason was asked and none was given, and the fourth clause of the arrangement made out loud in a room he paid for on the hundred and twentieth stood four inches long between them and was not renegotiated.

At about the seventh hour Ned came over with a coil of rope. "Shed says the road past the store is soft," he said. "Lip at the culvert you could catch a heel on. Keep left."

Tomas nodded. "Left."

Nobody thanked anybody. The gang worked on.

---

At about the ninth hour of the evening he was on ash beyond the shed with the barrow behind him and the four bolts made fast, and she was nine feet off with her back to a wall where stone met cinder and she could get her hand on both.

> *The hundred and sixty-fourth, at about the ninth hour of the evening. Ash, tar, a repair shed, rope, a corner post, a flat.*
>
> *Second column. A question put at the edge of a yard where the ground changes, about the road and not about a person. One word back. A no about going on, accepted, and going on anyway because a return stands forty years.*
>
> *Third column, ruled apart. Ruling: a no about a road can be accepted and still walked past, and that is not a quarrel.*
>
> *All five clauses stand. Nothing rung, by me or by her, and nothing bound, and nothing counted into iron, and no stage, and no perception attempted and none had, and the warrant stands. Four bolts made fast, and the frame has not been in a building since the thirty-third. The carrier in the sack, not rung, eighty-seven days from the seventy-seventh, and I have not put my hand on it and the hand was finished on the hundred and twenty-seventh. My own bell is in a sealed yard in Kells and the bar is on the bell and not on the licence, and it does not travel. Her bell is in her bag and it is not rung today and nobody asked her for a reason at any hour, including me.*
>
> *The register is thirteen. I have produced nothing and asked for nothing and been given nothing and taken nothing, and nobody was thanked today.*
>
> *Which office is it. It is handles mended, rope coiled, and a culvert with a lip in it.*
