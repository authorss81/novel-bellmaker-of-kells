# Chapter 0167 — Four People Asked The Same Question In The Same Words And Four Answers, And The One Man Who Would Carry A Sentence Is The One Man He May Not Put In A Column

It was the fourth hour of the hundred and fifty-third day and Tomas Vale made the four bolts fast on a flat above a hedge, and the four of them were on the road below him and he went down to them, because a man with a barrow who asks questions from above a hedge is a man arranging a thing.

**The frame has not been in a building since the thirty-third and it is a hundred and twenty days, and a flat, a hedge and a road are not buildings, and I asked all four of them the same question on the same ground on the same day and I wrote the four answers down in the same hand one under the other and I did not join them and I said so out loud before I asked the first one.**

---

At about the fifth hour he asked them, one at a time, out of earshot of the others, in the same words in the same order, and it was: **what is on this road between the inn and the ford.**

Pell Okell: “**Carts. Two of mine and about four a day off the inn, and a load of sacking, and a load of lime, and a man who goes up about nine days out of ten with a barrow of nothing, and that is the whole of what is on it and it has been the whole of what is on it longer than I have been at it.**”

Mab Sarter: “**Fences. I am at two hundred and six and going and the number is not finished and there is a gate at the second mile that was a fence when I counted it, and there is a soft place at the top by a heap of stone that a man has been at twice.**”

Isa Trevail: “**A bridge, and about a mile of road I would rather not be on with a barrow on it, and eleven trips' worth of going round, and a bank on the right for about a quarter mile that takes the afternoon off a loaded cart, and that is all I know because I go up and down it and I do not look left.**”

Ned Adley: “**Soft.**”

“**That is one word.**”

“**That is the trade.**” He did not stop. “**Soft at the top by a heap of stone, which I have done twice. Soft at the third mile in a wet week, which I have done once and I will do again. Two stringers and a deck on a bridge that has had three boards on it long enough. And that is every soft place between here and the ford that I know of, and I have been on that road on my own account for longer than the heap of stone has been there, and I am not paid for this stretch and I did not come here for you.**”

**Four answers and not two of them the same, and every one of them true, and about four in ten of any of them is right and none of them is a shape, and I have now got nine of these in ten days and I am not going to line them up and call the line a map. A map is a drawing of a road. A road is a fact about the people who walk it, and I wrote that on a wall on the hundred and forty-first and I have not improved it since and I am not going to today.**

---

At about the sixth hour he asked them the other question, the one he had come for, and he asked it the same way, one at a time, out of earshot, and it was: **is there anybody you would trust to carry a true sentence about this road four miles, on foot, tomorrow, without being paid.**

Pell Okell: “**No. I would carry a barrow of lime four miles. A sentence is heavier and it is not mine and I would want to know what happens to me if I am wrong about it, and there is no form that says what happens.**”

Mab Sarter: “**Me. And I would want paying, and not for the walking. I would want somebody to have written down what I said, so that if I say it different in a different place then one of the two of us is wrong and the other one can be found. And I do not know what that is called and I have never been asked to do anything like it, and if you have it then I will go tomorrow.**”

Isa Trevail: “**No. Not because of you. Because I have said four true things to you in three days and about two of them are not about this road at all, and if a person carries a sentence for a week and adds a thing to it every day, then at the end of the week the sentence is longer and the person is a man who has been talking.**”

“**And you.**”

“**And me.**” The shovel went in and came out. “**I will carry it and I will not be asked, and I will not be paid, and I will want it in a form, and I will tell you now that I am right about that and that you are not going to be able to give me one, and I have thought about it and I have still said yes, and I would like that written down in the correct order: I have said yes knowing I am right to want a thing I am not going to get.**”

“**Why do you want a form.**”

“**Because a sentence a man carries in his head is a sentence about a road, and a sentence on a piece of paper is a sentence about a road that somebody else can be wrong about, and I would like to know which of those I am holding when I am holding one, because I have been in a trade for a long time and the only thing I have ever been careful of is not being the last man to touch a thing that nobody can check.**”

He said that and then he put a foot on the shovel and looked at Tomas Vale, and Tomas Vale stood on a road above an old road in a district he had no standing in and did not answer him, and the silence was about nine seconds long, and it was the correct silence, and he has had a hundred and fifty-three days to learn the difference between a silence that is respect and a silence that is arranging and this was the first one and there was nothing in it at all.

---

At about the seventh hour he wrote the two sentences that keep a thing true in this kingdom, and he did not improve either of them, and he had written both of them out loud on a landing stage ten days ago and had thought they were finished.

**A thing said twice at ten feet in the open goes into the ears of whoever is standing there and there is no copy of it anywhere afterwards, and the people who heard it go off it one at a time, and it dies in about nine years with the last of them. A thing written down is read by every clerk who comes after for forty years and can be produced against you by a stranger you have never met. Those are the two instruments I have and I have been choosing between them as though they were the only two, and they are not the only two and they are not the best one.**

**The third is a person on foot. It cannot be edited. It costs a day. And a day of it is not mine to spend on somebody else, and there is no form in this kingdom on which a man with no standing may ask a stranger for a day, and so a route is a thing I have to be given and I cannot buy one and I cannot order one and I cannot put one in a book.**

**And here is what a hundred and fifty-three days has got me. Four people. One true sentence offered, one refused for being heavier than a barrow, one refused for being a thing that grows, and one accepted by a man who is right to want a form I cannot give him. A true sentence is the smallest thing anybody in this kingdom can carry and I have four people and not one sentence, and I did not arrange the four and I am not going to arrange the sentence, and the reason is four inches long and it is the fourth clause of an arrangement made out loud in a room I paid for on the hundred and twentieth, and I am not going to improve it or write it as a rule, because I have written it as a rule four times and it is still four inches.**

---

At about the ninth hour of the evening he was on the flat above the hedge with the barrow behind him and the four bolts made fast, and he wrote the last entry of the hundred and fifty-third and filled the first, second and third columns and left the fourth as it was.

> *The hundred and fifty-third, at about the ninth hour of the evening. **A flat above a hedge, four people on a road, one shovel, and a flat.**
>
> *Second column, and the same question four times, out of earshot, in the same words in the same order: **what is on this road between the inn and the ford.** Carts, and sacking, and a man with a barrow of nothing nine days out of ten. Fences, and a number that is not finished, and a gate that was a fence, and two goes at a soft place. A bridge, and about a mile of road a woman with a barrow would rather not be on, and eleven trips round it. And then one word, **soft**, and then three places, and then the reason, and then the sentence that finished it: **he has been on that road on his own account longer than the heap of stone, and he is not paid for this stretch, and he did not come here for me.** Four answers, all four true, and not two of them the same, and **I said out loud before I asked the first one that I was not going to join them, and I did not, and I am not going to.**
>
> *Third column, ruled apart, and the question I came for, asked the same way, one at a time, out of earshot: **would you carry a true sentence about this road four miles, on foot, tomorrow, without being paid.** No, because a barrow of lime is one thing and a sentence is heavier and there is no form that says what happens if I am wrong about it. And the second answer was a yes with a price on it, and the price was not money. **She wants the sentence written down, so that if she says it one way in one place and another way in another, then one of the two of them can be found and the other can be called wrong. She does not know what that is called, and I have not got a form to give her, and there is not one in this kingdom to give.** No, because I have said four true things in three days and two of them are not about this road, and a sentence that grows every day is a man who has been talking. And then the fourth man, who said yes before the question was finished, and who said he would not be asked and would not be paid, **and who wants a form, and who is right to, and who said yes knowing he was right to want a thing he is not going to get, and asked for that to be written down in that order.**
>
> *And I stood there and did not answer him, and the silence was about nine seconds, and it was the first silence in a hundred and fifty-three days that had nothing in it at all, and it was the correct one, and I did not put a form in his hand because I cannot certify anything and there is no form and if there were one he would be in a column in it, and he is right and I am not able and both of those are the same fact.
>
> *And the two sentences, carried forward and not improved. **Out loud, at ten feet, and it has no second copy anywhere and it leaves with the people who heard it, one at a time, over about nine years. Written down, and it outlives me in a drawer and a stranger can carry it out of that drawer in forty years. And a person on foot, which is the only one of the three that cannot be touched once it has left them, and it costs a day, and the day is not mine to spend on somebody else, and there is no form on which a man with no standing may ask a stranger for a day.** A true sentence is the smallest thing anybody in this kingdom can carry. **Four of them on this stretch of road and not one of the four is carrying one.**
>
> *All five clauses stand. **Nothing rung, by me or by her, and nothing bound, and nothing counted into iron, and no stage, and no perception attempted and none had and none reached for, and no Crown fitting called anything it is not and the word is not used, and no mechanism described, and no document produced, given, shown or asked for, and no name transferred and no child found by a document and no sheet taken off a board, and no Crown office defeated and nobody relieved of anything, and the warrant stands.** Four bolts made fast, **and the frame has not been in a building since the thirty-third and a flat, a hedge and a road are not buildings, and I went down off the flat to ask them and I did not ask them from above a hedge.** The carrier in the sack, **not rung, seventy-six days from the seventy-seventh, and it is not struck, and I have not put my hand on it and I am not going to, and the hand was finished on the hundred and twenty-seventh.** My own bell is in a sealed yard in Kells and I am barred from any public place in that county and the bar is on the bell and not on the licence, and I am not going to it, and it does not travel, and no bell I have ever made is unbarred anywhere in this kingdom tonight. **Her bell is in her bag and it is not rung today and nobody asked her for a reason at any hour of a hundred and fifty-three days, including this one, and including me.**
>
> *The register is thirteen. **Thirteen documents in a coat, none of them about him except the oldest one, and four answers to a question about a road and four answers to a question about a sentence, and not one of the eight is a fourteenth. I have produced nothing and asked for nothing and been given nothing and taken nothing, and nobody was thanked today, and a woman asked me for a piece of paper and I could not give her one and did not pretend I could.**
>
> *Which office is it. **It is a form, a column, and the four boxes a district's form has, and none of the four is a place to put a man with a shovel who wants to know which kind of sentence he is holding.** Nobody in it is wicked. **A true sentence is the smallest thing anybody in this kingdom can carry. Four of them on this stretch of road and not one of the four is carrying one, and a man with a shovel has said yes to carrying one on the condition that he can be wrong in public, and I cannot give him that and he knew I could not before he said yes, and neither of us is going to pretend otherwise.**
