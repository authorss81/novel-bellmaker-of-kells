# Chapter 0176 — The Nine Lines In A Return

It was the fourth hour of the hundred and sixty-second day and Tomas Vale made the four bolts fast on the cinders outside the district store, in the same place as the day before, because the clerk had said to come back if they wanted the road past the quarry, and the barrow sat level and the morning was cold.

The frame has not been in a building since the thirty-third and it is a hundred and twenty-nine days, and cinders and a yard wall are not buildings.

---

At about the fifth hour the clerk came out with two books under his arm and a pot of paste, and began pasting a fresh heading slip inside the cover of the return, smoothing it with his thumb. Pell was already at the yard fence, re-wedging a loose post with a stone, because a fence that leans is a thing a carter cannot leave alone.

"Hold that straight," Pell said, and the clerk held it without stopping his pasting.

Inside the store, through the counter window, Tomas could see the fair copy lying open on the counter. It was ruled in four columns with a fifth for the day, and down the left ran nine lines in a fair hand, each with a figure of mouths against it.

The clerk saw him looking and did not cover it. A man with nothing to hide does not cover a book.

"That is yesterday's list written fair," he said, dabbing paste. "Nine places up the water. Ford gang thirty. Market crew fifty. Landing shed forty. Stone gang twenty-two. Timber stack twelve. Ferry six. Store hands eighteen. Ditch gang twenty-five. Repair shed fifteen. All fed. All paid."

At about the sixth hour Tomas read the headings across the top while the clerk worked. The headings were not names of villages. They were work: sacks landed, loads carted, feet of ditch, days of hire. At the foot, the totals of coin and meal.

"They live there," Tomas said. "Washing. Children. Fires."

"They do," the clerk said. He was smoothing the slip, his mouth busy with the edge of it. "Two hundred and more altogether. Good workers."

"And the paper says work."

The clerk set the paste down and picked up his pen to rule the day's line, talking while he ruled, the way a man talks when the work is in his hands and the words are not the work.

"It says what there is a box for." He drew the line straight. "There is a box for sacks and a box for loads and a box for days and a box for mouths fed on the job. There is no box anywhere that says a household. I have looked. I keep this book four years and there is no such box in it."

He blotted the line.

"A house that is answered for is a house with a keeper, and a keeper is a man who can be asked to hold a line at the hour. I cannot get keepers up that water. No man there will take it, and I do not blame them, and without a keeper I cannot get a bell to go at the hour, and without that there is nothing to answer for. So they are on the return as mouths on a job, and they are paid coin and meal, and that is correct."

He capped the pen. He was right, and he was not sorry, and he did not look up for thanks and none was given.

At about the seventh hour Tomas walked out of the yard with the four behind him and said nothing for a long way. The rectangle in his coat, about two hands together with nothing in it, was the same shape as nine lines of mouths fed on a job. A map was a list of what was owed, and a person off the list was a person nobody owed anything to.

He had come up the road looking for a removed piece of road. What was there was nine places, each with people in it, each with a market or a ford or a landing, each entered as something other than a dwelling. Nobody had hidden them. Nobody was guilty. The paper was smooth where the rules stopped short because there was no column for what stood on the ground.

He wrote no name down. He had no form and no column, and he would not make one.

---

At about the ninth hour of the evening he was on dry grass beyond the store yard with the barrow behind him and the four bolts made fast.

> *The hundred and sixty-second, at about the ninth hour of the evening. Cinders, a counter window, a fair copy, nine ruled lines, dry grass, a flat.*
>
> *Second column. Nine places up the water read in a district's fair hand while the man pasted a slip and ruled a line. Each set as work with mouths fed against it, paid coin and meal. No box for a household in four years of book.*
>
> *Third column, ruled apart. Ruling: the removed piece of drawing is nine places taken off the list of what is owed, and a man who cannot get keepers cannot get an answer at the hour.*
>
> *All five clauses stand. Nothing rung, by me or by her, and nothing bound, and nothing counted into iron, and no stage, and no perception attempted and none had, and the warrant stands. Four bolts made fast, and the frame has not been in a building since the thirty-third. The carrier in the sack, not rung, eighty-five days from the seventy-seventh, and I have not put my hand on it and the hand was finished on the hundred and twenty-seventh. My own bell is in a sealed yard in Kells and the bar is on the bell and not on the licence, and it does not travel. Her bell is in her bag and it is not rung today and nobody asked her for a reason at any hour, including me.*
>
> *The register is thirteen. I have produced nothing and asked for nothing and been given nothing and taken nothing, and nobody was thanked today. The fair copy is the district's and I read it through a window and it stays there.*
>
> *Which office is it. It is a return about a thing in a ward, nine lines of work, and no household column.*
