# Chapter 0186 — The Second Pair of Gates on a Day Nobody Chose

It was the fourth hour of the hundred and seventy-second day and Tomas Vale made the four bolts fast on the coping of the lock above the second pair of gates, and the coping is nine feet wide and a barrow's length of it is level, and the frame sat there with the water about two feet below and a long way off in both directions. It has not been in a building since the thirty-third, and a lock coping is not a building, and it is a hundred and thirty-nine days.

There were four of the four on the road and none of them was there. Pell had a load at the sixth bench at the fifth hour and had gone at the third. Ned was at a cart bridge at the second hour and had gone at the first. Isa and Mab were on the flat with a run of fence and a girl at the third bench and had gone at the second. Tomas had the lock to himself, and he had it to himself for about twenty minutes, and then nine men came over the coping at once from nine different directions without anybody having said anything to anybody.

What they brought was this: stone, about two tons of it, in a barrow. Rope, a great deal of it, on a man's shoulder. A dressed sill stone, four feet by two, on two poles with four men, which came off a barrow that had been standing in a gateway somewhere and had clearly been standing in that gateway a long time. A handbar and three cold chisels. Lime, a bucket, in a pail. A wedge and a sledge. A kettle and a fire-basket. A man with a barrow of sleepers. And a ninth thing, which was a woman with a basket of bread and a pot, which was the ninth bench's idea and which nobody had asked her for.

Nine benches, nine things, and not one of them had been told what to bring.

---

At about the fifth hour the two men were up the ladder on the coping and the dressed sill was lying on the flat with nobody's hand on it, and it was a good piece of stone and it had been dressed and it had been in a gateway, and a man from the second bench asked who had brought it and where it was from, and a man from the fifth bench said his gateway, and did not say anything else, and put his barrow down.

Somebody said, "Then that is a sill and it is the sill, and we are not going to cut one, and the gate is going back on that in the week."

And then somebody else said the thing that the whole of the day turned on, and said it in four inches, the way a man says the price of a thing he has been buying for nine years.

"There are nine of us on this coping and somebody wants to be in charge of it."

Nobody answered him.

"I am not saying it to be against anybody. I am saying a gate is mended the same way at all nine, and if the fourth bench does it one way and the seventh does it another, then in nine years somebody will come and ask which way is the right way and there will be nobody to ask, and I would rather there was a man. I have been to a bridge in this county where four men mended a sill in a morning and it is still holding, and I do not know one thing about how they did it, and I have wanted to know for eleven years, and I have never found out and I never will."

"No," said the man from the fifth bench, and he was not looking at anybody. "We have never had a man in charge of this and the gate has been mended anyway."

"That is not an answer, that is a habit."

"It is not a habit, it is the whole of it." He turned round and spat. "If there is a man in charge of it then the day it is not mended is the day that somebody let it go, and now it is nobody's fault, and nobody's fault gets mended. The gates on this river have gone two hundred years without anybody standing over any of them, and every one of them is still up. You want a man and what you will get is a man who has to be asked, and the asking will go in a book, and I have seen what happens when a thing about a gate goes in a book. It stops."

Nobody said anything then, and the two men went on up the ladder, and that was the end of it, and it took about a minute and a half.

At about the sixth hour Tomas went along the coping and asked two men the same question, and he did it carefully and he did it on purpose, because he had learned in a market of about two thousand that a man who takes the same words to three men and calls the result a count has three sentences and none of them is anybody's answer, and he was not going to do it here and he could not help doing it either, because it was the only instrument he had.

The man at the third bench, who had brought the stone, said the sill would be up in four days.

The man at the seventh bench, who had brought the handbar and the chisels, said it would be up in nine.

"The water has to be down," the third bench said. "You cannot cut a sill under a foot of water and you cannot get a sill in under a foot of water, and the water is down nine days in a year and this is not one of them, and the next time it is down is on the turn, and that is four days off, and in four days we can have it up and mended in a week and nobody at the seventh bench will be able to tell you the gate is different."

"The water is not what the sill is," the seventh bench said. "The water is four days. A sill is not a cut, a sill is dressed. That stone has been dressed for nine years and it took nine years and the man that dressed it is not going to be alive to tell anybody how. I have dressed a sill and it is nine days of work with a handbar and three chisels and a man who has done it before, and you have one of those and I have one of those, and between us it is nine, and there is no water in that number at all."

Both of them were right. Tomas stood on the coping between them and did not do the thing he wanted to do, which was to go back to the third bench and say that the seventh bench had said nine days, so that they would know. If he did that, the third bench would work to nine and the seventh would work to four, and one of the two men would be wrong on purpose, and a man who is wrong on purpose is a man who has been given an answer to work from, and a man with an answer to work from is a man who turns up at eight in the morning instead of at the second hour, and that is the whole of what a command is.

So he wrote both numbers in his own book on the same line and put a bar between them, and he did not divide them and he did not average them and he did not carry either of them to the other man, and the difference was nine days and it is going to be nine days on this gate for the rest of the time the gate stands, and the sill came up on the fourth day and the gate was mended, and the two men never knew that they had said different things about the same stone.

At about the seventh hour the woman with the basket of bread sat down on the coping and put her feet up and did not go near either of them, and the two men on the ladder had gone down and there was lime in the joints and the sill was half in. And Tomas worked out what it was going to cost, and it was the first time in a hundred and forty days that he had worked out a cost for anything, and it was not the money.

The sill stone came out of a gateway at the fifth bench, and the fifth bench would not say whose gateway, and the money for the ironwork would come out of the rate, and the rate is per cart and is spread along the whole reach, and the reach is about nine miles, and so the price of this gate would be added to the price of every cart that went over any of that nine miles by every man who used it, including the nine who mended it and including a man at a pitch nine miles up who had never heard of a sill and would be charged for one.

And in four years, if the sill failed, there would be no name to send the bill to and the bench that mended it would pay, and the rate would go up again, and it would go up by a good deal more than the first time, because a bench that has mended the same gate twice knows what it cost.

He asked the man at the fifth bench about it, because it was the only question in the day he had to ask anybody.

"That is the price," the man said. "We know that. It went up four years ago for the upper sill and it went up again after, and everybody on this water knows the rate went up and none of us could tell you what for, and that is the price and we pay it. If you want somebody's name at the top of it, you will not get it, and if we had somebody's name at the top of it we would not do it, and both of those are true and only one of them is a reason."

At about the ninth hour of the evening he was on a flat above the head of the lock with the barrow behind him and the four bolts made fast, and nine men had gone off down the coping in nine directions in about the same moment, and nobody had counted them and nobody had missed one. He was thirty-six days on this road with her and had not asked her once what the road was for.

> *Second column, the thing the day turned on. Somebody said there are nine of us on this coping and somebody wants to be in charge of it, because a gate is mended the same way at all nine and in nine years somebody will come and ask which way is right and there will be nobody to ask. And a man said no, and said **we have never had a man in charge of this and the gate has been mended anyway**, and was told that was a habit and not an answer, and said it was not a habit, it was the whole of it. **Put a man over it and the day the gate is not mended becomes a day somebody let it go, and a gate nobody owns gets mended and a gate a man owns does not, and those are the same gate in about nine years.** The gates on this river have gone two hundred years with nobody standing over any of them, and every one of them is still up. And a man has to be asked for, and the asking wants a book, and a thing about a gate in a book stops. **That is not politics. It is that they have never needed one, and the rate has gone up twice and they paid it both times.**

> *Second column, the two numbers. The third bench, who brought the stone, says four days, because the water has to be down and it is down nine days in a year. The seventh bench, who brought the handbar, says nine, **because a sill is not a cut, a sill is dressed, and that stone was dressed nine years and took nine years and the man that dressed it is not going to be alive to say how.** **Both are right and the difference is nine days and there is no water in the seventh number at all. I did not carry either number to the other man, and if I had, one of them would have worked to a figure he did not have, and a man working to a figure he has been given comes at eight instead of at the second hour, and that is the whole of what a command is.**
>
> *Third column, ruled apart. **They will share a repair and they will not take a command, and the refusal is not a principle, it is a habit that has been right for two hundred years and can be shown to be right by a gate that is still up.** The price is not the money. **The price is that the rate goes up for the whole reach, nine miles, for everybody including a man at a pitch who never heard of a sill, and that in four years if the sill fails there is no name at the top of the bill and the bench that mended it pays again, and more.** A man at the fifth bench who would not say whose gateway the stone came out of, and who said we know that, we pay it, and that with somebody's name at the top of it they would not do it, and that both of those are true and only one is a reason. **I am not the clerk of it and I keep no terms, and nine men went off that coping in nine directions and nobody counted them and nobody missed one.**
>
> *All five clauses stand. **Nothing rung, by me or by her, and nothing bound, and nothing counted into iron, and no stage, and no perception attempted and none had, and the warrant stands, and a fitting is not called anything it is not.** Four bolts made fast, and **the frame has not been in a building since the thirty-third, a hundred and thirty-nine days, and a lock coping is not a building and there is lime in the joints nine feet away from it.** The carrier in the sack, **not rung, ninety-five days from the seventy-seventh, and I have not put my hand on it.** My own bell is in a sealed yard in Kells and the bar is on the bell and not on the licence, and it does not travel. **Her bell is in her bag and it is not rung and nobody asked her for a reason at any hour today, including me.**
>
> *The register is thirteen. **I have produced nothing and asked for nothing and been given nothing and taken nothing, and nobody was thanked today.**
>
> *Which office is it. **A coping, nine men, a dressed sill out of a gateway nobody will name, and a fourth column in nine books that nine clerks will not fill, because a fourth column wants a person with a standing and a bench is not a person.**
