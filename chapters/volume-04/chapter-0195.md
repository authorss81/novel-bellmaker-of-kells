# Chapter 0195 — Nine Deck Boards Off a Landing and Dressed Stone Into the Water

It was the fourth hour of the hundred and eighty-first day and Garret Poole was standing in the middle of a landing stage with a spade in his hand, throwing out nine inches of river that had come up through the boards overnight, and the landing stage was a hundred foot of plank on forty legs over mud and the water at the low end of it was seven foot deep, and Tomas Vale came down the steps at the top end and stopped where the plank was sound.

"Forty legs," Poole said, without looking round. "Nine of them are soft at the foot. I have known that for four years and I have told a bench about it twice and a bench is not a thing you can tell about a leg."

"Then somebody is standing on a hundred foot of plank on nine soft legs."

"Somebody is standing on it. So am I. That is what a landing is for." He threw another spadeful out and the water went back in. "There is a barge against the outside of the low end of me with nine tons of dressed stone on her and she is riding high because nine tons is nothing on a hull that size, and I have had her there two days waiting for the water to turn so that a man at the fourth bench can get a gang plank onto her, and if the water is where it is at the sixth hour I am not going to get that plank on and the stone is stone and the bench wants it by the turn of the year."

"You have nine boards spare."

"I have no boards spare. I have had no boards spare for four years. That is nine boards I am about to have." He leaned the spade and looked at Tomas, and he was the sort of man who looked at a barrow and did arithmetic. "You are the man off the flat. Say the words. I have had them twice at the top of those steps and I have not had them here, and if I am going to have them here I would like them before the ones who have not come yet."

Tomas said them, in the order he always said them, standing at the top end of a hundred foot of plank on nine soft legs, to Garret Poole and to nine other people who were on a landing stage at the fourth hour of a morning because a landing stage is where the boats are and that is the whole of what a landing stage is.

He said them and he did not add anything to the end of them, and he had learned that on the day before yesterday from a woman with a rope round her neck, and one of the nine other people said, "Right," and went off up the steps at a fast walk, and another of them said nothing and stood there and looked at the river.

---

At about the fifth hour thirty people came down to the landing stage, and they came down it in a body and not in ones, and Tomas Vale was at the top of the steps and could not get to the other end of a hundred foot of plank because there was thirty people on it.

They were not running. That was the thing he wrote down afterwards and the thing he could not get past for three days. Nobody was running, and nobody was shouting, and what they were doing was exactly what four people had told him on the day before yesterday in three different trades, which was carrying.

An old man went up the steps first with a woman of about seventy and a child, because the bank is nine foot and a hundred and forty foot of it is the only high ground at the water's edge in the low quarter, and the old man had a stick and the woman had a bag and the child was carried. And a boy of fifteen went in the river to knee deep and got under the end board of the stage and worked the nails out with the claw of a cold chisel he had brought with him, because he had thought about it on the way down, which was a thing Tomas had not credited a person with until that morning.

It took him four minutes to lift nine boards. And with the ninth board out, the low end of the landing stage was a nine-foot hole with the river running through it at seven foot of depth, and the hole was where the boats came alongside, and the current through nine foot of new opening was faster than the current at the side of it had been for about a minute, and the water in the middle of a landing stage is never the water at the edge of one.

The barge went in four minutes later, and nobody hit her, and Tomas Vale was fifty feet away at the top of the steps and could not tell afterwards whether the stage moved or the water did.

She lifted at the bow, which is what a hull with nine tons of dressed stone in her bottom does when the bottom of her comes out from under her, and she lifted at the bow and rolled about a third of the way and went over the other way, and she was not deep and she was not gone in about a minute, but nine tons of dressed stone went into seven foot of river and stayed there, and it stayed there because a dressed stone is a dressed stone and a river is a river and there is no way of picking nine tons of dressed stone off the bottom of a landing stage with a boat and a rope and nine men and no crane.

Thirty people were standing in nine inches of water on a hundred foot of plank with a nine-foot hole at the end of it, and they got off it and up the steps and onto a bank of nine foot, and nobody drowned, and a woman lost a basket and a boy of fifteen lost a cold chisel and a man lost a hat.

They were nine minutes getting up the bank, and it went the way a thing goes when everybody in it already agrees, which is that the old ones went first and the strong ones at the back shouted the old ones along and the ones in the middle carried the boards and did not drop them, and there was a woman of about seventy who got to the top of ninety foot of bank and sat down on the grass and would not go up the last fifty feet, and four people stood with her on the wet grass while the rest of it went on, and nobody made her.

What the boy of fifteen said, to nobody in particular, and to Tomas, was that he had thought about it on the way down, and that it was not clever, because anybody could have thought about it, and that in four years of nobody doing it he had a cold chisel in his hand and four minutes of work. Then he went up the bank after the boards with nine other people and did not say anything else, and Tomas Vale did not follow him, because a man of fifteen who has just done a thing he has never done before does not need a stranger to come and talk to him about it on the steps.

Tomas got to the top of the steps at about the sixth hour and stood there and did not go down, because there was nothing at the bottom of it he could do that a man with a barrow could be useful at, and he had in his coat a sheet of paper that he could not read about a schedule that had no column for a town.

At about the seventh hour Garret Poole came up the steps past him and stopped, and Poole was not a man who stopped.

"That was nine tons of stone," he said. "That is two seasons of hauling for me and a bench that wanted it by the turn of the year, and the rate of that stone is in the rate for this reach and every cart on nine miles pays it and the stone is in the river. That is what you have done. I am not saying it to be cruel to you, and I would have said it the same way to any man who came down those steps this morning and said those four words, and I would have said it to the man at the bench before him, and I would have said it to the man who put the fitting on the cart bridge gate and did not tell me, and I would have said it to anybody, and there is nobody in this town I could not say it to this evening, and I have said it to you because you are standing here and I would have had to walk past you to say it to anybody else."

"That is right."

"It is right and I want you to have it in front of you and not in a book somewhere in forty years." Poole shifted the spade. "And I want to tell you the other half of it, and I have been standing on a landing stage thinking about it for an hour, and here it is. I have had no boards spare for four years. Four years. And in four years there have been four times when thirty people came down here with things to carry, and I have had nine boards every one of those four times and I did not know it, and I have told a bench twice about nine legs being soft and there is no form on which a man tells a bench that a leg is soft. **I could have had the boards out of that stage and up the bank myself in a morning. That is the whole of it and it is a fact about nine boards and four years and it is not about you at all.**"

He went up the bank. He did not ask Tomas for anything and he did not say he was not angry, and Tomas did not say he was sorry, because a man who says he is sorry to a man who has just lost nine tons of dressed stone is making the other man's morning about himself, and Tomas Vale had learned that in four counties and a city.

At about the ninth hour of the evening he was on the flat with the barrow behind him and the four bolts made fast, and the water was about a foot and a half over the highest iron and still going, and a landing stage at the top of the town had a nine-foot hole in the end of it and would have one until a carpenter came, and a barge was sitting upright in seven foot of water with nine tons of dressed stone under her, and thirty people from the low quarter were on a bank a hundred and forty foot above the low quarter with nine boards under them and everything they had not carried.

> *All five clauses stand. **Nothing rung, by me or by her, and nothing bound, and nothing counted into iron, and no stage, and no perception attempted and none had, and the warrant stands, and a fitting is not called anything it is not.** Four bolts made fast, and **the frame has not been in a building since the thirty-third, a hundred and forty-eight days, and a hundred foot of plank on forty legs is not a building, and I stood at the top end of it and nothing was struck and nothing could be.** The carrier in the sack, **not rung, a hundred and four days from the seventy-seventh.** My own bell is in a sealed yard in Kells and the bar is on the bell and not on the licence, and it does not travel. **Her bell is in her bag and it is not rung and nobody asked her for a reason at any hour today, including me.**

> *The register is thirteen. **I have produced nothing and asked for nothing and been given nothing and taken nothing, and nobody was thanked today, and nobody blamed me either, and both of those are the same absence.** I was on this road with her forty-five days and did not ask her what it was for, and she was not on the landing and I did not send anybody to see whether she was on a bank with thirty people on it.
