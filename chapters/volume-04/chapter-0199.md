# Chapter 0199 — Four Men in a Bench Room and a Frame They Can All See

It was the fourth hour of the hundred and eighty-fifth day and Harlan Loxley was putting a road sheet flat on a bench-room table and weighing it down with eleven iron weights out of a press in a shed at the upper bench, four of them duplicates of bench weights and seven of them not anything else on this river and never anything else on this river, and a bench-room table in a room with a stove in it is the only table in nine miles a paper will lie flat on.

Nine benches' people were in the room. It is thirty foot by twenty with a stove, a long table, a bench on each side and a rate book on the table, and it is not an office and has no name on a form anywhere, and all nine benches use it because it is there and always has been.

Tomas Vale stood nine feet off the long side of the table with his barrow in a yard outside and the four bolts made fast on a stone, and he had not put the sheet down and had not asked anybody to look at it. The sheet had come out of his coat at the third hour because a man at the door said *put that on the table and let us look at it* and then said *I did not say that* and did not say it again.

"It has been on four tables," Loxley said, with both hands flat on the paper, "in a hundred and forty days, and four men have looked at it and none of them could read a run, and I could not see the place where it stops and I told him why and I have not changed my mind about that in a hundred and forty days and I am not going to today." He took his hands off. "I can see the frame."

He put a finger two inches in from the left edge and ran it down the sheet to nine inches from the bottom and did not cross anything.

"A mould frame has an outside and an inside, and everything in a frame is about the frame and nothing in it is about the thing until it is out of the frame and cooled. I have been looking at that sheet for a hundred and forty days as a drawing of a road, and it is not one." He weighted a corner that was lifting. "**That is a frame. That rectangle is not a hole in a road. That rectangle is a bay in a frame that nothing was run into, and it is in the same place in the frame because it is the same frame, and I do not know what was not run into it and I am not going to guess and I would like to be asked in about nine years.**"

Nobody asked him then and nobody asked him in nine years and the bay is not explained and is not going to be.

---

At about the fifth hour Perry Nunn came round the table, because Merrit Sallow had said *Perry, there is a figure at the top of that column and I want to know whether you have got it anywhere*, and Nunn was a bench clerk of eleven years who had never filled a fourth column and was not starting on a paper he did not write, and it was Sallow who had asked and not Tomas Vale, and Tomas was nine feet off the table and did not open his mouth.

"I have got it," Nunn said, and put the rate book down and did not open it, which is a thing about him. "In the corner of a reach book, , and it is the sheet's own number. I did not join the two and I am not joining them now. Joining the two is not a thing anybody does."

"I have asked you to do one thing."

"I have said I have not done it and I have not done it, and I will tell you what I have done instead, and it took me nine minutes." He put two fingers on the sheet without touching it. "**This sheet is a return and not a road. A road sheet is drawn to a scale and has a scale on it and a road does not have a number in the corner of it. A return has a number in the corner of it because a return is a thing that was filled in and filed and a return wants to be found again. That is a bench clerk's job and I am a bench clerk.** So the panels on it are not places. They are answers. And a panel at the head of this sheet has a keeper in it, and a keeper is a person who is answered for, and a panel halfway down has a rate in it and a return in it and nothing else, and a panel at the bottom third has a crew in it and a figure of mouths and nothing else at all, and the columns are not places, they are obligations, and the number at the head of each column is the number of the thing that is owed."

"And the ones that are not there?"

"The ones that are not there are the ones with no keeper in them and no return in them and no crew in them, and they have been gone from every copy in the same bay in the same frame since before the four of us were in a room together, and I have never had a reason to ask about it because a bench is not a district and a reach is not a sheet and the sheet is a return that a district's office keeps and a bench gets a copy of once in a while for a rate."

Nobody said anything for about a minute, and then Merrit Sallow put down a chalk.

"That is the fourth question in nine years I have asked that came back with an answer I did not expect, and all four were asked in this room." He put his hand flat on the reach book. "The rate went up four years ago for the upper sill and again after that, and I have asked twice and got an answer about carts, and the figure in the corner of this book is that sheet's number, and I have never put the two side by side, because there is no form on which a weighmaster writes down that he has."

He put the two side by side, on a bench-room table, at about the sixth hour, and Tomas Vale watched him do it from nine feet off and could not have told anybody afterward how it was done.

"The panels at the head are dense," Sallow said. "Keeper, return, rate, keeper, and a box for a reason with a line under it and nothing printed over it. And going down the reach they get thinner, and then rate and crew, and then crew and a figure of mouths, and then nothing." He put the chalk down and picked it up again. "**Every panel that has gone has gone off an end, and what is in the place of a panel that has gone is a rate. The rate is not a price for a cart. The rate is the only thing left of the places that stopped being answered for, and it is spread along nine miles on four hundred and forty carts, and that is the whole of why a cart on this river has got more expensive four times in four years and no man on it can say what for.**"

The room did not make a noise. Nine benches' people looked at a sheet of paper.

"And the head of the sheet," Sallow said. "I have never been. You have not, Perry. I do not know that a man could not go and I have not asked. **What a sheet says about the head of the sheet is not what anybody in this room has seen, and I have nine years of rate in this rate book and every bit of it is a piece of that same thing, and I am not going up there on a morning like this.**"

---

At about the seventh hour the thing the nine benches do with a gate came up, because nine men were in the room and somebody said the word *command*, and it took about a minute and a half, and Tomas Vale had heard a version of it on a coping four days before and heard it again here and neither time did anybody raise their voice.

Two benches would share the mending; that was not in question, they had shared a sill, a rope and a week. What was in question was a man over it, and the answer was a fact about a habit and not about a principle: a gate nobody owns gets mended, and a gate a man owns does not, and those are the same gate in about nine years, and every gate on this river has gone two hundred years with nobody standing over it, and a man in charge wants to be asked, and the asking goes in a book, and a thing about a gate in a book stops.

Tomas asked the same question at two benches on purpose, and wrote both answers on one line in his own book with a bar between them, and carried neither to the other man.

The man from the second bench said, "The bench that mends it pays out of the rate, and the rate is set in the room by nine men, and that is all."

The man from the sixth bench said, "Whoever is on the reach that week takes it round, and in four years nobody will know and the sill will still be up."

Both are true. The difference between them is the whole of what a central command is, and he had written two numbers on that line four days ago and would write two more in nine years and would still not carry one to the other, and he is not the clerk of it, he keeps no terms, and nobody in that room knows he asked anybody at all.

Cobb Arley was at the back of the room. He lives on the water, nine benches turn up and nobody counts who turns up, and four hundred yards above him on a bank there are four doors, and he had said nothing for about an hour.

At about the ninth hour of the evening the room had emptied and the bench-room table had a road sheet on it and eleven iron weights on it, and Tomas Vale folded the sheet in three along the creases it already had and put it in the inside pocket of his coat, and the rectangle was where it had been for fifty-one days, and nobody thanked anybody.

"You have all got a frame and no keeper in part of it," he said, to nobody. "I have got a line and four doors on a bank and no box anywhere in this kingdom that says what happens to four doors if the man holding the line stops, and I was asked in the spring in words with no form, and I said I would, and I have not been up the bank." He looked at the sheet. "That is not a finding and I am not going to call it one, and I am not going to be the man who says we have found it, because I have been at the head of a reach that is not on that paper and have still not found out what the top of the frame is for."

> *The hundred and eighty-fifth, at about the ninth hour of the evening. A bench room, a stove, a long table, nine benches, thirty people, eleven iron weights, a sheet, a frame, a bay two hands together, a reach book, a figure in a corner, keeper, return, rate, crew, nine miles, four hundred and forty carts, two bars on one line, four doors, four hundred yards, four bolts.*
>
> *Second column. **Four tables in a hundred and forty days, four men looking, and not one of them can read a run. I could not see where it stops and I told him why and I am not changing my mind today. I can see the frame.** A mould frame is a frame, and everything in one is about the frame and nothing in it is about the thing until the thing is out of the frame and cooled. **A man who makes a thing out of a drawing does not see a drawing stop, because his whole trade is a place where the marks stop. The rectangle is a bay that nothing was run into, and it is in the same place in every copy because it is the same frame, and I do not know what, and I would like to be asked in about nine years.** Nobody asked him then or in nine years.
>
> *Second column, what a clerk said. **The sheet is a return and not a road, and the difference is the number in the corner of it: a road has a scale on it and no number, and a return has a number because a return is a thing that was filled in and filed and wants finding again.** So the panels are not places, they are answers. A keeper in a panel is a person somebody is answerable for. Halfway down there is a rate and nothing else. In the bottom third there is a crew and a figure of mouths and nothing else at all. **The panels with nothing in them have no keeper in them, and the ones that are gone are gone from every copy in the same bay in the same frame, and he has never asked, because a bench is not a district.** A weighmaster: **dense at the top with keepers and returns and a box for a reason with a line under it and nothing printed over it, and then thinner, and then rate and crew, and then crew and a figure of mouths, and then nothing.**
>
> *Third column, ruled apart. **A sheet that is a list of what is owed and not a list of where things are. Keeper in a panel is a person answered for. No keeper in a panel, no panel, and a rate in the place of it. The rate is not a price for a cart and I have been wrong about that for about nine years. Four hundred and forty carts on nine miles paying for nine panels that are not there, and the price of a cart going up four times in four years, and him at the beam asking twice and being told about carts.** He put the two side by side himself and I was nine feet off and I did not put the sheet down. **What a sheet says about the head of the sheet is not what anybody in that room has seen, and neither of us has been.**
>
> *All five clauses stand. **Nothing rung, by me or by her, and nothing bound, and nothing counted into iron, and no stage, and no perception attempted and none had, and the warrant stands, and a fitting is not called anything it is not.** Four bolts made fast, and **the frame has not been in a building since the thirty-third, a hundred and fifty-two days, and a bench room is a building and the barrow was in the yard on a stone the whole three hours.** The carrier in the sack, **not rung, a hundred and eight days from the seventy-seventh, and I have not put my hand on it.** My own bell is in a sealed yard in Kells and the bar is on the bell and not on the licence, and it does not travel. **Her bell is in her bag and nobody asked her for a reason at any hour today, including me.**
>
> *The register is thirteen. **I have produced nothing and asked for nothing and been given nothing and taken nothing, and nobody was thanked today, and a sheet that was on four tables went onto a fifth and came off it in my own hands, and a drawing is not a document.** I was on this road with her forty-nine days and did not ask her what it was for.
>
