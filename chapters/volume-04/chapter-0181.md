# Chapter 0181 — A Carton of Sacking and One Thumb on a Fold

It was the third hour of the hundred and sixty-seventh day and the beam on the cart bridge was only right in the cold, which was why Pell Okell was standing under it with one hand under a pan waiting for the pan to stop moving, and Tomas Vale came up the slope behind the barrow and stopped short of the stones because a man waiting on a balance is not a man you talk to.

The bridge was two arches and a timber deck with the planking worn pale in the wheel lines and dark at the kerb, and the beam stood on an oak post at the near end with a hood of three boards and a half over it to keep the rain off the book's ledge. There was a weight-box of dressed stone on a bar, a book open face down on the ledge, and the man who kept the beam was a long quiet fellow who said good morning once and then went back to his own business and did not say it again.

Pell had a hundred and sixweight of sacking on the near pan and the weight-box on the far one and he was moving his hand a finger's width at a time under the pan, which is not a thing a man does when he is in a hurry.

"You are standing in my light," he said.

"I am standing on your bridge," Tomas said.

"You are standing in my light and there is a difference." Pell got the pan to sit still and took his hand away. "That is a hundred and six. It is a hundred and four if the far stone is the one I think it is, and it is the one I think it is, and I do not want to hear about the other one."

A man who has spent his life inside a beam does not know what it is until somebody asks him what the number was. Pell had nine hundred crossings of it behind him and every one of them in the order it happened.

At about the fourth hour the sacking went over the deck in four loads and the beam came off its post and went up the slope on Pell's shoulder, which is how a beam is moved when there is no cart free, and the man who keeps the beam let him take it and offered nothing and said nothing, which is a thing a man does when he has decided somebody else is not going to be long in the district.

Tomas set the barrow down on the bridge's parapet shelf where the stones were dry, and it was a shelf a cart could not use and a barrow fitted, and he made the four bolts fast on it. The frame in its cradle sat level on the shelf and the reinforced padding took the road out of it. Nothing in the frame could strike anything. He had a sheet folded in three in the inside pocket of his coat and he took it out.

---

He did not ask.

He put it down flat on the plank table at the near end, under the hood, where the beam's book was kept, and he put his hand flat on it once to settle the crease, and then he took his hand off and stepped back about four feet and stood with his back against the parapet and waited.

Pell came back over the deck for his own book, because a man who keeps a beam has a book whether anybody reads it or not.

He saw the paper and stopped.

"That is a road sheet," he said.

"Yes."

"Then put it somewhere it is not going to be rained on, because it is not going to be rained on, and because the wind on this bridge comes up off the water in the middle of the day and takes a wet sheet and puts it in the river." He came the rest of the way. "I am not opening it."

"I did not ask you to open it."

"You did not ask me anything at all, which is the first thing anybody has done on this bridge in about nine years." He came up to the table and stopped on the far side of it and did not put his hands on it. He stood there the way a man stands at a gate he is not sure of.

Tomas said nothing, and he had practised saying nothing at about four inches long for a hundred and thirty-six days, and it was the only instrument he owned that cost him nothing to use.

Pell looked at it for a while. Then he said, "I cannot read that."

"I know."

"You do not know that, you have not asked." Pell put his thumb on the edge of the table. "I have carried four of them for a district. Two hundred in a cart, forty on my back, and I have never opened one. They are for a man in a room with a book on a shelf to know what he has got to argue with, when a road is disputed. A sheet is not a road. It is a thing to argue with."

He said that without any weight on it at all, the way a man says the price of rope, and then he stopped, and he looked at the sheet again for a bit.

"That bit there," he said. "On the crease, the second one along. That one is not folded."

Tomas did not move.

"The rest of it has been folded and opened and folded and opened a great many times and the edges of it stand up a little where the damp gets into them, and you can see that from here with the light behind it." He moved his thumb along the edge of the table without touching the paper. "That bit lies down. It has not been folded. Everything else on that sheet has been folded and folded and it has come up each time and it lies down, and that one lies down, and I would not know that about a book but I know it about a paper because I have had a wet paper in a cart going over a bad road for nine years and you learn where a thing has been let go of and where it has not."

"That is a rectangle about two hands together," Tomas said. "It is a third of the way along a run and it is in the same place in every copy of this sheet anybody has ever had, and the rules that rule the panels either side of it stop short and do not cross it, and the ink either side of it is two blacks that are not the same black, and the numbers either side go on without a break."

Pell did not look at the rectangle then he looked at the rectangle.

"You have had it a long time," he said.

"Thirty-four days."

"Then you will go on having it a long time," he said, "and I will tell you what a carter can tell you about it and it is not what is in it. That bit has not been folded. Somebody has been careful with it or somebody has been told to be careful with it, and I have no way of knowing which, because it is not my paper and I have never once in my life been the man who knows about a piece of paper."

"Then you have got nothing off this table," Pell said, and put his hand on the beam post to lean on, which is what the post is for. "And I am a man who is late."

He was, and he said so without being asked, and it was the second thing of his own that he had put on the table that morning and neither of them was about the road. A gate at the top of a lane about two miles on where a man was loading hops, and the man did his own loading when the carter was not there when he said he would be, and did not ask that carter again that week. One of two men out and the other at home with a chest. On that beam since the third hour because a beam is only right in the cold, and he had wanted the whole of it over the deck before the sun came on the arch.

"You have been at this bridge about a quarter of an hour," he said. "I have been counting it off the sun coming round the hood, which is what I do instead of a watch. That is a quarter of an hour of a morning I do not have, and I am not going to say that to the man at the gate, because he does not need to know where it went, and I am not going to say it to you either, and I have already said it, which is twice more than I said anything yesterday."

Tomas took the sheet up off the table. He folded it in three along the creases it already had, which took him a moment because the second crease did not want to take, and he put it back in the inside pocket of his coat.

"You did not ask me to look at it," Pell said.

"No."

"You put it down and you stood over there and you waited."

"Yes."

"That is not how anybody does anything on this road." Pell shouldered the beam. "A man with a paper and a barrow comes up to a beam and asks a carter to read it, or he does not stop, and there is no third thing that has ever happened here. What I wanted with it was to tell you about a crease, and that is what you have got, and I hope it is worth the quarter of an hour."

He went up the slope with the beam on his shoulder. He did not say good morning back, because the good morning had been used up.

---

At about the fifth hour Tomas made the four bolts fast again where the frame was standing, which took him four minutes and is the whole of the second clause of a receipt in five clauses, all five standing.

Two of the four were not on the road behind him. Isa Trevail had gone up at the second hour with a barrow of lime and no yoke, which she did twice in three days and had told nobody why, and Ned Adley had gone down to a soft place at the lower ford with a hod and would be at the lock ground by the fifth hour of the next day, so the road above the bridge had Tomas on it and nobody else, and that was the shape of the morning and he had not made it.

He stood after Pell had gone and looked at the water going under the second arch, and worked out the thing about the table, which took him about four minutes and was not worth what it cost.

He was going to put the sheet on four tables and step back, and he was not going to ask anybody to look at it, and he was not going to tell one of them what he wanted off it, and he was not going to thank any of them afterwards, and he was not going to be able to pay any of them, because a barrow is not currency and a barrow of sacking is a barrow of sacking.

And the instrument had just shown him its own size, because a carter had spent a quarter of an hour of a morning he did not have, unasked, and had come away with a crease and a warning about a gate, and neither of those had been asked for.

He had been on this road with her thirty-one days. He had not asked her once what the road was for and he was not going to, and he put the figure in his book that night with the others, and it went up by one every day the way a stone goes up a barrow when you have nothing to put in it.

At about the ninth hour of the evening he was on a flat of beaten earth above the water with the barrow behind him and the four bolts made fast, and the wind off the river came up the bank and put the smell of a lime stand nine hundred yards below him into the air, and the bridge with the beam on it was behind and below and no longer had anything on its table.

> *The hundred and sixty-seventh, at about the ninth hour of the evening. A cart bridge, two arches, a beam on an oak post, a hood of three boards and a half, a weight-box, a book on a ledge, a plank table, a flat.*
>
> *Second column. A hundred and six weight of sacking, weighed, and a hundred and four if the far stone is not the one he thinks it is. Four loads over the deck. The beam carried up the slope on a shoulder, because no cart was free. One of two men out and the other at home with a chest. A gate at the top of a lane about two miles on, loading hops, and a man there who does his own loading when the carter is not there when he said he would be and does not ask again that week. Nine hundred crossings of that beam and they are in his head and not in a book.*
>
> *Second column, the paper. A road sheet out of the inside pocket of my coat, put down flat on the plank table, and **I did not ask him to look at it and I stepped back about four feet and said nothing for as long as it took him to do whatever he wanted with it.** He did not open it. He has carried four for a district and has never opened one, and a sheet is not a road, it is a thing to argue with. He put his thumb along the edge of the table and never touched the paper: **the whole sheet has been folded and opened and the edges stand up where the damp gets into them, and the third crease along is not folded and lies down.** I did not tell him about the rectangle and he found the same place.*
>
> *And what I have got off the table is a fact about him and not about the sheet: a quarter of an hour of a morning he does not have, told to a man at a gate nobody needs to be told about, said twice more than yesterday. He said he hoped it was worth it. I did not tell him it was and I am not going to, and he did not do it for me.*
>
> *Third column, ruled apart. A sheet is not a road and a road does not need a drawing, and the only instrument in this kingdom that cannot be edited is a person on foot, and it costs a day, and the day belongs to whoever gives it. I put it down. I stepped back. **A crease is what a drawing and a carter have in common so far, and a crease is not an answer, and I have spent a quarter of an hour of a cold morning finding out that I already knew that and had hoped otherwise.**
>
> *All five clauses stand. **Nothing rung, by me or by her, and nothing bound, and nothing counted into iron, and no stage, and no perception attempted and none had, and the warrant stands, and a fitting is not called anything it is not.** Four bolts made fast, and **the frame has not been in a building since the thirty-third, a hundred and thirty-four days, and a plank table under three boards and a half of hood is not a building; I put the sheet on it and took it off it myself.** The carrier in the sack, **not rung, ninety days from the seventy-seventh, and I have not put my hand on it.** My own bell is in a sealed yard in Kells and the bar is on the bell and not on the licence, and it does not travel. **Her bell is in her bag and it is not rung and nobody asked her for a reason at any hour today, including me.**
>
> *The register is thirteen. **I have produced nothing and asked for nothing and been given nothing and taken nothing, and nobody was thanked today. A sheet going onto a stranger's table and coming off it in my own hands is not a fourteenth.**
>
> *Which office is it. **It is a beam, a hood, a book nobody reads, a weight-box of dressed stone, and a table a paper can be put on for a quarter of an hour by a man who has nine hundred crossings of that beam in his head and not one of them in a book.**
