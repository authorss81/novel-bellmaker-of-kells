# Chapter 0192 — Three Households Below the Bank and Three Things to Do About Water

It was the fourth hour of the hundred and seventy-eighth day and Isa Trevail was at a lime stand with a barrow and a yoke, and the stand was six boards and a lean-to in the low quarter of Hallowmere with a heap under a sacking cover held down with stones, and Tomas Vale came down the stone steps behind the barrow and stopped at the top of the third one because the bottom four were under about a foot and a half of the river going past sideways.

"That is the stand on the other bank of this reach," Isa Trevail said, without turning round, "and I have carried to it for two years, and I know the price on it to the penny, and you are stood in my light."

"I am stood in your water."

"You are stood in both and one of them is mine." She set the barrow down and put her yoke against the lean-to. "Nine loads of my own on that yoke by the sixth hour and not eight, and the ninth is against a wall at the upper stand, nine foot higher than this one. I am paid for the load when it is off the yoke and not before, and the man up there is a good man and he pays it, and if the water is over those steps by then I am not going to be on that wall and he is not going to have a load and I am not going to have the money and nobody in that arrangement is at fault."

"How much of it is the water."

"About a foot and a half, and the good ground here is about a foot above the river, so that is a step and a foot of room, and I have got about an hour." She hooked two baskets onto the yoke. "And I am going to say a thing once and not again this week. Four days of pay owed me by a man at the upper landing, and he is not a bad man, he has had a bad month with the stone, and I have never once said so out loud to And if the water is over those steps I will be in his yard an hour later asking for it with my boots full of this town's water, and I have not decided whether I am going to do that."

She went up the yoke. Tomas took the other end of the bar without being asked, and they went up nine foot of bank in one go and set the baskets down, and she counted them off on her fingers and did not say the number out loud.

---

At about the fifth hour he was in the low quarter, and two hundred and forty people down there do not have a street, they have a ground, and the ground is wet grey clay with a channel cut through the middle of it that a man called Farrow cut in the spring and has not been paid for.

Obin Farrow was on his knees at a sluice board with a mallet and a bit of oak, and he had nine foot of board in three pieces in front of him and none of it in place, and he was the sort of man a stranger would not have identified as anybody in particular except for the fact that he was working in the middle of a flood.

"You will be the man off the flat with the barrow."

"I am."

"Then you have come down here to tell us to move." Farrow got the mallet back on the oak and hit it twice and the nail went in about a finger's depth and stopped. "I will tell you the whole of it and then you can go and tell whoever you are telling. The rate on this reach is set by weight and every cart on nine miles pays it, and a month ago the town put eleven days of cutting out to me with no date on it and not a penny paid. That channel is the eleven days. There is no money in it. If you flood this ground on the word of a man with a barrow then the channel fills and the board goes and I do the same eleven days again on wet ground, and I cannot put it in a return because a return wants feet of ditch and I have cut a channel, and I am paid by the load like everybody on this reach and the load is not there twice."

"And if it does not flood."

"Then I cut the channel again in the autumn and I am owed it twice and I will take the second one." He hit the nail again. "I am not saying go out. I am saying there is no money in this ground and there is money in nine feet of boards I am holding together, and you do not pour nine feet of boards into a town for a man you met this morning."

Tomas did not argue with him, because he had no answer and because Farrow was right about the eleven days, and being right was not a door.

At about the sixth hour the water came up over the bottom step and made the stair into a fall of nine foot and standing on it was a man in his shirtsleeves with a spade who wanted to know what a barrow on his steps was for. His name was Trayle and he was a lighterman and he had nine fathoms of mooring in a barge at the top of the town and no house on the low quarter and a room in it for two people and a kitchen in a yard.

"Two years I have wanted somebody to come down those steps and tell me this town is going under," Trayle said. "Two years and nobody came. My family is on the flat and it is the flattest ground on this river and there is a bank on it that has gone two foot since the spring because somebody cut the channel you are looking at and moved the silt somewhere, and when the water comes down past that bank it has nothing to run against, and the water knows that and I do not need anybody to tell me anything." He leaned the spade on the wall. "I take stone up on the turn and I have been idle since the frost and I will not be idle in a wet month. I am telling you that as a fact about my trade and not as a favour to anybody. That low quarter can have tonight if it wants it. I have nine fathoms of rope out and a boy on the top landing who can pole a flat-bottomed thing off a flat that is going under, and if the yard behind these steps wants the ground on the flat at eight o'clock, my boy will take anybody who walks down to him, and I will do it for nothing because it is my ground and I have not been asked anything yet."

The fourth of them was a woman on a step thirty yards further down, out because her door was on the passage and a barrow going past a door is a thing a household looks at. She had two people in a room at the back of that house, the lowest room in Hallowmere, and she had been at the front door since the fourth hour.

"I moved them up once in my life," she said, to Tomas, and she did not name them. "Nine years ago, when my man was in the town and the water came, and I carried two of them up nine foot of stair in my arms and put them in a church and got the church shut and could not get back in for six hours, and I have never done it again because I have not had to, and I am not going to be told by a man off a flat with a barrow that I am going to have to do it again with what I have got this time." She put her hand on the door frame. "Go and tell whoever you are telling that I am not going and that I have not been asked anything and that I am not waiting for anybody to ask me."

Nobody in that ground was wicked, and every one of the three was right, and Tomas Vale stood on a wet step with a barrow behind him and could not add the three together into anything.

That was the part that took him the whole of the sixth hour and most of the seventh, and it was not a thing about the water. **Get out** was one sentence and it was three instructions, and to Farrow it meant get out of a job he was owed, and to the lighterman it meant get out of a flat he wanted, and to the woman at the door it meant get out of a house she had already got out of once. Say it in a yard and a man hears his landlord, and say it in a room and a man hears the last person who had any business in it. A signal that flattened them was a false thing, and he had no way of unflattening it and no other instrument.

---

At about the seventh hour he was back on the flat of beaten earth above the water with the barrow behind him and the four bolts made fast, and the water had gone over the top of the mark on Trope's post and had gone over it by four inches at the seventh hour, and Abel Trope was on the boards three foot under with his launch on a painter and would not come off.

Tomas Vale took the schedule out of the inside pocket of his coat, and unfolded it along the three creases it already had, and put his knee on the corner, and looked at a column of numbers and a panel with a rate in it and about a third of the way along a run a rectangle two hands together with nothing in it.

What he had was a number. Nine foot of river in four days, and the water two years back on the second mark, and four inches over the highest iron at the seventh hour, and about an hour and a quarter of a night in front of it in which a river on this reach has gone up nine foot four times in nine years. He could not tell whether the town at the foot of those steps was off the sheet because it was not owed anything, or because the sheet had never had a column for a town.

At about the ninth hour of the evening he was on the flat of beaten earth with the barrow behind him and the four bolts made fast, the water holding over the top of the mark on Trope's post, and three answers in three trades' grammars in his own book, and he had not added them.
>
> *The hundred and seventy-eighth, at about the ninth hour of the evening. A lime stand, six boards, a lean-to, a heap, a basket that is a size, nine stone steps with four under, a sluice board, a channel, a spade, a door, a flat, a post with the water over the highest iron, a barrow, four bolts.*
>
> *Second column. **Nine loads by the sixth hour and not eight, paid for when the load is off the yoke, and the water means no load and no money and nobody at fault.** And I said a thing once and am not saying it again this week: **four days of pay owed me at the upper landing, never said out loud to anybody, and I will be in his yard an hour after the steps go under asking for it with my boots full of this town's water.** I counted nine baskets and did not say the number. He took the other end of the bar without being asked and neither of us said so.
>
> *Second column, the three. **Farrow: eleven days of cutting with no date and no penny, a channel through two hundred and forty people's ground, and a return that wants feet of ditch when what he has done is a channel. He is not saying go out. He is saying there is no money in this ground and there is money in nine foot of boards.** **Trayle: nine fathoms of mooring, no house here, a bank gone two foot since the spring, idle since the frost and not going to be idle in a wet month, and his boy will take anybody who walks down to him for nothing.** The woman at the fourth door: two people in the lowest room in Hallowmere, carried up nine foot of stair into a church nine years ago and shut out of it for six hours. **I am not going and I have not been asked anything and I am not waiting for anybody to ask me.**
>
> *Third column, ruled apart. **Four sentences in three trades' grammars and the three of them do not fit one another, and I did not add them up, and it took the sixth hour and most of the seventh to find that out.** A signal that flattens three households into one thing is a false thing, and I have no instrument that is not a mouth. What I have is a true number: nine foot in four days, four inches over the highest iron at the seventh hour, an hour and a quarter of night in front of it. **A number nobody else has is not a warning. The only thing here that cannot be edited between a man and a man is legs and a mouth, and it costs a day, and the day belongs to whoever gives it, and I have four people on this road and not one of them has offered me one.**
>
> *All five clauses stand. **Nothing rung, by me or by her, and nothing bound, and nothing counted into iron, and no stage, and no perception attempted and none had, and the warrant stands, and a fitting is not called anything it is not.** Four bolts made fast, and **the frame has not been in a building since the thirty-third, a hundred and forty-five days, and a lime stand with six boards and a lean-to is not a building, and I stood under that roof for nine minutes and the sheet never came out of my coat.** The carrier in the sack, **not rung, a hundred and one days from the seventy-seventh.** My own bell is in a sealed yard in Kells and the bar is on the bell and not on the licence, and it does not travel. **Her bell is in her bag and nobody asked her for a reason at any hour today, including me.**
>
> *The register is thirteen. **I have produced nothing and asked for nothing and been given nothing and taken nothing, and nobody was thanked today. I did not take the same words to three people and call the result a count: I asked one man what he would lose and I did not say his answer to either of the other two.** I was on this road with her forty-two days and did not ask her what it was for, and I am not going to.
>
> *Which office is it. **A channel with no date on it, nine foot of boards, a lighterman's painter, and a woman at a door who has not been asked anything.**
