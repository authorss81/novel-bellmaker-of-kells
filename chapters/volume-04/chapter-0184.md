# Chapter 0184 — Harlan Loxley's Mould Shed at the Head of the Plank

The fourth hour of the hundred and seventieth day belonged to Harlan Loxley, who was in a shed at the head of a landing with a lofting frame in front of him and a drawing-board propped against the end post, and he was laying off a line on a batten with a bevel and a straightedge and did not stop for anybody. Behind him, up the plank, a barge was taking on stone with a gang of nine and a chain through a ring in a post, and the chain came taut every few minutes and the whole hundred feet of it knocked.

Mab Sarter was sitting on the bollard at the head of the plank with her feet off the mud and a tally stick across her knees, and she was not counting the plank and she had said so.

"I am not counting the gang," she said. "I am counting a run of fence on the flat behind the timber stack for a man who is going to move a gate, and I have to be on that flat at the fourth hour because a wall in the shade at the fourth hour is the only hour I get a straight sight along it, and I have not told anybody that and nobody has asked, and if the light is different at the far end this afternoon than it was this morning then the number I have is wrong and it will be wrong in a man's book by the evening."

"Then go and be on it," Tomas said.

"I will. I am going to be late because there is a girl at the third bench who wants counting and she will have gone home." She turned the tally stick over. "I am not saying that so somebody will do it. I am saying it because it is a thing that is going to happen."

---

At about the fifth hour Harlan Loxley finished the line, stepped back, wiped the bevel on his apron, and turned round for the first time and looked at the barrow and at the cradle on it, which he had not looked at in the hour since Tomas came up the plank.

"That has not been stood on end since it was made," he said.

"No."

"I can tell because the bedding is pressed into a flat and not into a corner, and a thing that has been stood on end has its bedding pressed into a corner at one end. I have never seen one of those and I have looked at a great many." He looked at the wet plank. "And you were going to set it down on that."

"I was."

"Do not. A cradle is a building's worth of oak and this is a plank over mud and the water comes up under the plank twice in nine days." He went and got a board out of the shed, an old one, and put it on the deck at the head of the plank where the water never reaches, and he did not touch the frame and he did not offer to. "There. Now it is on a board and not on a bridge, and I have not touched it, and if it is still level in a month that is the board's doing and not mine."

Ned Adley watched the board go down and said nothing, and then said, "That is the second time this week somebody has handed that thing a piece of ground before anybody asked."

Tomas set the cradle down on the board and made the four bolts fast where it stood. Nothing in the frame could strike anything. It has not been in a building since the thirty-third, and a shed with an open front and a lofting frame in it is a building, and he had stood at the edge of it for an hour and had not gone in, and he would not go in with the sheet either.

---

Then he took the sheet out.

He did not ask.

He put it down flat on the bench outside the shed's front, on the plank, on the board's other end, and stepped back.

Harlan Loxley came out and stood over it the way a man stands over a thing that is on his table and not on somebody's table, and he did not touch it, and he did not say anything for a while, and what he said was this.

"I can read that."

"You can read a road."

"No." He crouched. "I can read a drawing. I have been reading drawings for nineteen years and I have never in my life been asked to read a road and I am not going to pretend to you that those are the same thing, because they are not. I have made a hull out of a drawing. A drawing is a set of instructions and it is marked, and if it is marked properly a man in another county who has never seen my yard can make the same hull I made, and that is the whole point of a drawing, and it is the whole point of nothing else."

He put his finger on the middle column of figures without touching the paper, the way a man points at a line in the air over a thing he is reading at arm's length.

"This mark here is a length. It is after every figure in this half, and it is the same mark every time, and a mark after a figure is the difference between a figure being a length and a figure being nothing but a figure. My father used to call it the tail on the number and he used to be right to." He moved his finger a foot along the column. "And along here the tails have stopped. Some of them have. Not all of them. The figures are the same size and the ruling is the same and there is a different mark after some of them, and I have never seen that on a drawing in my life, and I do not know what it is for, and I am not going to guess at it in front of you because a guess in a draughtsman is worth about the same as a guess in anybody else and costs more."

Tomas had not moved and had not put his hands in his pockets.

"You have told me the tails change."

"I have told you the tails change about a third of the way along and that the two sorts of tail are not in the same half of the sheet." He crouched a little lower. "And I am telling you the other thing now, which is the thing I have been not telling you for about a minute, and I would rather say it out of my own mouth than have you work it out and ask me whether I had thought of it."

"Go on."

"I did not look at that." He put his finger in the air about a third of the way along the sheet, where a rectangle about two hands together had the paper smooth under it and the rules stopping short on either side of it. "I want you to know that I did not look at it and I want you to know why I did not look at it, because you have been putting this paper on tables for four days and I am the first man you have put it in front of who reads a drawing, and you have been hoping about me since you got off the bank, and I have watched you hoping since about the fourth minute."

"Then you are right and I was hoping."

"I did not look at it because a man who makes a thing from a drawing is used to the drawing stopping." He stood up, and his knees went as he did it. "Every job I have ever had, the drawing stops where I am going to stop. The lines go as far as the hull and no further. The marks go as far as the length of the boat and no further. My whole trade is a man looking at a place where the marks stop and saying, that is the end of it, and that is my problem now and not the paper's. A place where the marks stop is the one place on a drawing I would not look at twice if you asked me to look at it, and you have had it in front of four people in four trades in four days and there are two kinds of man you could have shown it to and I am the one you showed it to."

The chain came up taut behind him and the plank knocked for about a second and went quiet.

"That is the sharpest thing anybody has said to me in a hundred and seventy days," Tomas said, "and I have had a good many sharp things said to me by people who owed me nothing, and I am not thanking you for it, and I am not going to tell you that it was worth the plank, and I am not going to ask you a second question."

"Do not ask me a second question. I have got a loft to finish at dusk and the frame is out in the open and there is weather coming in off that water before dark, and if the lofting frame gets wet I lose the day, and the day is the only thing in that shed I own that a man cannot take off me." He went back inside. "Come and look at the loft if you have nothing to do, because it is on the end of a shed and it is not a secret and I have never once asked a stranger not to look at it. But I am not going to stand here and be talked at, and I have not asked you for anything, and if you have a paper with a third mark on it I hope somebody in this river knows what the third mark is for, because I have been at this for nineteen years and I do not, and that is not modesty, that is a hole."

At about the sixth hour Tomas went and looked at the lofting frame for a while, because he had nothing to do, and it was a hull the length of nine of his barrows built out of battens, and there were eleven of the battens on it and a string line and a number chalked on every one of them, and none of those numbers had a mark after it, because they did not need one.

Mab Sarter had gone to the flat behind the timber stack at the fourth hour and had gone at the fifth and a half instead, and the light on the far end of the run was not the light she had left it in, and she knew that before she got there, and she went anyway, because the number she has at the end of the day is the number she has at the end of the day.

---

At about the seventh hour Tomas walked back up the plank with the sheet in the coat, and four days of hoping had come off him somewhere between the bench and the head of the plank, and what he had in its place was worse and smaller and true. A carter's thumb had found a crease that had not been folded. A weighmaster's hand had found a corner heavier than a crease. A lime seller had said the price is the price of that day. And a draughtsman had looked straight at the place where the marks stopped, and had not seen it, and had told him why, and had been right, and had not done it to be kind.

At about the ninth hour of the evening the barrow was on a flat above the water with the four bolts made fast, and the lofting frame was in the light below him on the head of the plank with a tarpaulin half over it, and he was thirty-four days on this road with her and had not asked her once.

> *Third column, ruled apart. **He did not look at the rectangle, told me he did not look at it, and told me why: a man who makes a thing from a drawing is used to the drawing stopping, because the lines in a hull go as far as the hull and no further, and the marks go as far as the boat, and a man who has been making a thing for nineteen years says the end of it out loud and takes it home with him, and a place where the marks stop is the one place on a drawing he would not look at twice.** That is not anybody hiding anything. **I had been hoping about him since I got off the bank and he watched me hoping from about the fourth minute, and he was right to, and I am not thanking him for it and I am not going to ask him a second question.** The two questions are no narrower today than they were and they are a good deal older.*

> *All five clauses stand. **Nothing rung, by me or by her, and nothing bound, and nothing counted into iron, and no stage, and no perception attempted and none had, and the warrant stands, and a fitting is not called anything it is not.** Four bolts made fast, and **the frame has not been in a building since the thirty-third, a hundred and thirty-seven days, and there is a shed with an open front twenty feet away that I stood at the edge of for an hour and did not go into, and a board on a deck is not a building either.** The carrier in the sack, **not rung, ninety-three days from the seventy-seventh, and I have not put my hand on it.** My own bell is in a sealed yard in Kells and the bar is on the bell and not on the licence, and it does not travel. **Her bell is in her bag and it is not rung and nobody asked her for a reason at any hour today, including me.**

> *The register is thirteen. **I have produced nothing and asked for nothing and been given nothing and taken nothing, and nobody was thanked today, and a man put a board under my cradle and did not touch the frame and I did not thank him for that either.**
