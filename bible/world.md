# World Bible: Mereward and the Bell Network

## Overview

Mereward is a temperate river country of market towns, hill farms, salt flats, monasteries, and flood-cut roads. It is not a magical kingdom in the sense that spells solve everyday problems. Bells matter because they organize work and memory: they open markets, close bridges, announce deaths, warn of floods, mark school hours, call people to court, and preserve the fact that someone once accepted a duty.

The country is ruled from **Sennet**, a river capital built around the old **First Answer** chamber. The Crown presents the bell network as a public safety system. In practice, a series of `Crown clappers` lets one official's response stand for an entire settlement. A person can be recorded as having accepted a civic duty they never freely accepted. The result is a quiet form of rule: the order sounds like a promise, and obedience looks like consent.

The magic is small, physical, and dangerous to misunderstand. It does not know what the future holds. It carries a person's accepted responsibility through metal, stone, and shared memory. The social danger comes from people who are willing to forge that responsibility on another person's behalf.

## Geography

The nine historic wards are circuits of roads and settlements, not magical borders. A ward may contain several villages and may disagree about which bell should speak for it.

### Kells Ward

A highland market town called **Kells** sits where the Kells Water meets the old western road. Its bellhouse, the **Vale Bellhouse**, is one of the surviving independent workshops. Kells is proud of its bells and poor enough that many residents cannot afford the repairs they need. The town has a watch, a market court, a low ward beside the river, and a public cistern.

### Sennet Ward

The capital is built on three terraces above the Sennet River. The Quiet Office, the Crown archives, the river courts, and the sealed **First Answer** chamber are here. Sennet is wealthy, crowded, and dependent on a network that makes distant grain shipments and emergency orders look instantaneous.

### Miremarket Ward

The lower river towns handle grain, timber, clay, and iron. Their warehouses are full of cheap bellmetal and imported wire. Miremarket is the first place where a royal mandate can be turned into a shipping delay, a wage dispute, or a disappearance.

### Orra Pass Ward

A chain of high passes and the **Cloister of Orra**, where old bell records and flood histories are copied by hand. The cloister has no monopoly on truth. It has a strong interest in deciding which memories are worth preserving.

### Salt Road Ward

Eastern salt producers and caravan towns use portable bells to announce price changes, water arrivals, and safe crossings. Their practical signal-ringers are organized into the **Road Hearths**, a loose confederation that the Crown treats as both useful and suspect.

### Blackfen Ward

Wet peat, reed cutting, and isolated homesteads make this the least reliably connected ward. Many people here have never heard a royal bell. The Crown calls that independence a safety problem; locals call it a reason to keep working bells rather than palace bells.

### Tallow Reach Ward

Clay and peat villages share a dense web of small family bells. It is the best surviving example of a local Hearthline circuit and the place where Tomas learns that a network can be maintained without one master craft.

### Grey Weir Ward

The western river mouth and coastal villages use bells to warn of storms and fishing closures. Grey Weir has the strongest oral memory of the Hearthline's original consent rules, though no surviving bell can prove which version is authentic.

### Hushline Ward

The northern line where the network failed during the Hush of Kells. It contains abandoned bellhouses, displaced settlements, and people who refuse any new connection. The Crown claims the line is empty. It is not.

## Historical timeline

- **Reckoning 402 — The Hearthline Pact:** Nine settlements build a shared warning network after the Blackwater Famine. The pact requires local answers, physical redundancy, and the right to withdraw. It creates no permanent central relay.
- **Reckoning 487 — The Sennet conquest:** The Crown takes Mereward and gains control of the roads, archives, and copper supply. The old bells continue to work locally because their keepers have not surrendered their responses.
- **Reckoning 493 — The Quiet Edict:** Sennet legalizes Crown answers. A clerk may answer for a household, a workshop, or a ward in the name of public order. The first Crown clappers are installed.
- **Reckoning 509 — The Hush of Kells:** A northern rebellion and a network failure cause several lines to go silent. The Crown blames bellmakers and removes independent keepers. There is no single publicly accepted account of who ordered the final severing.
- **Reckoning 619 — The Renewal Campaign:** The Crown begins requiring households to renew a civic oath. Refusal removes a person from the legal rolls and can place their children, land, or tools under municipal custody.
- **Reckoning 631 — The present:** Tomas Vale hears a warning in Kells before the **Silent Muster** takes place. The crime is planned, and the warning is a genuine consequence of accepted responsibility rather than prophecy.

## The old and new networks

### Hearthline

The Hearthline is a web of local bells, handbells, water clocks, and hand-copied answer records. Its original principle is simple: a message can travel, but the people who hear it must be able to answer for themselves. Every local node has a maker, at least one keeper, and an independent witness. A public warning needs three different local answers before it can be treated as a route-wide signal.

### Crown clapper

A Crown clapper is a metal fitting that repeats one accepted answer across a physical line. It is valuable because it makes distant control cheap. It is dangerous because it turns a single clerk's choice into a supposed answer from every linked household. A clapper does not create belief; it manufactures the appearance of a free response.

### First Answer

The First Answer is the oldest surviving chamber in the Hearthline network. It was built as a place where several communities could compare answers during a flood, not as a throne. The Crown sealed it, connected it to the royal archive, and made its chamber the relay for all nine wards. The current system can imitate the authority of a shared answer while removing the people who would have disagreed.

## Factions and institutions

### The Crown of Sennet

The Crown maintains roads, courts, flood defenses, and the legal rolls. It is not a single villain: clerks, judges, soldiers, engineers, and ordinary officials all keep parts of the system running. The Crown's public promise is that shared signals prevent another war. Its institutional habit is to treat any refusal as a safety problem.

### The Quiet Office

The Quiet Office is the administrative bureau that records births, deaths, property, public duties, and bell answers. Its clerks are trained to separate a person's stated duty from a household's presumed duty. The distinction is useful in a famine and disastrous in a state that wants obedience to look like a record.

The Office contains sincere public servants who protect people from counterfeit warnings, compromised keepers, and expensive panic. It also contains people who know how to turn a carefully qualified record into a mandate.

### The Bellwrights' Measure

The Measure is the craft guild of bellmakers, metalworkers, tuners, and signal-ringers. It sets standards for safe casting and maintains the road maps. It is divided over ownership: some members want a licensed master bell for every ward; others believe a bell's authority belongs to the people who answer it.

### Hearthkeepers

Hearthkeepers are local people trained to maintain a bell route, collect witness answers, and withdraw a node when the route is unsafe. They are not elected officials by default. Some wards appoint them, some pay them, and some simply decide that the person who knows the bridge deserves to speak for it.

### The Unrung

The Unrung remove bells they believe have become weapons. They range from careful local saboteurs to people who smash every route in the name of freedom. The Unrung are a useful warning against assuming that dismantling a system is the same as repairing it.

### River Cantons

The River Cantons trade along the Mere and want a network that can warn of floods without granting Sennet a tax register. They are practical allies when the Crown threatens trade, and unreliable allies when a network asks them to share authority.

### Cloister of Orra

The cloister preserves old bell inscriptions, flood records, and stories about the Hearthline Pact. Its archivists value memory, but some prefer a beautiful, unified history to a messy record of disagreement. They are not a magical oracle.

## Everyday life

Most people do not think of the network as magic. They think of particular bells:

- A market bell tells a seller when the agreed opening has been accepted.
- A flood bell warns that a keeper has accepted responsibility for inspecting a particular bridge.
- A school bell carries a promise by a teacher to return a confiscated book, not a magical compulsion to attend.
- A funeral bell records a family's acknowledgment that someone has died; it does not speak for the dead.
- A bridge or boundary bell can carry a warning made by a keeper who has personally accepted the risk of being wrong.
- A traveling courier may carry a small handbell that repeats a local signal, but cannot add a sentence the local bell did not carry.

A town can be prosperous with many bells and still be vulnerable. A bell's social power depends on whether people trust the keeper, understand the message, and have a way to refuse. Once those conditions disappear, a signal becomes propaganda.

## Material culture and limits

- Bells are cast from copper, tin, and a small controlled amount of lead. The alloy matters for tone but has no power on its own.
- A bound bell uses an ordinary object as a witness: a bridge pin, a market key, a family ribbon, a piece of a public register. The object is not a soul container.
- Bellwire is braided copper laid in shallow stone channels. A break in the route can silence a ward without destroying the bell.
- Travel is by foot, horse, cart, and river barge. There are no instant messengers outside a functioning route.
- The technology level is late medieval: hand tools, bellows, simple lathes, water clocks, paper ledgers, and lockboxes. There are no firearms, modern electronics, or scientific measurement that can replace judgment.
- Bells can injure people. A close, forced resonance may cause vertigo, nosebleed, confusion, or a memory that feels like a memory. A large network does not become safer merely because the message is benevolent.

## Social limits and consequences

Every bell changes a social situation. A warning can save a bridge and bankrupt a mill. An apology can reopen an inheritance dispute. A royal mandate can keep a convoy moving and remove a child from their family. The story treats consequence as part of the craft, not as an unfortunate side effect to be waved away by a good intention.

The network is therefore never merely light or sound. It is a claim about who has standing, who may answer, and who bears the cost when the answer is wrong.
